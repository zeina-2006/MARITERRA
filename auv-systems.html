01 / Foundations

Understanding AUV Systems

An Autonomous Underwater Vehicle is an uncrewed robotic platform designed to travel underwater using its own onboard energy, computing, sensors, and navigation systems.

What Is an Autonomous Underwater Vehicle?

An Autonomous Underwater Vehicle (AUV) is a self-propelled underwater robot that performs a planned mission without requiring continuous, real-time piloting through a physical cable.

Before deployment, operators define the mission: navigation routes, waypoints, operating depth, speed, sensor configuration, survey patterns, and safety limits. The vehicle receives this mission information and uses its onboard control system to execute the task.

During the mission, the AUV collects information through its sensors and usually stores the recorded data in onboard storage. Depending on its communication equipment, it may exchange limited information with a support vessel through underwater acoustic communication.

When the mission ends, the vehicle normally follows a programmed recovery procedure, which may involve returning to a designated location and surfacing. The collected data can then be downloaded and processed.

Important engineering distinction: Autonomous operation does not mean the vehicle has no human involvement. Humans still plan missions, configure systems, establish safety limits, supervise operations where possible, and analyze the collected information.

Why Are AUVs Important?

  • They can survey large underwater areas systematically.
  • They can operate in environments that are difficult or dangerous for divers.
  • They can collect data at depths beyond the practical reach of many human-operated systems.
  • They reduce the need for continuous direct piloting during a survey.
  • They support repeatable scientific and industrial measurements.

What Makes an AUV Autonomous?

  • An onboard mission computer.
  • Predefined mission plans and navigation objectives.
  • Sensor feedback about vehicle condition and surroundings.
  • Control algorithms that adjust vehicle movement.
  • Safety procedures for abnormal conditions.
  • Stored mission data and recovery logic.
02 / Vehicle Classification

AUV vs ROV vs Underwater Glider

These underwater robotic platforms can share some sensors and scientific objectives, but their operating principles and engineering priorities are different.

Characteristic AUV ROV Underwater Glider
Connection Normally untethered Connected by a tether Normally untethered
Control Onboard autonomous mission logic Remote human piloting Programmed buoyancy and navigation logic
Movement Usually powered propulsion Powered thrusters Buoyancy changes and hydrodynamic lift
Energy Onboard batteries or other configured power systems May receive power through tether or use other architectures Designed for very low energy consumption
Typical tasks Mapping, survey, inspection, data collection Intervention, manipulation, close inspection Long-duration ocean measurements
Communication Limited underwater communication or communication at the surface Continuous communication through tether Often communicates at the surface

AUV and ROV

An ROV is generally operated by a human pilot using a tethered connection. An AUV executes its mission using onboard computing and control logic.

This difference affects communication, power architecture, mission planning, recovery procedures, and the level of direct human control.

AUV and Underwater Glider

Many conventional AUVs use propellers or other propulsion mechanisms to move through the water. Underwater gliders primarily change buoyancy and use hydrodynamic lift from their wings to travel.

Some technical classifications overlap, so a glider can be considered an autonomous underwater platform in a broad sense. Its propulsion principle is what makes it a distinct engineering topic.

03 / Engineering Architecture

Main AUV Components and Subsystems

An AUV is not a single mechanical body. It is an integrated engineering system in which mechanical, electrical, electronic, software, and sensing components must work together under underwater conditions.

01

Hull and Structural Design

The hull is the external structure that protects the vehicle's internal components and provides the required hydrodynamic shape.

Many survey AUVs use streamlined, torpedo-like bodies to reduce drag and support efficient forward movement. However, not every AUV has this shape. Some platforms use modular, multi-body, or specialized configurations.

  • Pressure-resistant housings: protect electronics and batteries from external water pressure.
  • External fairings: provide a smoother hydrodynamic surface.
  • Structural frames: support payloads and internal assemblies.
  • Seals and connectors: help prevent water ingress.
  • Corrosion protection: reduces degradation in seawater environments.

Engineers must consider pressure rating, structural strength, material compatibility, maintenance, accessibility, and the effect of the external shape on hydrodynamic drag.

02

Buoyancy and Trim

Buoyancy determines how the vehicle behaves vertically in water. AUVs are commonly designed to operate close to neutral buoyancy, allowing propulsion and control surfaces to manage their movement efficiently.

Positive buoyancy means the vehicle tends to rise, while negative buoyancy means it tends to sink.

Neutral buoyancy occurs when the upward buoyant force approximately balances the vehicle's weight.

  • Buoyancy material may be used to compensate for vehicle weight.
  • Internal ballast or trim adjustments help control balance.
  • Mass distribution affects pitch and stability.
  • Emergency buoyancy or weight-release mechanisms may support recovery in certain designs.

Trim is important because an incorrectly balanced vehicle may require additional control effort and consume more energy to maintain its intended path.

03

Propulsion and Steering

Propulsion provides the force needed to move the AUV through water. Many conventional survey vehicles use an electric motor connected to a propeller.

  • Electric motor: converts electrical energy into mechanical rotation.
  • Propeller: produces thrust by accelerating water.
  • Control fins: change hydrodynamic forces to control direction and attitude.
  • Motor controller: regulates motor operation and speed.

Other designs may use ducted propellers, multiple thrusters, or specialized propulsion arrangements. The choice depends on mission requirements, maneuverability, efficiency, and vehicle geometry.

Unlike a hovering ROV, many torpedo-shaped survey AUVs are optimized for forward motion rather than stationary close-up intervention.

04

Battery and Energy Storage

Since a conventional AUV cannot rely on a continuous surface power cable, it carries onboard energy storage. Rechargeable battery systems are widely used.

  • Battery modules: store the electrical energy required by the vehicle.
  • Battery management system (BMS): monitors battery conditions and helps protect against unsafe operation.
  • Power distribution: supplies different subsystems with suitable electrical power.
  • Monitoring circuits: track voltage, current, temperature, and other operating parameters.

Energy is consumed by propulsion, computers, navigation equipment, sensors, communication devices, and environmental control systems where fitted.

Mission endurance depends on battery capacity, propulsion demand, speed, payload power consumption, water conditions, and operating strategy.

05

Onboard Computing System

The onboard computer is the central processing unit responsible for executing mission software and coordinating vehicle subsystems.

  • Reads information from navigation and environmental sensors.
  • Processes mission waypoints and operating instructions.
  • Monitors vehicle status and subsystem health.
  • Calculates control commands for propulsion and steering.
  • Records mission information and sensor measurements.
  • Executes programmed responses to selected fault conditions.

Some vehicles use multiple processors or distributed controllers so that navigation, payload management, propulsion, and safety functions can operate as separate but coordinated subsystems.

06

Payload and Scientific Equipment

A payload is the equipment installed to perform the mission's scientific, industrial, or survey task. Payload configuration varies according to the information required.

  • Multibeam sonar: measures seafloor depth across a wide swath.
  • Side-scan sonar: produces acoustic images of objects and seabed features.
  • Sub-bottom profiler: investigates sediment layers beneath the seabed.
  • Camera systems: capture visual information when lighting and visibility permit.
  • CTD instruments: measure conductivity, temperature, and pressure, from which salinity and depth-related information can be derived.
  • Magnetometers: measure magnetic-field variations.

Payload selection affects vehicle mass, energy consumption, available storage, data processing, and mission duration.

04 / Underwater Navigation

How Does an AUV Know Where It Is?

Underwater navigation is one of the most challenging parts of AUV engineering because satellite navigation signals such as GPS do not normally provide a position fix to a deeply submerged vehicle.

The Underwater Positioning Problem

GPS works by receiving radio signals from satellites. These signals do not travel effectively through seawater to a submerged AUV.

An AUV therefore needs alternative navigation methods. These may combine inertial measurements, water-relative or bottom-relative velocity measurements, depth data, and acoustic positioning.

Key principle: GPS can be used when the vehicle is at the surface and has a suitable GPS antenna with a clear satellite view. It is not the normal positioning method while the AUV is submerged.

Inertial Navigation System (INS)

An INS estimates the vehicle's movement and orientation using inertial measurement devices, typically accelerometers and gyroscopes.

  • Gyroscopes measure angular motion.
  • Accelerometers measure specific force along their axes.
  • Navigation algorithms estimate orientation, velocity, and position.
  • Errors can accumulate over time, producing navigation drift.

INS is valuable because it can provide continuous navigation estimates without requiring an external signal, but its accuracy generally improves when supported by other measurements.

Doppler Velocity Log (DVL)

A DVL uses the Doppler effect to estimate the vehicle's velocity relative to the seabed or, in some operating modes, relative to the surrounding water.

  • It transmits acoustic signals.
  • It receives echoes from the seabed or scattering particles.
  • It measures frequency shifts in the returned signals.
  • It calculates velocity estimates along its measurement axes.

When bottom tracking is available, DVL measurements can significantly improve navigation when integrated with INS data.

Bottom lock is not guaranteed at every altitude or in every environment. Water tracking and bottom tracking are different operating modes.

Pressure and Depth Sensors

Pressure sensors measure the surrounding water pressure. Since pressure increases with depth, measurements can be used to estimate the vehicle's depth.

Accurate depth information is important for maintaining a planned operating level, following terrain, collecting measurements at specific depths, and supporting safe vehicle control.

Acoustic Positioning Systems

Acoustic positioning systems use sound signals exchanged between underwater equipment and reference transponders or a support vessel.

  • LBL — Long Baseline: uses an array of acoustic reference transponders whose positions are known.
  • USBL — Ultra-Short Baseline: uses a compact transducer arrangement, often installed on a support vessel, to estimate the direction and range of an underwater target.
  • SBL — Short Baseline: uses separated acoustic transducers on a support platform.

Acoustic positioning can support navigation and tracking, but its performance depends on geometry, sound propagation, equipment calibration, and environmental conditions.

Sensor Fusion: Combining Navigation Measurements

A single navigation sensor rarely provides every piece of information required for reliable underwater positioning.

Sensor fusion combines measurements from multiple sources. For example, an INS may provide continuous motion estimates, while a DVL supplies velocity updates and a pressure sensor provides depth information. Acoustic positioning may provide additional external corrections.

Depending on the system, estimation algorithms such as Kalman filters or related methods can combine these measurements and account for their uncertainties.

Engineering challenge: Navigation systems must manage uncertainty. If external corrections are unavailable for a long period, estimated position error may increase even when the vehicle is operating normally.
05 / Control and Mission Software

Autonomous Control and Mission Execution

The control architecture transforms mission objectives into vehicle actions and continuously checks whether the AUV is operating within its defined limits.

01

Mission Planning

Operators define the mission area, route, waypoints, depth, speed, survey pattern, payload settings, and recovery location.

02

Navigation and Estimation

The vehicle estimates its position, orientation, velocity, and depth using available navigation sensors and estimation algorithms.

03

Guidance

Guidance logic determines the direction and movement needed to follow the planned route or reach the next mission waypoint.

04

Control

Control algorithms calculate commands for motors, propellers, and control surfaces to achieve the required motion.

05

Payload Management

Mission software coordinates sensor activation, measurement timing, data recording, and payload operating modes.

06

Health Monitoring

The onboard system checks selected parameters such as battery condition, leakage indicators, temperature, and navigation status.

How Does an AUV Follow a Survey Route?

A common survey strategy is a lawnmower pattern. The vehicle follows a series of parallel survey lines across a defined area to collect systematic coverage.

The route is divided into waypoints. The guidance system determines how the vehicle should move toward each waypoint, while the control system adjusts propulsion and steering to follow the intended path.

Survey planning must account for vehicle turning radius, speed, depth, seabed terrain, sensor coverage, navigation uncertainty, and safety margins.

06 / Underwater Communication

How Do AUVs Communicate?

Communication underwater is more difficult than communication through air because radio-frequency signals are strongly attenuated in seawater.

Acoustic Modems

Underwater acoustic modems transmit information using sound waves. They can exchange selected commands, status information, and navigation data between an AUV and a support platform.

  • Can support underwater vehicle tracking.
  • May provide mission status and health information.
  • Can support limited mission updates on compatible platforms.
  • Can be affected by noise, multipath propagation, range, and water conditions.

Acoustic communication generally has lower data rates and higher latency than typical radio or wired networks.

Surface Communication

When an AUV reaches the surface, it may use communication technologies that are more suitable for above-water transmission.

  • Wi-Fi: can support local data transfer and configuration.
  • Satellite communication: may support remote status reporting where installed and available.
  • GPS: provides a surface position fix when the antenna has suitable satellite visibility.
  • Radio links: may be used for local communication with nearby support equipment.
An AUV should not be assumed to provide continuous live video or uninterrupted remote piloting. Many survey vehicles store large sensor datasets onboard and transfer them after recovery. Communication capabilities depend on the specific platform.
07 / Operational Workflow

From Mission Planning to Recovery

A successful AUV operation requires more than launching a robot into the sea. It involves preparation, calibration, mission execution, recovery, and data analysis.

PHASE 01

Mission Definition

Define the mission objectives, survey boundaries, required measurements, route, depth, and recovery plan.

PHASE 02

Mission Configuration

Configure mission software, waypoints, sensor settings, safety limits, and onboard data recording.

PHASE 03

Pre-Dive Checks

Inspect the hull, seals, batteries, navigation equipment, payload, communications, and software status.

PHASE 04

Launch and Initialization

Deploy the vehicle, establish its initial position and orientation, and verify that the mission can begin.

PHASE 05

Autonomous Execution

The AUV follows its programmed route, collects sensor measurements, and monitors its operating state.

PHASE 06

Recovery

The vehicle follows its recovery procedure, which may include returning to a designated point and surfacing for retrieval.

PHASE 07

Data Download

Engineers and researchers retrieve stored mission data and check that the required measurements were recorded.

PHASE 08

Data Processing

Raw measurements are processed, corrected, georeferenced where possible, and converted into useful survey products.

PHASE 09

Maintenance and Review

Inspect the vehicle, evaluate system performance, recharge batteries, and prepare for the next mission.

AUVs support scientific research, maritime infrastructure, environmental monitoring, and underwater survey operations.

09 / Real-World Engineering

Case Study: WHOI Sentry AUV

A real deep-ocean autonomous platform demonstrates how multiple engineering disciplines are integrated into one operational system.

Sentry — Deep-Sea Exploration Vehicle

Sentry is an autonomous underwater vehicle operated by the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI). It is designed for deep-sea exploration and can operate at depths reaching 6,000 meters.

Its scientific capabilities include producing bathymetric, side-scan, sub-bottom, and magnetic maps of the seafloor, as well as collecting digital images and other scientific measurements.

Sentry uses a navigation architecture that includes a Doppler Velocity Log and an Inertial Navigation System. Acoustic positioning systems such as USBL or LBL can provide additional navigation support.

Its acoustic communication capability can be used to obtain vehicle state and sensor information and, where supported, to send mission-related instructions while the vehicle is underwater.

This example illustrates an important engineering principle: deep-sea autonomy depends on the coordinated performance of navigation, propulsion, power, payload sensors, software, and recovery systems.

Additional Example: MBARI Seafloor Mapping AUV

The Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI) describes a seafloor-mapping AUV equipped with an inertial navigation system, DVL, pressure sensor, multibeam sonar, side-scan sonar, and sub-bottom profiler.

Its documented operating procedure illustrates how GPS may establish a surface position before descent, while underwater navigation is supported by INS, DVL, and acoustic tracking where required.

This is a useful example of how navigation architecture changes with depth and sensor availability.

10 / Technical Challenges

Engineering Challenges in AUV Operations

Autonomous underwater systems must operate in a physically demanding environment where failures can be difficult to detect, diagnose, and recover from.

Navigation Drift

Inertial navigation errors accumulate over time. Without reliable external corrections, the estimated vehicle position can gradually diverge from its actual position.

Limited Energy

Battery capacity limits operating time and mission range. High propulsion demand, sensor power, and unfavorable operating conditions can reduce endurance.

Communication Limitations

Acoustic communication has limited bandwidth and can be affected by environmental noise, propagation conditions, and distance.

Water Ingress and Pressure

Seal failure, connector problems, or structural weaknesses can cause water ingress or damage at depth. Pressure design and testing are essential.

Sensor Reliability

Incorrect calibration, fouling, sensor degradation, and environmental interference can reduce the quality of collected data.

Recovery and Vehicle Loss

Unexpected currents, navigation errors, equipment failure, or unsuccessful surfacing can complicate recovery and may result in a lost vehicle.

Complex Seafloor Terrain

Slopes, obstacles, rugged seabed features, and uncertain bathymetric information can increase collision risk and make mission planning more difficult.

System Integration

Navigation, control, power, communication, and payload systems must exchange information reliably. Interface failures can affect the entire mission.

Critical operational lesson: AUV autonomy must include defined responses to abnormal conditions. A vehicle that can follow a route but cannot recognize unsafe conditions or execute a recovery strategy is not a robust operational system.
11 / Future Developments

The Future of Autonomous Underwater Vehicles

Research is advancing AUV capability through improved autonomy, navigation, sensing, energy efficiency, and coordinated operations.

AI-Assisted Mission Decisions

Machine learning and intelligent algorithms may help AUVs identify features of interest, classify selected sonar or image data, and adjust mission behavior within approved operational limits.

AI outputs still require validation. An algorithm should not be assumed to make every decision safely or correctly in unfamiliar underwater conditions.

Improved Underwater Navigation

Advances in inertial sensors, acoustic positioning, terrain-aided navigation, and sensor fusion can improve position estimation and mission accuracy.

Multi-AUV Coordination

Multiple vehicles may work together to survey larger areas, distribute tasks, and collect complementary measurements.

This requires coordination strategies, communication planning, collision avoidance, and reliable mission management.

Energy Efficiency

Improvements in battery technology, hydrodynamic design, propulsion efficiency, and mission planning may increase endurance and operational range.

Advanced Sensor Processing

More capable onboard processing can support real-time data quality checks, feature detection, and selective data recording.

Greater Operational Reliability

Better fault detection, health monitoring, fault-tolerant software, and recovery strategies can improve mission resilience.

QUESTION 01

What is the main characteristic that distinguishes an AUV from a conventional ROV?

Correct answer: B. An AUV normally carries its own power and executes mission logic onboard, while a conventional ROV is connected to a surface platform through a tether.
QUESTION 02

Why can a submerged AUV not normally rely on GPS for continuous underwater positioning?

Correct answer: C. GPS relies on satellite radio signals. A submerged vehicle generally uses inertial, Doppler, pressure, and acoustic navigation methods instead.
QUESTION 03

What is the primary function of an Inertial Navigation System (INS)?

Correct answer: A. INS uses inertial sensors, typically gyroscopes and accelerometers, to estimate movement and orientation. Its errors can accumulate over time.
QUESTION 04

How does a DVL help an AUV navigate?

Correct answer: D. A DVL uses acoustic Doppler measurements to estimate velocity relative to the seabed or surrounding water, depending on the operating mode.
QUESTION 05

Which statement correctly describes underwater acoustic communication?

Correct answer: B. Acoustic modems transmit data using sound. Their bandwidth and latency are generally more limited than typical wired or above-water communication technologies.
QUESTION 06

What is the main purpose of a mission-planning system?

Correct answer: C. Mission planning defines where the AUV should travel, what it should measure, how it should operate, and how the mission should end.
QUESTION 07

Which sensor is commonly used to measure surrounding water pressure and estimate vehicle depth?

Correct answer: A. A pressure sensor measures surrounding water pressure, which increases with depth and can be used to estimate the vehicle's depth.
QUESTION 08

What is a major advantage of combining INS and DVL measurements?

Correct answer: D. INS provides continuous inertial estimates, while DVL can provide velocity corrections. Their integration can reduce navigation drift, although it cannot eliminate every source of error.
QUESTION 09

Which statement best describes a typical underwater glider?

Correct answer: B. Underwater gliders change buoyancy and use wings to convert vertical movement into forward motion, allowing energy-efficient long-duration missions.
QUESTION 10

Which of the following is a significant operational challenge for AUVs?

Correct answer: C. AUVs must manage navigation uncertainty, finite energy, communication limitations, environmental conditions, and the possibility of unsuccessful recovery.
