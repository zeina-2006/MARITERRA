01 / INTRODUCTION

The New Cybersecurity Era of Maritime AI

Understanding why security and ethics are essential to the digital transformation of the maritime industry.

The maritime industry is experiencing a major digital transformation. Modern vessels, ports, shipping companies, and offshore facilities increasingly rely on connected technologies to monitor operations, communicate information, and improve efficiency.

Artificial Intelligence introduces new capabilities into this environment. AI can predict equipment failures, analyze navigation information, identify unusual behavior, optimize routes, and support operational decisions.

However, greater connectivity also creates new risks. A vulnerable system, compromised dataset, manipulated AI model, or unauthorized connection may affect more than a computer. Depending on the system involved, an incident could influence business continuity, operational safety, and environmental protection.

04 / THREAT INTELLIGENCE

The Maritime Cyber Threat Landscape

Recognizing common threats that can affect shipping companies, vessels, ports, and maritime service providers.

CYBER THREAT INTELLIGENCE

Protecting Connected Maritime Operations

🦠

Ransomware

Malicious software that can encrypt or disrupt access to systems and information. It may interrupt business operations and complicate recovery.

🎣

Phishing

Deceptive messages designed to manipulate users into revealing credentials, opening malicious files, or taking unauthorized actions.

🔑

Credential Theft

Unauthorized acquisition of passwords, authentication tokens, or other identity information.

🚫

Denial of Service

Attempts to disrupt system availability by overwhelming or interfering with service resources.

🔗

Supply Chain Attacks

Compromise introduced through a trusted supplier, software dependency, service provider, or maintenance connection.

👤

Insider Threats

Risks caused by authorized users who intentionally or unintentionally misuse their access.

Potential Maritime Consequences

  • Loss of access to fleet management systems.
  • Disruption of port and cargo-related digital services.
  • Exposure of sensitive commercial or personal information.
  • Reduced confidence in operational information.
  • Delays in maintenance and technical support.
  • Potential safety consequences when critical systems are affected.
Remember: Cyber incidents do not always require sophisticated techniques. Weak passwords, insecure remote access, outdated software, and human error can create significant exposure.
05 / MARITIME ATTACK SURFACE

Where Can Maritime Cyber Risks Exist?

Understanding the different technologies and interfaces that may introduce cybersecurity exposure.

An attack surface is the collection of points where an unauthorized person or process might attempt to interact with a system. In maritime environments, this can include onboard devices, remote services, user accounts, software, and communication links.

Vessel Systems
Port Infrastructure
Shore-Based Services

Navigation Data Sources

Navigation-related information may depend on sensors, electronic systems, communication links, and external data sources. Its authenticity and reliability matter.

Remote Maintenance

Remote support can improve maintenance efficiency, but poorly controlled remote connections can increase exposure.

Software Dependencies

Applications may depend on third-party libraries, operating systems, firmware, and external services.

Human Interfaces

User accounts, operator workstations, portable media, and administrative tools require appropriate controls.

Important distinction: Identifying an attack surface does not mean that every listed system is vulnerable. Actual exposure must be established through authorized assessment and system-specific evidence.
06 / AI SECURITY

Cyberattacks Against Artificial Intelligence

AI systems create additional security considerations involving datasets, model behavior, training, and deployment.

Traditional cybersecurity protects infrastructure, applications, and information. AI security also considers the datasets, model architecture, training process, inference services, and software components supporting an AI application.

Data Poisoning

Manipulated or misleading examples are introduced into training data, potentially influencing what the model learns.

Adversarial Inputs

Carefully crafted inputs may cause a model to produce incorrect classifications or unexpected predictions.

Model Theft

Unauthorized extraction or replication of model behavior, architecture, or valuable intellectual property.

Model Evasion

Inputs are designed to avoid detection or cause a security model to misclassify suspicious activity.

AI Supply Chain Risk

Threats introduced through compromised pretrained models, datasets, libraries, dependencies, or deployment tools.

Prompt Injection

Malicious instructions embedded in untrusted content may manipulate an AI application that processes text or documents.

Example: Maritime AI Anomaly Detection

Imagine an AI system that analyzes vessel network activity. It learns normal behavior from historical data and identifies unusual patterns for investigation.

If the training data is incomplete, inaccurate, or manipulated, the model may learn misleading patterns. It could miss suspicious activity or generate excessive false alarms.

07 / DATA PROTECTION

Maritime Data Security & Integrity

Protecting the information that AI systems use to learn, predict, and support maritime operations.

Maritime AI systems may process navigation information, engine measurements, maintenance records, cargo data, voyage information, and crew-related information. Different categories require different levels of protection.

Data Classification

Identify whether information is public, internal, confidential, or subject to additional protection requirements.

Encryption

Use appropriate cryptographic protections for information stored on systems and information transmitted between services.

Access Control

Limit access according to verified identity, authorization, role, and operational need.

Data Integrity

Use validation, audit records, controlled updates, and integrity checks to detect unauthorized changes.

Data Retention

Define appropriate retention periods and secure disposal procedures.

Backup & Recovery

Maintain protected backups and test recovery procedures to support continuity after an incident.

AI data quality is also a security concern. Incorrect timestamps, altered sensor readings, duplicated records, missing values, or manipulated labels can affect model outputs even if the model's code has not changed.

10 / OPERATIONAL CONTINUITY

Cyber Resilience & Incident Recovery

Preparing maritime organizations to withstand, respond to, and recover from cyber incidents.

Cybersecurity aims to reduce the likelihood and impact of attacks. Cyber resilience goes further by considering how an organization continues essential operations and recovers when preventive controls are not enough.

🛡️

Prepare

Identify critical assets, define responsibilities, maintain protected backups, and create response plans.

🚨

Respond

Detect, assess, contain, communicate, and coordinate according to approved procedures.

♻️

Restore trusted systems, verify their integrity, and review lessons learned after an incident.

11 / RESPONSIBLE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Ethical Principles in Maritime AI

Ensuring intelligent systems respect people, safety, privacy, and responsible decision-making.

AI ethics examines how intelligent systems affect individuals, organizations, and society. In maritime applications, ethical considerations become important when AI influences crew monitoring, safety decisions, employment, environmental reporting, or access to sensitive information.

Fairness

Evaluate whether model behavior creates unjustified differences across people, groups, or operating contexts.

Transparency

Explain the system's purpose, capabilities, limitations, and appropriate use.

Accountability

Define who is responsible for development, deployment, monitoring, and decisions based on AI outputs.

Privacy

Protect personal and sensitive information through proportionate collection and appropriate safeguards.

Safety

Assess foreseeable harms and ensure AI use does not undermine established protective measures.

Human Oversight

Keep qualified people involved in decisions where professional judgment and responsibility are required.

Ethical challenge: A system may be technically capable of monitoring crew behavior. That does not automatically mean every possible form of monitoring is necessary, fair, proportionate, or justified.
12 / TRUSTWORTHY AI

Explainable AI & Decision Transparency

Understanding why an AI system produced a particular result and how much confidence should be placed in it.

Some advanced machine learning models are difficult to interpret. In safety-related maritime applications, users need more than a prediction. They need context about the information used, uncertainty, and model limitations.

Feature Importance

Identify which input variables contributed most to a prediction under a selected explanation method.

Local Explanations

Explain factors associated with an individual prediction rather than describing only overall model behavior.

Confidence & Uncertainty

Communicate when evidence is incomplete or a prediction may be unreliable.

Auditability

Maintain records of model versions, data sources, decisions, and relevant changes.

Example: Explaining a Cybersecurity Alert

Instead of displaying only "HIGH RISK", an AI security system could provide supporting observations:

  • An unusual login time compared with the account's established pattern.
  • An access attempt from an unfamiliar network location.
  • Multiple failed authentication attempts within a short period.
  • A connection pattern that differs from the system baseline.

These observations are evidence for investigation, not proof that an attack has occurred. Explainability should help people question and verify AI outputs rather than simply persuade them to trust the model.

Important limitation: An explanation is not necessarily a complete description of a model's internal reasoning. Explanation methods can be approximate and should be evaluated for reliability.
13 / PRIVACY & GOVERNANCE

Privacy, Data Governance & Responsible Deployment

Managing the information lifecycle of maritime AI systems.

Maritime AI projects may process personal information about crew members, contractors, passengers, or other individuals. They may also use commercially sensitive information about vessels, voyages, customers, and operations.

Purpose Limitation

Collect and use data for clearly defined and legitimate purposes.

Data Minimization

Avoid collecting more personal or sensitive information than necessary for the intended task.

Retention Policies

Define appropriate retention periods and secure disposal procedures.

Access Governance

Document who can access information and under which conditions.

For example, a crew safety monitoring system should have a clearly documented purpose, appropriate access restrictions, proportionate data collection, and transparent procedures.

14 / HUMAN FACTORS

Human Oversight & Professional Responsibility

Keeping qualified people responsible for decisions that affect maritime operations.

AI can support analysis and recommendations, but maritime operations involve legal responsibilities, professional standards, and safety obligations. These cannot be transferred to an algorithm simply because it generates a prediction.

Human-in-the-Loop

A person reviews and approves relevant AI-supported decisions before action is taken.

Human-on-the-Loop

A person supervises an automated process and can intervene under defined conditions.

Human Authority

Operational authority and professional responsibilities remain clearly assigned.

Training & Competence

Users understand the system's purpose, limitations, warnings, and appropriate fallback procedures.

Human factors matter: Too many alerts, unclear explanations, poor interface design, and overconfidence in automation can reduce the effectiveness of an otherwise capable AI system.
15 / INTERNATIONAL FRAMEWORKS

Global Cybersecurity & AI Governance Standards

Recognized frameworks help organizations structure security management and responsible AI deployment.

IMO Maritime Cyber Risk Guidance

IMO guidance addresses maritime cyber risk management as part of the safety and security management of ships and shipping operations.

IACS UR E26 & E27

IACS unified requirements address cyber resilience of ships and onboard systems within their applicable scope and implementation arrangements.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework

Provides a structured approach to understanding, assessing, prioritizing, and managing cybersecurity risk.

NIST AI Risk Management Framework

Supports trustworthy AI through governance, mapping, measurement, and risk management.

ISO/IEC 27001

Specifies requirements for an information security management system.

ISO/IEC 42001

Specifies requirements and guidance for an AI management system within an organization.

Scope matters: Standards and regulations have different purposes, applicability, and implementation dates. A framework is not automatically a legal requirement for every ship or AI application. Always verify the applicable edition and requirements.
16 / THE NEXT MARITIME ERA

The Future of Cybersecurity & Ethical Maritime AI

Preparing for more autonomous, connected, and data-driven maritime operations.

THE NEXT GENERATION

Building a Safer Digital Maritime Future

Autonomous Maritime Systems

Greater automation will increase the importance of secure control architectures, remote supervision, communication resilience, and clearly defined human authority.

AI-Generated Cyber Threats

AI tools may help attackers create convincing deceptive content, automate reconnaissance, or adapt malicious activity.

Post-Quantum Cryptography

Organizations will need to assess future cryptographic risks and migration requirements as quantum technologies develop.

Federated Learning

Collaborative model training may allow organizations to learn from distributed data without centralizing all raw records.

Digital Twins & Connected Fleets

Connected digital models will increase the importance of data integrity, identity management, and secure interfaces.

Trustworthy AI Assurance

More structured testing, assurance, documentation, and governance may become important for safety-related AI deployments.

What Responsible Maritime AI Should Aim For

  • Secure system design from the beginning of development.
  • Reliable and traceable data sources.
  • Continuous monitoring of model performance and drift.
  • Transparent limitations and uncertainty communication.
  • Human oversight appropriate to operational risk.
  • Tested incident response and recovery arrangements.
  • Compliance with applicable maritime and information security requirements.
Final perspective: The future of maritime AI will not be determined only by how intelligent models become. It will also depend on how securely, fairly, transparently, and responsibly they are designed and operated.
