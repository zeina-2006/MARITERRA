AUTONOMOUS SHIPS / TOPIC 02

Levels of Maritime Autonomy

Explore how maritime vessels perform their functions through onboard automation, remote control, and autonomous decision-making. Understand the four degrees of autonomy identified by the International Maritime Organization for its regulatory scoping exercise.

01 / Introduction

Understanding Maritime Autonomy Levels

Maritime autonomy describes the extent to which a vessel can perform operational functions independently of direct human interaction. These functions may include navigation, monitoring, machinery control, route planning, and responses to changing operational conditions.

Not every ship uses the same level of autonomy. Some vessels rely mainly on onboard seafarers while using automated equipment. Others may be controlled remotely, and more advanced systems may make decisions and perform selected actions independently.

To examine how existing international maritime regulations apply to autonomous vessels, the International Maritime Organization (IMO) identified four degrees of autonomy for its Regulatory Scoping Exercise (RSE).

Key learning point: The four degrees describe different arrangements for human involvement, remote control, and autonomous decision-making. They should not be interpreted as a universal technical certification scale or as four mandatory stages that every ship must pass through.

02 / The IMO Framework

The Four Degrees of Autonomy

The IMO framework distinguishes between four operational arrangements. The main differences concern whether seafarers are onboard, where control is exercised, and whether the ship's operating system can make decisions independently.

DEGREE ONE
01 / ONBOARD CREW

Automated Processes and Decision Support

Seafarers remain onboard and operate and control the ship's systems and functions. Some operations may be automated and may sometimes run without continuous supervision, while crew members remain available to take control when necessary.

Human role: The crew performs the main operational role and supervises automated functions.

DEGREE TWO
02 / REMOTE CONTROL + CREW

Remotely Controlled Ship with Seafarers Onboard

The vessel is controlled and operated from another location. However, seafarers remain onboard and are available to take control and operate the shipboard systems and functions.

Human role: Remote operators control the ship, while onboard seafarers can intervene and take control.

DEGREE THREE
03 / REMOTE CONTROL WITHOUT CREW

Remotely Controlled Ship without Seafarers Onboard

The vessel is controlled and operated from another location, but no seafarers are present onboard. Remote operators are responsible for performing the relevant control functions from outside the ship.

Human role: Control is exercised remotely, without onboard seafarers available to take over.

DEGREE FOUR
04 / INDEPENDENT DECISION-MAKING

Fully Autonomous Ship

The ship's operating system is able to make decisions and determine actions by itself. The system performs its defined functions autonomously within its designed capabilities and operating conditions.

Human role: The system performs autonomous decision-making. Human oversight and responsibility still require appropriate arrangements.

Important: "Fully autonomous" does not mean that the ship has no legal responsibility, no safety requirements, or no human accountability. Autonomous decision-making must operate within a defined safety and regulatory framework.

03 / Degree One

Degree One: Automated Processes and Decision Support

Degree One describes a vessel where seafarers remain onboard and continue to operate and control shipboard systems. Technology supports their work by automating selected processes and providing information that helps them make operational decisions.

How It Works

The ship's systems collect information from navigation equipment, sensors, machinery, and monitoring devices. Automated functions process this information and may perform specific tasks according to programmed logic. The crew remains responsible for the overall operation and can take control when required.

Typical Technologies

  • Autopilot and heading-control systems.
  • Automatic engine monitoring.
  • Alarm and monitoring systems.
  • Electronic Chart Display and Information System (ECDIS).
  • Decision-support tools for navigation.

Human Responsibilities

  • Maintain navigational watch.
  • Monitor automated equipment.
  • Verify important system information.
  • Respond to alarms and abnormal conditions.
  • Take manual control when necessary.

Operational Example

A conventional cargo ship uses an autopilot to maintain a selected heading. The system makes steering adjustments automatically, but the officer on watch monitors the vessel's position, traffic, weather, and navigation conditions and remains ready to intervene.

Key characteristic: Automation supports the crew; it does not replace the crew's operational role.

04 / Degree Two

Degree Two: Remote Control with Seafarers Onboard

In Degree Two, the vessel is controlled and operated from another location. Unlike Degree Three, seafarers remain onboard and are available to take control of the ship's systems and functions.

How It Works

A remote operator uses communication links and remote control interfaces to monitor and control selected vessel functions. Data from onboard sensors and navigation systems is transmitted to the remote operating location.

The onboard crew provides an additional operational capability. If remote control becomes unavailable or an abnormal situation occurs, the crew may take over according to the vessel's operating procedures.

Typical Technologies

  • Remote-control interfaces.
  • Ship-to-shore communication systems.
  • Remote monitoring dashboards.
  • Navigation data transmission.
  • Onboard backup control stations.

Human Responsibilities

  • Remote operators control designated functions.
  • Onboard crew remains available for intervention.
  • Clear transfer-of-control procedures are required.
  • Both teams need reliable communication.
  • Emergency responsibilities must be defined.

Operational Example

A vessel is monitored and controlled from a shore-based operations centre during a specific voyage. The ship still carries seafarers who can take over if the remote control link fails or if an onboard intervention becomes necessary.

Key characteristic: Remote control is introduced, but onboard seafarers remain available as an operational backup.

05 / Degree Three

Degree Three: Remote Control without Seafarers Onboard

Degree Three describes a vessel that is controlled and operated from another location without seafarers onboard. Remote operators perform the relevant control functions from a shore-based or other authorized operating location.

How It Works

The vessel relies on onboard equipment to collect and transmit operational data. Remote operators receive information about navigation, machinery, alarms, and vessel status through communication systems and use remote interfaces to control the ship.

Because there are no seafarers onboard, the vessel requires carefully designed procedures for equipment failure, communication loss, emergencies, and situations that would normally require direct onboard intervention.

Typical Technologies

  • Remote Operations Centre infrastructure.
  • Redundant communication links.
  • Remote propulsion and steering control.
  • Automated machinery monitoring.
  • Remote alarms and fault diagnostics.

Major Operational Requirements

  • Reliable ship-to-shore connectivity.
  • Defined emergency-response procedures.
  • Safe behavior during communication failure.
  • Remote monitoring and fault detection.
  • Clear responsibility for remote operators.

Operational Example

An uncrewed surface vessel conducts a planned survey mission. Its navigation and operational status are monitored from a remote centre, where operators can send commands and respond to alerts.

Important challenge: Removing onboard seafarers also removes immediate human intervention at the location of an emergency. Fire, flooding, equipment damage, and rescue situations therefore require dedicated solutions.

06 / Degree Four

Degree Four: Fully Autonomous Ship

Degree Four describes a vessel whose operating system can make decisions and determine actions by itself. The system evaluates information from the ship and its surroundings and performs defined functions without requiring a human command for every individual action.

How It Works

The ship uses sensors, data-processing technologies, decision-making software, and control systems to understand its operating situation and perform authorized actions.

For example, an autonomous navigation system may detect nearby traffic, estimate the movement of surrounding vessels, assess a developing encounter, and select a suitable maneuver within its approved operating conditions.

Typical Technologies

  • Advanced sensor-fusion systems.
  • Artificial intelligence and decision-support software.
  • Autonomous navigation and control.
  • Automated fault detection.
  • Independent operational response functions.

Critical Requirements

  • Reliable perception and decision-making.
  • Safe responses to unexpected situations.
  • Defined operating limits.
  • Robust cybersecurity protection.
  • Clear human accountability and oversight arrangements.

Operational Example

A vessel operating within a defined and approved environment uses autonomous navigation functions to follow a planned route, monitor surrounding traffic, and perform selected navigation actions without continuous human commands.

Key characteristic: The operating system can make decisions and determine actions by itself within its designed capabilities. This does not remove the need for safety requirements or human accountability.

Comparing the Four Degrees

08 / Human Element

The Role of Humans Across Autonomy Levels

Maritime autonomy changes how people interact with ships, but it does not make human expertise irrelevant. The location and nature of human involvement may change from direct onboard operation to remote supervision and oversight.

Onboard Seafarers

In Degree One and Degree Two, onboard seafarers remain available to operate systems, monitor conditions, and intervene when necessary.

Remote Operators

Remote operators monitor vessel information and may control designated functions from an authorized operating centre.

Masters and Operational Responsibility

The master's responsibilities and the relationship between onboard and remote personnel must be clearly established under the applicable regulatory and operational framework.

Engineers and Technical Specialists

Engineering teams maintain, test, monitor, and troubleshoot propulsion, electrical, communication, automation, and control systems.

The human element includes training, workload, situational awareness, decision-making, fatigue, communication, and the ability to intervene effectively during abnormal conditions.

09 / Operational Flexibility

Can One Ship Operate at More Than One Degree?

Yes. The IMO's original framework noted that a MASS could operate at one or more degrees of autonomy during a single voyage.

This means the operational arrangement may change depending on the vessel's location, task, operating conditions, and available systems.

Illustrative Voyage Scenario

01

Departure from Port

The vessel uses onboard personnel and automated navigation support while operating in a complex port environment. This resembles a Degree One arrangement.

02

Remote Supervision at Sea

During a suitable voyage segment, selected vessel functions may be controlled remotely while seafarers remain onboard. This resembles Degree Two.

03

Uncrewed Remote Operation

A vessel specifically designed and approved for uncrewed remote operation may perform a defined mission without seafarers onboard. This resembles Degree Three.

04

Autonomous Functions

Within a suitable operating environment, the vessel's operating system may make decisions and determine actions independently. This resembles Degree Four.

Scenario limitation: This is an illustrative teaching example, not a claim that every vessel can freely switch between all four degrees. Any change in operating mode must be supported by the vessel's design, approvals, procedures, and applicable regulations.

10 / Safety and Regulation

Regulatory Context: From the RSE to the MASS Code

The IMO Regulatory Scoping Exercise examined how existing international maritime instruments apply to ships operating with different degrees of autonomy. It helped identify provisions that could apply, create barriers, require clarification, or need further regulatory consideration.

The four degrees described in this topic belong to that regulatory scoping framework. They are useful for understanding the differences in crew presence, remote control, and autonomous decision-making.

In May 2026, the IMO adopted the non-mandatory International Code of Safety for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS Code). It entered into effect on 1 July 2026.

The MASS Code establishes a goal-based safety framework for covered cargo ships using remote control or autonomous functions. It addresses matters such as design, operation, risk assessment, connectivity, remote operations, and human oversight.

The Code also requires a description of the different modes of operation for which a MASS is designed. This functional approach is important because a ship may use different arrangements for different functions or operational situations.

Regulatory distinction: Do not assume that the four RSE degrees are identical to every operational mode or approval category under the 2026 MASS Code. The Code and its applicable requirements must be read directly when assessing a particular vessel.

11 / Key Takeaways

What You Should Remember

  • The IMO identified four degrees of autonomy for its Regulatory Scoping Exercise.
  • Degree One involves onboard seafarers and automated processes or decision support.
  • Degree Two involves remote control while seafarers remain onboard.
  • Degree Three involves remote control without seafarers onboard.
  • Degree Four describes a ship whose operating system can make decisions and determine actions by itself.
  • A vessel may operate at more than one degree during a voyage, depending on its operational arrangements.
  • Remote control and autonomous decision-making are different concepts.
  • Safety, cybersecurity, human responsibility, and regulatory compliance remain essential.
