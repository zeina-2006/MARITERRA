01 / INTRODUCTION

Understanding Autonomous Ships

Autonomous ships are vessels that use sensors, software, artificial intelligence, communication technologies, and automated control systems to perform some or all operational tasks with reduced direct human intervention.

Their capabilities may include collecting information about the surrounding environment, identifying other vessels, monitoring machinery, supporting navigation decisions, and controlling propulsion or steering systems.

Autonomy does not necessarily mean that a ship has no crew. Different systems operate at different levels of automation, and some autonomous operations still require continuous human supervision or remote intervention.

🧠

Artificial Intelligence

Processes sensor information, identifies patterns, and supports operational decisions.

📡

Advanced Sensors

Collect information about surrounding vessels, obstacles, weather, and machinery.

⚙️

Automated Control

Executes authorized commands through propulsion, steering, and onboard control systems.

🌐

Remote Operations

Connects vessels with shore-based control centers and monitoring infrastructure.

Engineering distinction: An automated ship follows predefined procedures, while an autonomous system can interpret changing information and select actions within its designed operational boundaries. Real-world autonomy may combine both.

02 / AUTONOMY FRAMEWORK

Levels of Maritime Autonomy

Maritime autonomy is not a single technology. It represents a spectrum of operational arrangements, ranging from conventional ships with automated functions to vessels capable of operating without people onboard.

1️⃣

Automated Functions

Systems perform specific tasks automatically while the crew remains responsible for overall operation.

2️⃣

Remote-Controlled Operations

Human operators control vessel functions from another location, with or without onboard crew.

3️⃣

Remote Supervision

The vessel performs certain operations autonomously while remote personnel monitor its behavior and intervene when necessary.

4️⃣

Fully Autonomous Operation

The vessel makes and executes operational decisions independently within its approved operational design domain.

The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has used four degrees of autonomy in its regulatory scoping work for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS). These descriptions are useful for understanding operational differences, but they should not be treated as a universal certification scale for every commercial product.

A vessel may have highly automated navigation but still depend on human operators for maintenance, emergency response, legal responsibility, or decisions outside its approved operating limits.

03 / SYSTEM DESIGN

Autonomous Ship System Architecture

An autonomous vessel requires multiple engineering subsystems to operate together. A failure in one component can affect the reliability of the entire operational chain.

01. Sensor Layer

Radar, AIS, cameras, GNSS, inertial sensors, lidar where suitable, and machinery sensors collect raw information.

02. Data Processing Layer

Filters, synchronizes, validates, and combines data from different sensor sources.

03. Perception Layer

Identifies vessels, obstacles, navigational features, and relevant environmental conditions.

04. Decision Layer

Evaluates possible actions using operational rules, predictions, and safety constraints.

05. Control Layer

Translates approved decisions into steering, propulsion, and other equipment commands.

06. Monitoring Layer

Checks system performance, detects faults, records events, and supports emergency procedures.

How the architecture works together

  1. Sensors collect observations from the vessel and its environment.
  2. Processing systems check data quality and combine relevant information.
  3. Perception algorithms construct an operational picture of the surroundings.
  4. Decision systems evaluate possible actions and applicable constraints.
  5. Control systems execute authorized commands and monitor the response.
  6. Feedback returns new measurements to the system for continuous assessment.
04 / ENVIRONMENTAL AWARENESS

Perception & Sensor Technologies

An autonomous vessel must understand what is happening around it. This capability is called environmental perception. It depends on collecting and interpreting information from several complementary sensor systems.

📡

Marine Radar

Detects surrounding targets and provides range and bearing information. Performance can be affected by sea clutter, rain, and target characteristics.

📍

AIS

Provides identity and navigational information transmitted by participating vessels. It does not detect every object and its information may be incomplete or inaccurate.

📷

Computer Vision

Uses cameras and image-processing models to recognize vessels, navigation marks, and visible obstacles.

🛰️

GNSS

Provides positioning and timing information, subject to interference, obstruction, and other limitations.

🧭

Gyroscopes & IMUs

Measure heading, angular motion, and acceleration to support navigation and motion estimation.

🌊

Environmental Sensors

Measure conditions such as wind, temperature, pressure, and other operational parameters.

Sensor Fusion

Sensor fusion combines measurements from multiple sources to create a more complete estimate of the surrounding environment. For example, radar may provide target range while AIS provides reported vessel identity and GNSS provides own-ship position.

Fusion algorithms must account for different update rates, measurement uncertainty, sensor calibration, coordinate systems, and communication delays.

Important: More sensors do not automatically produce better awareness. Poor calibration, correlated errors, incorrect timestamps, or faulty data association can make a fused estimate less reliable.

05 / INTELLIGENT DECISIONS

AI-Based Decision-Making

Decision-making systems transform information about the vessel and its environment into operational choices. AI can support this process by recognizing patterns, estimating future states, and comparing possible actions.

Major AI approaches

Rule-Based Systems

Apply predefined rules, conditions, and engineering constraints. They are useful when requirements can be expressed explicitly.

Supervised Learning

Uses labeled historical data to learn relationships between inputs and expected outputs, such as equipment states or object classifications.

Reinforcement Learning

Studies action-selection strategies through interaction with an environment. Maritime deployment requires rigorous safety constraints and validation.

Predictive Models

Estimate future conditions such as machinery degradation, energy demand, or environmental changes.

Decision-making cycle

  1. Observe the current operational state.
  2. Estimate the reliability and uncertainty of available information.
  3. Identify relevant objectives, rules, and constraints.
  4. Generate candidate actions.
  5. Evaluate candidate actions against safety and performance criteria.
  6. Request authorization or execute actions according to the approved system design.
  7. Monitor the results and reassess when conditions change.

AI recommendations must not bypass applicable navigational regulations, safety barriers, or the responsibilities of authorized personnel.

06 / MARINE ENGINEERING

Autonomous Control Systems

Control systems convert desired vessel behavior into commands for physical equipment. They are a critical connection between software intelligence and real marine engineering hardware.

Key control components

⚓

Steering Control

Controls rudder or other steering mechanisms to influence vessel heading and maneuvering behavior.

🔧

Propulsion Control

Regulates propulsion commands, engine output, and related operating parameters within equipment limits.

📈

Feedback Control

Compares measured behavior with desired behavior and adjusts commands to reduce the difference.

🛡️

Safety Interlocks

Prevent or restrict unsafe commands when defined conditions, equipment limits, or fault states are detected.

PID Control

A Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controller is a widely used feedback control method. It calculates a control response based on the current error, accumulated error, and rate of change of the error.

PID control is not artificial intelligence by itself. It is a classical control technique that can operate alongside AI-based estimation, prediction, and supervisory systems.

Control system engineering considerations

  • Actuator response time and physical limitations.
  • Vessel inertia, turning behavior, and environmental forces.
  • Control stability and robustness under changing conditions.
  • Sensor noise, delays, and communication interruptions.
  • Fail-safe behavior and manual override availability.
07 / AUTONOMOUS OPERATIONS

Autonomous Navigation

Autonomous navigation involves estimating the vessel's position, understanding the surrounding environment, evaluating navigational constraints, and supporting safe movement toward an intended destination.

Core navigation capabilities

Localization

Estimates the vessel's position and orientation using navigation sensors and available reference information.

Environmental Mapping

Builds a representation of relevant objects, navigational features, and operational boundaries.

Path Following

Supports movement along an approved path while accounting for vessel dynamics and control limits.

Operational Monitoring

Detects deviations, changes in conditions, and situations that require reassessment or intervention.

Autonomous navigation must consider chart quality, depth restrictions, traffic, weather, visibility, maneuverability, navigational rules, and the vessel's approved operational domain.

Navigation autonomy is not simply the ability to follow GPS coordinates. A vessel must continuously assess whether its intended behavior remains safe and permissible under changing conditions.

08 / CONNECTED SHIPPING

Communication & Remote Control Centers

Some autonomous and remotely operated vessels depend on communication links between the ship and shore-based control centers. These links may carry telemetry, alerts, video, operational data, and authorized commands.

Satellite Communication

Supports connectivity over long distances, including operations far from coastal infrastructure.

Coastal Networks

Provide communication coverage in suitable coastal and port environments.

Remote Operations Center

Allows trained personnel to monitor vessel status, receive alerts, and perform authorized remote tasks.

Cybersecurity

Protects communication channels, operational systems, identities, and control access.

What happens when communication is lost?

Autonomous vessel designs need predefined responses for communication failures. Depending on the vessel's design and operating conditions, the system may enter a safe operating mode, maintain a restricted state, request alternative communication, or follow an approved contingency procedure.

The appropriate response must be established through engineering risk assessment and operational approval. There is no single universal behavior suitable for every vessel and situation.

10 / HUMAN FACTORS

Human Supervision & Remote Operations

Autonomous shipping changes the role of maritime professionals. Instead of performing every task directly onboard, personnel may supervise systems, interpret alerts, verify decisions, manage exceptions, and coordinate emergency responses.

Responsibilities in a human-supervised system

  1. Monitor vessel status and operational boundaries.
  2. Review important alerts and system explanations.
  3. Intervene when automation reaches its limits.
  4. Coordinate emergency procedures and communication.
  5. Maintain operational records and review system performance.

Human-machine interface design is essential. Operators need clear information, understandable alerts, appropriate workload, and sufficient time to respond.

A poorly designed autonomous system can create automation complacency: operators may trust the system excessively or lose awareness of its current limitations. Human supervision must be designed, trained, and tested—not merely assumed.

11 / SAFETY & REGULATIONS

Safety, Regulations & Operational Responsibility

Autonomous vessel systems operate in a complex regulatory environment. Technology alone cannot establish that a vessel is safe, compliant, or suitable for a particular operation.

Important regulatory considerations

COLREGs

The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea provide rules governing vessel conduct and collision avoidance.

SOLAS

The International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea establishes important safety requirements for applicable ships.

IMO MASS Work

IMO has examined regulatory implications associated with Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships.

Classification & Assurance

Technical assurance, risk assessment, verification, and applicable classification requirements are important to system approval.

Safety engineering principles

  1. Define the intended operational design domain.
  2. Identify hazards and possible failure scenarios.
  3. Establish safety constraints and fallback procedures.
  4. Verify software, sensors, control systems, and interfaces.
  5. Validate performance under realistic operating conditions.
  6. Maintain monitoring, incident reporting, and improvement processes.

Autonomous systems do not automatically transfer or eliminate legal responsibilities. Responsibility depends on applicable law, the vessel's operation, and the roles of the parties involved.

12 / ENGINEERING WORKFLOW

Developing an Autonomous Vessel System

Building an autonomous ship is a multidisciplinary engineering project involving marine engineering, control theory, software, artificial intelligence, communications, cybersecurity, and safety.

  1. Mission definition: Identify the vessel type, operational purpose, and intended autonomy capabilities.
  2. Requirements engineering: Define performance, safety, environmental, and regulatory requirements.
  3. System design: Select sensors, processors, communication equipment, and control interfaces.
  4. Data engineering: Collect, label, validate, and manage representative operational datasets.
  5. Algorithm development: Build perception, prediction, decision-support, and control components.
  6. Simulation: Evaluate system behavior across normal, unusual, and hazardous scenarios.
  7. Hardware-in-the-loop testing: Test software with representative hardware interfaces.
  8. Sea trials: Conduct controlled tests under an approved safety and operational framework.
  9. Continuous assurance: Monitor performance, investigate failures, and update systems under controlled change management.

Why simulation matters

Simulation allows engineers to test situations that may be dangerous, expensive, or difficult to reproduce at sea. However, simulated success does not prove real-world safety. Simulation models must be validated against physical behavior, and results must be supplemented by other forms of testing.

13 / ENGINEERING LIMITATIONS

Challenges & Technical Limitations

Environmental Uncertainty

Fog, waves, rain, glare, and changing sea conditions can reduce sensor performance.

Sensor Reliability

Hardware failures, calibration problems, and inconsistent measurements can affect perception.

Cybersecurity Threats

Unauthorized access, malware, spoofing, and communication attacks can threaten vessel operations.

AI Generalization

Models trained in one environment may perform poorly in unfamiliar conditions or unusual scenarios.

Communication Limitations

Latency, bandwidth restrictions, and network interruptions can affect remote operations.

Human Factors

Operator workload, unclear alerts, trust, training, and intervention time affect overall safety.

Regulatory Complexity

Different operating areas and legal frameworks create complex approval and compliance requirements.

Cost & Infrastructure

Advanced sensors, computing systems, communications, maintenance, and testing require investment.

The most important engineering question is not whether a vessel can operate autonomously under ideal conditions. It is whether the system can recognize when it is no longer capable of operating safely and respond appropriately.

14 / THE NEXT GENERATION

The Future of Autonomous Shipping

The future of autonomous shipping will likely involve increasingly connected vessels, advanced onboard intelligence, improved operational assurance, and closer integration between ships, ports, and maritime infrastructure.

🧬

Digital Twins

Virtual representations of vessels and equipment can support monitoring, simulation, and maintenance planning.

🤖

Advanced AI Agents

More capable systems may support complex operational workflows while remaining subject to safety controls.

🌐

Cooperative Shipping

Connected maritime infrastructure may improve information exchange between vessels and shore facilities.

⚡

Edge Computing

Onboard processing can reduce dependence on continuous cloud connectivity for time-sensitive tasks.

🔐

Trustworthy AI

Greater emphasis on explainability, robustness, cybersecurity, testing, and operational accountability.

🌱

Efficient Operations

Intelligent monitoring and optimization may contribute to improved energy and resource management.

Progress will depend not only on algorithmic performance but also on regulation, infrastructure, safety evidence, public confidence, and the ability to integrate autonomous technologies into existing maritime operations.
