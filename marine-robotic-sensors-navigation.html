01 / Foundations

Understanding Marine Robotic Sensors & Navigation

Marine robotic sensors collect information about the vehicle, the water, and the surrounding environment. Navigation systems use selected measurements to estimate position, orientation, and movement.

Why Are Sensors Essential?

An underwater robot cannot operate effectively using mechanical components alone. It needs information about depth, orientation, movement, surrounding objects, water conditions, and system health.

Sensors convert physical quantities into measurable signals. These signals are processed by onboard computers or external systems to support navigation, control, scientific measurements, and operational decisions.

Depending on the mission, a vehicle may carry sonar systems, cameras, pressure sensors, inertial measurement units, Doppler velocity logs, environmental instruments, and acoustic positioning equipment.

FUNCTION 01

Environmental Perception

Detect objects, terrain, obstacles, seafloor features, and nearby structures using acoustic and optical instruments.

FUNCTION 02

Vehicle State Estimation

Measure or estimate depth, orientation, angular motion, acceleration, and velocity to understand the vehicle's current state.

FUNCTION 03

Scientific Data Collection

Measure temperature, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, currents, and other environmental properties.

FUNCTION 04

Position and Navigation

Combine inertial, acoustic, velocity, depth, and other measurements to estimate where the vehicle is and how it is moving.

Important distinction: A sensor measurement is not automatically a complete navigation solution. A pressure sensor can estimate depth, but it cannot independently determine the vehicle's full geographic position.

03 / Acoustic Sensing

Sonar Technologies

Sonar is one of the most important sensing technologies in underwater robotics because sound can travel much farther through water than visible light or conventional radio signals.

What Is Sonar?

SONAR stands for Sound Navigation and Ranging. It uses sound waves to detect objects, measure distances, and investigate underwater environments.

An active sonar system transmits an acoustic pulse and receives the returning echo. The time between transmission and reception helps estimate the distance to a reflecting object when the sound speed is known.

A passive sonar system listens for sounds generated by other sources without transmitting its own acoustic pulse.

SONAR TYPE 01

Active Sonar

An active sonar transmits sound into the water and analyzes the returning echo.

  • Object detection.
  • Distance estimation.
  • Seafloor mapping.
  • Obstacle detection.
SONAR TYPE 02

Passive Sonar

A passive sonar system receives underwater sound without transmitting its own interrogation pulse.

  • Acoustic monitoring.
  • Marine mammal observation.
  • Ambient noise studies.
  • Listening for machinery and vessels.

How Does Active Sonar Estimate Distance?

A simplified range calculation is:

Range = (Sound Speed × Echo Travel Time) / 2

The division by two is necessary because the measured time includes both the outgoing journey and the returning echo.

Actual sonar processing may require corrections for sound-speed variation, transducer geometry, vehicle motion, refraction, and signal-processing effects.

Sound Speed in Seawater

The speed of sound in seawater depends on environmental conditions, particularly temperature, salinity, and pressure.

These variables can change with depth and location. Accurate sonar and acoustic navigation applications may therefore require a sound-speed profile or suitable sound-speed measurements.

Ignoring sound-speed variation can introduce errors in range measurements, bathymetry, and acoustic positioning.

1. Multibeam Echosounder

A multibeam echosounder transmits multiple acoustic beams in a fan-shaped pattern. It measures the seabed across a wider area than a conventional single-beam system.

Multibeam systems are widely used for bathymetric mapping, underwater terrain measurement, and detecting features on or above the seafloor.

  • Creates swath bathymetry.
  • Provides depth measurements across multiple beams.
  • Supports three-dimensional seabed mapping.
  • Requires suitable motion and sound-speed corrections.

2. Side-Scan Sonar

Side-scan sonar produces acoustic images of the seafloor by transmitting sound to either side of the sensor and analyzing the returning echoes.

Differences in echo strength and acoustic shadows help reveal objects, seabed textures, wrecks, and other features.

  • Seafloor imaging.
  • Shipwreck detection.
  • Search and survey operations.
  • Seabed feature identification.

Side-scan imagery is valuable for detecting objects, but conventional side-scan sonar does not independently provide the same direct bathymetric measurements as a multibeam echosounder.

3. Imaging Sonar

Imaging sonar creates acoustic images of nearby objects and structures. It can be useful when visibility is poor or when optical cameras cannot provide reliable images.

Depending on the system, imaging sonar can support obstacle detection, inspection, navigation, and robotic manipulation.

4. Single-Beam and Split-Beam Sonar

Single-beam systems transmit acoustic pulses in a relatively narrow direction and measure the returning echo.

Split-beam systems use divided transducer elements to obtain additional information about the direction of returning echoes. They can be used in applications such as water-column observation and biological acoustic measurements.

Engineering distinction: Multibeam sonar is commonly used to measure bathymetry across a swath. Side-scan sonar is primarily used to create seafloor imagery. They may be combined in one survey, but they do not produce identical information.
04 / Optical Sensing

Underwater Cameras and Optical Systems

Optical systems provide visual information that acoustic sensors cannot always describe in sufficient detail.

Underwater Cameras

Underwater cameras capture images and video of the surrounding environment. They are commonly installed on ROVs, AUVs, and other marine robotic platforms.

Cameras can help operators and automated systems identify objects, inspect structures, observe marine organisms, and document underwater operations.

High-Definition Cameras

Provide detailed visual imagery for close-range inspection, documentation, and scientific observation.

Low-Light Cameras

Designed to capture useful imagery under limited illumination, often combined with artificial underwater lighting.

Stereo Camera Systems

Use multiple synchronized cameras to estimate depth and three-dimensional structure through stereo vision, when calibration and visibility permit.

Structured-Light Systems

Project a known light pattern and analyze its deformation to estimate surface geometry at close range.

Why Is Underwater Imaging Difficult?

  • Light is absorbed and scattered by water.
  • Suspended particles reduce visibility.
  • Artificial lighting can produce backscatter.
  • Color changes with distance and water properties.
  • Camera housings must withstand external pressure.
  • Lens ports and underwater optics require appropriate calibration.

These limitations mean that an underwater camera cannot always replace sonar. Optical and acoustic systems are often complementary.

05 / Environmental Instrumentation

Scientific and Environmental Sensors

Marine robots can carry instruments that measure the physical and chemical properties of seawater during a mission.

1. Pressure and Depth Sensors

A pressure sensor measures the pressure exerted by the surrounding water. Hydrostatic pressure generally increases with depth.

Using pressure measurements, sensor calibration, and appropriate seawater assumptions, the vehicle can estimate its depth below the surface.

Depth measurements support vertical navigation, mission constraints, and monitoring of the vehicle's operating limits.

2. CTD Instruments

CTD stands for Conductivity, Temperature, and Depth. A CTD instrument measures conductivity, temperature, and pressure, which is commonly used to estimate depth.

Conductivity is used with temperature and pressure to calculate seawater salinity. These measurements help researchers characterize water masses and ocean circulation.

3. Dissolved Oxygen Sensors

Dissolved oxygen sensors measure the concentration of oxygen in water. They can support studies of biological activity, water quality, and oxygen depletion.

Sensor technologies may include electrochemical and optical measurement approaches, each with specific calibration and maintenance requirements.

4. Turbidity and Optical Backscatter Sensors

Turbidity sensors estimate the cloudiness of water by measuring how suspended particles affect transmitted or scattered light.

Optical backscatter measurements can help characterize particles in the water column. Their interpretation depends on particle properties, sensor design, and calibration.

5. Chlorophyll Fluorometers

Fluorometers measure fluorescence associated with substances such as chlorophyll.

They can help researchers investigate phytoplankton distribution and changes in biological activity.

6. Acoustic Doppler Current Profilers

An ADCP uses the Doppler effect to estimate water velocity at different distances from the instrument.

It transmits acoustic signals and analyzes the frequency shift of echoes returned by particles moving with the water.

ADCPs are used to investigate ocean currents and water movement. They should not be confused with a DVL, even though both can use Doppler acoustic measurements.

7. Hydrophones

A hydrophone is an underwater acoustic sensor that receives sound pressure signals.

Hydrophones can be used for passive acoustic monitoring, marine mammal research, underwater noise studies, and acoustic communication systems when configured for those purposes.

06 / Underwater Navigation

Why Is Underwater Navigation Different?

A submerged vehicle cannot normally rely on direct GPS positioning. It must estimate its movement and position using onboard sensors, acoustic references, or a combination of navigation technologies.

The GPS Limitation

GPS and other GNSS satellite signals are not normally available to a submerged vehicle because radio signals are strongly attenuated by seawater.

A vehicle may obtain a GNSS position when surfaced, or it may receive acoustic position updates from a support vessel or deployed transponders.

1. Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)

An IMU measures the vehicle's motion using inertial sensors.

A typical IMU includes:

  • Accelerometers to measure linear acceleration.
  • Gyroscopes to measure angular velocity.
  • Sometimes magnetometers to measure the local magnetic field.

These measurements help estimate vehicle attitude and motion. An IMU does not automatically provide an accurate geographic position by itself.

2. Inertial Navigation System (INS)

An INS uses inertial measurements and mathematical integration to estimate position, velocity, and orientation over time.

It can continue operating without external radio or satellite signals, which makes it valuable for underwater vehicles.

However, small errors in acceleration, angular-rate measurements, alignment, and initial conditions can accumulate over time. This effect is known as inertial drift.

3. Doppler Velocity Log (DVL)

A DVL is an acoustic instrument that estimates the velocity of a moving underwater platform relative to the seabed or surrounding water.

It transmits acoustic beams and measures Doppler frequency shifts in the returning echoes.

When bottom lock is available, the DVL can estimate velocity relative to the seabed. This information can help constrain inertial navigation drift.

When bottom lock is unavailable, some systems can operate in water-track mode, estimating velocity relative to the water rather than the seabed.

4. Compass and Heading Sensors

Heading sensors help estimate the direction in which a vehicle is pointing.

Magnetic compasses use the Earth's magnetic field, but readings can be affected by nearby metal, electrical equipment, magnetic interference, and local magnetic conditions.

Inertial gyroscopes and other heading-reference systems may be used to provide additional orientation information.

5. Depth Sensors

Depth measurements provide information about the vehicle's vertical position relative to the water surface.

They are important for controlling mission depth and combining vertical information with horizontal navigation estimates.

Key concept: INS estimates motion continuously, while DVL can provide velocity measurements that help limit accumulated navigation error. Neither instrument should be described as a perfect standalone underwater positioning solution.
07 / Acoustic Positioning

LBL, SBL and USBL Systems

Acoustic positioning systems use sound signals and known reference geometry to estimate the position of submerged vehicles and equipment.

Why Use Acoustic Positioning?

A submerged robot may need an external position reference to reduce navigation uncertainty. Acoustic systems provide a way to exchange signals between the vehicle and reference transponders.

Different architectures are designed for different deployment arrangements, operational areas, and accuracy requirements.

SYSTEM 01

Long Baseline (LBL)

LBL systems use multiple acoustic transponders deployed at known positions, commonly on or near the seafloor.

The underwater vehicle exchanges acoustic signals with these reference points. Measured acoustic ranges are combined with the known transponder positions to calculate the vehicle's position.

  • Uses a network of fixed acoustic references.
  • Suitable for a defined survey area.
  • Can provide accurate local positioning.
  • Requires deployment and calibration of the acoustic array.
SYSTEM 02

Short Baseline (SBL)

SBL systems use multiple acoustic receivers mounted on a surface vessel.

The spacing between these receivers provides a reference geometry for estimating the position of an underwater transponder.

  • Receiver array installed on the support vessel.
  • Uses range and geometric information.
  • Requires suitable vessel motion and alignment information.
  • Performance depends on geometry and deployment conditions.
SYSTEM 03

Ultra-Short Baseline (USBL)

USBL systems use a compact acoustic transceiver array, typically mounted on a support vessel or deployed from it.

The system measures acoustic travel time and the direction of the received signal to estimate the position of an underwater transponder relative to the vessel.

  • Compact transducer arrangement.
  • Often used to track ROVs and underwater vehicles.
  • Can provide position updates to a support vessel.
  • Depends on vessel position, orientation, calibration, and acoustic conditions.

08 / Sensor Fusion

How Navigation Systems Work Together

Reliable underwater navigation usually depends on combining complementary sensors rather than relying on one instrument.

What Is Sensor Fusion?

Sensor fusion is the process of combining information from multiple sensors to produce a more useful estimate of the vehicle's state or environment.

Each sensor has limitations. Combining different measurement types can improve reliability, provided the sensors are correctly calibrated and synchronized.

IMU / INS

Provides continuous estimates of vehicle motion and orientation.

Limitation: inertial errors can accumulate over time.

DVL

Provides acoustic velocity measurements relative to the seabed or water.

Limitation: bottom lock and measurement quality depend on conditions.

Depth Sensor

Provides vertical position information based on pressure measurements.

Limitation: does not determine horizontal geographic position.

Acoustic Positioning

Provides external position references through acoustic measurements.

Limitation: depends on reference geometry, calibration, and acoustic conditions.

Example: A Combined Navigation Architecture

Consider an AUV operating near the seafloor during a mapping mission.

  1. The IMU measures acceleration and angular motion.
  2. The INS estimates the vehicle's changing attitude, velocity, and position.
  3. The DVL measures velocity relative to the seabed when bottom lock is available.
  4. The pressure sensor provides depth information.
  5. An acoustic positioning system may provide external position updates.
  6. The navigation processor combines these measurements into an updated state estimate.

This architecture can reduce uncertainty compared with relying on inertial measurements alone. Its actual performance depends on sensor quality, geometry, calibration, and environmental conditions.

Common Sensor Fusion Algorithms

Navigation systems may use mathematical estimation algorithms to combine measurements and account for uncertainty.

  • Kalman Filter: estimates system state using a model and noisy measurements.
  • Extended Kalman Filter: applies Kalman filtering to nonlinear systems through linearization.
  • Unscented Kalman Filter: handles nonlinear transformations using selected sample points.
  • Particle Filter: represents uncertainty using a collection of weighted state hypotheses.

The appropriate algorithm depends on the vehicle's motion model, sensor characteristics, computational resources, and required performance.

09 / Operational Workflow

From Sensor Measurements to Navigation Decisions

A marine robotic platform must acquire, process, validate, and interpret measurements before they can support navigation and mission control.

01

Data Acquisition

Sensors collect raw measurements from the vehicle and environment.

02

Calibration

Sensor offsets, alignment, and measurement characteristics are checked.

03

Processing

Measurements are filtered, synchronized, and transformed.

04

Sensor Fusion

Complementary data are combined to estimate vehicle state.

05

Navigation Output

Position, attitude, depth, and uncertainty support mission decisions.

Data Quality and Synchronization

Different sensors may operate at different sampling rates. A camera may capture images at one rate, an IMU may generate measurements much faster, and a sonar system may produce data according to its ping schedule.

Accurate interpretation requires timestamps, synchronization, coordinate transformations, and appropriate handling of measurement delays.

Poor synchronization can create misleading results even when each individual sensor is functioning correctly.

10 / Practical Applications

Where Marine Sensors and Navigation Are Used

Sensing and navigation technologies support a wide range of underwater scientific, industrial, and operational missions.

Seafloor Mapping

Multibeam and other sonar systems collect bathymetric information used to create maps of underwater terrain.

Underwater Infrastructure Inspection

Cameras, imaging sonar, and navigation systems support inspection of pipelines, cables, and subsea structures.

Marine Archaeology

Side-scan sonar and underwater imaging help locate and document shipwrecks and submerged cultural heritage sites.

Oceanographic Research

CTD, oxygen, current, and other environmental sensors support research into ocean processes.

Search and Survey Operations

Sonar and navigation systems help search large underwater areas and identify objects of interest.

Autonomous Vehicle Navigation

Sensor fusion and acoustic positioning help AUVs estimate their location and follow planned routes.

11 / Real-World Engineering

Case Study: WHOI Sentry AUV

An operational example of how multiple navigation and sensing technologies work together in a deep-submergence vehicle.

AUTONOMOUS UNDERWATER VEHICLE

Sentry — Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution

Sentry is an autonomous underwater vehicle developed for deep-ocean scientific exploration and survey missions.

Its navigation architecture uses a Doppler Velocity Log and an inertial navigation system, aided by acoustic navigation technologies such as USBL or LBL.

This combination illustrates why underwater vehicles integrate different navigation technologies instead of depending on one sensor.

Sentry also carries scientific instruments selected for individual missions. These may include specialized imaging and environmental measurement equipment.

What Can We Learn From This Architecture?

  • DVL contributes velocity measurements.
  • INS provides continuous motion estimation.
  • Acoustic systems provide external navigation references.
  • Scientific payloads collect mission-specific measurements.
  • Communication systems can support vehicle monitoring and mission updates.

The important lesson is that a reliable underwater navigation solution is an integrated engineering architecture, not simply a GPS replacement.

12 / Technical Challenges

Limitations and Engineering Challenges

Marine sensors operate in a demanding environment where pressure, water properties, acoustic conditions, and mechanical movement can affect measurement quality.

1. Inertial Drift

Small inertial sensor errors can accumulate during navigation. Long missions may require external references or velocity aiding to constrain the error.

2. Acoustic Noise and Multipath

Machinery, vessels, marine life, reverberation, and reflections from surfaces can affect acoustic measurements and communication.

3. Sound-Speed Variation

Temperature, salinity, and pressure affect sound propagation. Incorrect sound-speed assumptions can introduce range and positioning errors.

4. Optical Visibility

Turbidity, suspended particles, low illumination, and backscatter can reduce camera image quality.

5. Sensor Calibration

Incorrect calibration, mounting alignment, timing, and coordinate transformations can introduce systematic measurement errors.

6. DVL Bottom-Lock Loss

A DVL may lose bottom lock when the seabed is outside its effective range or when acoustic returns are insufficient. Water-track operation may provide alternative information, but it measures velocity relative to the water.

7. Pressure and Mechanical Reliability

Sensor housings, connectors, cables, and mounting structures must withstand the pressure and conditions associated with the intended operating depth.

8. Data Integration Complexity

Combining data from different manufacturers and sensor interfaces requires compatible communication protocols, data formats, timestamps, coordinate frames, and software interfaces.

13 / Future Developments

The Future of Marine Robotic Sensing and Navigation

New sensors, estimation algorithms, and autonomous technologies are expanding the capabilities of marine robotic systems.

AI-Assisted Perception

Machine learning can support object detection, image classification, seabed interpretation, and anomaly identification.

Visual-Inertial Navigation

Combining optical observations with inertial measurements may improve navigation in environments where visual features are available.

Advanced Acoustic Mapping

Improved sonar processing and imaging techniques can provide more detailed information about underwater environments.

Adaptive Sensor Fusion

Future systems may dynamically adjust the contribution of individual sensors according to their quality and uncertainty.

Cooperative Navigation

Multiple underwater vehicles may exchange selected information to improve their collective awareness and mission coordination.

Digital Ocean Observation

Sensor networks and robotic platforms may contribute to connected ocean observation systems and improved environmental models.
