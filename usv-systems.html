01 / Foundations

Understanding USV Systems

Uncrewed Surface Vehicles are marine platforms that operate on the water's surface without people onboard. They combine vessel engineering, navigation, communication, sensors, propulsion, and control software.

What Is an Uncrewed Surface Vehicle?

An Uncrewed Surface Vehicle (USV) is a marine vessel designed to perform tasks on the surface of the water without carrying an onboard human crew.

USVs can be remotely controlled by operators located on a nearby support vessel, onshore control station, or another authorized remote location. They can also execute pre-planned routes and perform selected navigation and operational decisions using onboard autonomous systems.

Their design varies according to the mission. A small battery-powered survey craft may operate in shallow coastal waters, while a larger diesel-powered or wind-propelled platform may conduct long-duration operations in offshore environments.

Depending on their equipment, USVs can collect oceanographic, atmospheric, hydrographic, and maritime information, transmit selected data to shore, and cooperate with other marine platforms.

Important distinction: Uncrewed does not mean completely independent. USVs may operate with different levels of automation, but qualified human operators remain responsible for mission planning, supervision, safety procedures, and operational decisions.

Why Are USVs Important?

  • They can collect data without placing crew members onboard.
  • They can access selected environments that are difficult or costly for conventional vessels.
  • They can perform repetitive survey routes for extended periods.
  • They can support maritime research and hydrographic surveying.
  • They can work alongside crewed ships and underwater vehicles.
  • They can transmit selected measurements to remote operators.

What Makes a USV an Integrated System?

  • A marine hull and structural platform.
  • Propulsion and steering equipment.
  • Onboard energy and power distribution.
  • Navigation and situational awareness sensors.
  • Communication and remote-control systems.
  • Autonomous guidance and control software.
  • Payload equipment for mission-specific tasks.
02 / Vehicle Classification

Types of USV Platforms

USVs are not built around one universal hull or propulsion design. Their dimensions, structure, and operating principles depend on mission duration, sea conditions, payload requirements, and deployment environment.

01

Small Survey USVs

Compact platforms designed for nearshore surveys, rivers, lakes, harbors, and shallow-water operations.

They may carry multibeam sonar, GNSS receivers, cameras, and other hydrographic instruments.

Shallow Water Hydrographic Survey
02

Long-Endurance USVs

Designed for extended operations over large ocean areas, often using wind, solar energy, or other energy-efficient propulsion arrangements.

They are useful for oceanographic and atmospheric data collection over long periods.

Ocean Monitoring Long Endurance
03

Motorized USVs

Powered by conventional or electric motors. Depending on the platform, they may use diesel, gasoline, battery-electric, or hybrid power systems.

Their capabilities can include surveying, inspection, transportation of equipment, and specialized missions.

Motorized Propulsion Industrial Missions
04

Research USVs

Configured to collect scientific measurements from the atmosphere, sea surface, and upper water column.

Their payloads may include weather stations, water-quality sensors, and acoustic instruments.

Marine Science Environmental Data
05

Specialized Operational USVs

Designed for specific operational roles, including maritime monitoring, infrastructure support, and other specialized maritime tasks.

Their payload and safety requirements depend heavily on the intended operation.

Specialized Payloads Maritime Operations

Some USVs combine different power sources, communication methods, and operating modes.

A platform may support remote piloting, supervised autonomy, and different mission configurations.

Hybrid Systems Flexible Operations

03 / Marine Engineering

Hull Design and Structural Architecture

The hull is the physical foundation of a USV. It affects stability, resistance, payload capacity, maneuverability, speed, and performance in waves.

01

Monohull Design

A monohull uses one main hull to provide buoyancy and support the vessel's equipment.

It can provide a relatively simple structural arrangement and may be suitable for different vessel sizes and operating conditions.

  • One primary hull structure.
  • Space for propulsion and internal equipment.
  • Hydrodynamic performance depends on hull geometry.
  • Stability depends on displacement, center of gravity, and hull form.
02

Catamaran Design

A catamaran uses two parallel hulls connected by a structural platform or cross-members.

The separation between the hulls can provide a wide platform and useful transverse stability. It may also provide space for sensors and equipment.

  • Two main buoyant hulls.
  • Wide structural arrangement.
  • Potentially useful working deck area.
  • Performance depends on hull spacing and wave interaction.
03

Trimaran Design

A trimaran has a main central hull and two smaller side hulls, commonly called outriggers.

The side hulls can improve stability and provide additional buoyancy, depending on the vessel's dimensions and loading.

  • One central hull.
  • Two supporting side hulls.
  • Wide overall beam.
  • Specialized structural and hydrodynamic considerations.
04

Specialized Hull Forms

Some USVs use unusual or mission-specific designs, such as wave-piercing forms, surfboard-like platforms, or streamlined hulls.

Saildrone-type platforms, for example, use a slender hull with a distinctive wind-propulsion wing rather than a conventional motorboat layout.

  • Optimized for particular sea conditions.
  • May support unusual propulsion arrangements.
  • Can prioritize endurance over high speed.
  • Require mission-specific structural evaluation.

Materials and Structural Protection

Marine structures must withstand repeated exposure to seawater, waves, vibration, sunlight, and mechanical loads.

  • Marine-grade aluminum: offers a useful combination of strength and relatively low structural weight when properly designed.
  • Composite materials: can provide corrosion resistance and favorable weight characteristics, depending on their construction and repair requirements.
  • Marine-grade stainless steel: may be used for selected fittings, fasteners, shafts, and components with appropriate corrosion considerations.
  • Protective coatings: help protect surfaces from corrosion, fouling, and environmental degradation.

Material selection must consider strength, fatigue, galvanic corrosion, manufacturing, maintenance, and the marine environment.

Hydrodynamics and Vessel Stability

Hydrodynamics studies how water flows around the hull and how the vessel interacts with waves, currents, and surrounding water.

Hull resistance influences the power needed to maintain a particular speed. A poorly optimized shape may increase drag and reduce mission endurance.

Stability describes the vessel's ability to resist or recover from disturbances such as waves, wind, and changes in payload distribution.

Engineers consider displacement, center of gravity, center of buoyancy, metacentric height, freeboard, roll behavior, and loading conditions when assessing the vessel's stability.

04 / Propulsion and Energy

Propulsion, Power and Energy Systems

The propulsion system determines how the USV moves, while its energy architecture determines how long it can operate and which equipment it can support.

Conventional Motor Propulsion

Many USVs use a combustion engine or electric motor to drive a propeller.

  • Diesel engines: commonly used in some larger motorized platforms.
  • Gasoline engines: may be used in selected smaller craft.
  • Electric motors: convert electrical energy into mechanical rotation.
  • Propellers: generate thrust by accelerating water.
  • Motor controllers: regulate electric motor operation.

The correct arrangement depends on vessel size, mission duration, required speed, noise, maintenance, and fuel or battery availability.

Wind-Based Propulsion

Some long-endurance USVs use wind as their primary source of propulsion.

A rigid sail or wing interacts with the wind to produce aerodynamic forces. The vessel uses these forces to move through the water, with its steering system controlling direction.

  • Can reduce dependence on conventional fuel.
  • May support long-duration ocean missions.
  • Performance depends on wind conditions and route planning.
  • Requires control of the wing or sail and vessel heading.

Solar-Assisted Power

Solar panels convert sunlight into electrical energy that can support onboard equipment and recharge batteries when the power architecture permits.

Solar power may be used alongside wind propulsion, electric propulsion, or other energy systems.

  • Photovoltaic panels collect solar energy.
  • Power electronics regulate electrical output.
  • Battery storage supports operation when sunlight is unavailable.
  • Energy management balances generation and consumption.

Hybrid Power Systems

A hybrid USV may combine a combustion engine, electric propulsion, batteries, solar generation, or other energy sources.

The system architecture determines how energy is generated, stored, distributed, and used by the propulsion and payload systems.

  • Flexible power generation.
  • Potentially improved energy management.
  • Multiple operating modes.
  • More complex electrical and control integration.

Power Management and Energy Monitoring

A USV's power system must supply the propulsion equipment and the electrical loads required for navigation, communication, computing, sensors, and safety systems.

  • Battery management: monitors battery voltage, current, temperature, and operating limits.
  • Power distribution: supplies electrical loads through suitable circuits and protection devices.
  • Energy monitoring: estimates remaining energy and consumption trends.
  • Load prioritization: can preserve essential navigation and safety functions when energy becomes limited.

Mission endurance is affected by speed, sea state, wind, current, propulsion efficiency, payload consumption, and energy-generation conditions.

Engineering principle: More battery capacity does not automatically produce a better USV. Increasing battery mass can change displacement and stability, while propulsion and sensor requirements may increase energy consumption. The entire energy architecture must be designed together.
05 / Navigation and Awareness

How Does a USV Navigate?

A USV must determine its position, maintain a planned route, monitor nearby vessels and obstacles, and respond appropriately to changing maritime conditions.

Surface Navigation Environment

Unlike submerged AUVs, surface vehicles can normally receive satellite navigation signals when antenna visibility and operating conditions are suitable.

However, satellite position alone is not enough for safe navigation. The USV also needs information about its heading, movement, surrounding vessels, obstacles, and environmental conditions.

GNSS and GPS

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) provide positioning information using satellite signals. GPS is one of the GNSS constellations.

  • Provides geographical position.
  • Can support route following and waypoint navigation.
  • May provide speed and timing information.
  • Accuracy depends on receiver quality, signal conditions, and correction methods.

High-accuracy applications may use correction services such as RTK GNSS when suitable infrastructure and conditions are available.

Compass and Heading Sensors

A heading sensor estimates the direction in which the vessel is pointing.

Marine compasses and inertial measurement systems can contribute to heading and attitude estimation.

Accurate heading information helps the autopilot determine whether the vessel is aligned with the desired route.

Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)

An IMU typically includes gyroscopes and accelerometers to measure angular motion and specific force.

It can help estimate vessel movement and attitude, particularly when combined with GNSS and other navigation sensors.

Radar and Marine Awareness

Marine radar detects surrounding objects by transmitting radio-frequency energy and receiving reflected signals.

It can support detection of vessels, coastlines, and selected obstacles, depending on target characteristics and environmental conditions.

AIS — Automatic Identification System

AIS is a maritime communication system through which equipped vessels exchange identity, position, course, speed, and other navigation-related information.

AIS can help a USV build awareness of cooperative traffic, but not every vessel transmits AIS information.

Cameras and Other Detection Sensors

Cameras can provide visual information about nearby vessels, floating objects, and the surrounding environment.

Some platforms may also use thermal cameras, LiDAR, or additional sensors to improve situational awareness.

Each sensor has limitations related to visibility, range, weather, lighting, reflections, and environmental conditions.

Sensor Fusion for Surface Navigation

Sensor fusion combines information from different sensors to produce a more useful estimate of the vessel's state and surrounding environment.

For example, GNSS can provide geographical position, an IMU can provide motion information, a compass can support heading estimation, and radar or cameras can help identify surrounding objects.

The navigation software can use these inputs to estimate the USV's position, velocity, orientation, and operational situation.

06 / Control and Autonomy

Remote Control and Autonomous Operations

USVs can operate through different control arrangements. The level of automation depends on the platform, mission, operating environment, and safety requirements.

01

Remote Control

A human operator directly sends commands to control the vessel's movement and operating functions through a communication link.

The operator may monitor the vessel through cameras, telemetry, navigation data, and other available information.

02

Supervised Autonomy

The USV follows programmed routes and performs selected navigation tasks automatically while a human operator monitors its operation.

Operators can intervene or modify the mission when the system and communication architecture allow it.

03

Higher-Level Autonomy

More advanced systems can make additional decisions onboard, such as adapting a route or responding to selected operational events.

Their decisions must remain within defined safety limits and operational authority.

Guidance, Navigation and Control (GNC)

Guidance, Navigation and Control is a useful way to understand the relationship between route planning, position estimation, and vessel movement.

  • Guidance: determines the desired route, heading, speed, or next waypoint.
  • Navigation: estimates the current position, heading, velocity, and movement of the vessel.
  • Control: converts the desired motion into commands for propulsion and steering equipment.

These functions work together in a feedback loop. The controller compares the desired motion with the estimated actual motion and adjusts its commands accordingly.

Autopilot and Route Following

A USV's autopilot can use navigation information and predefined mission instructions to maintain a heading, follow a route, or travel toward a waypoint.

Route-following software may account for vessel turning behavior, speed limits, route boundaries, and navigation uncertainty.

The system must also distinguish between following a planned route and navigating safely through a changing maritime environment.

Collision Avoidance and Maritime Safety

A USV operates in an environment shared with crewed vessels, other uncrewed platforms, fishing activity, floating objects, and fixed maritime infrastructure.

Collision-avoidance systems may combine radar, AIS, cameras, navigation data, and decision-making software to identify potential collision risks.

  • Detecting and tracking nearby targets.
  • Estimating relative position and movement.
  • Identifying potential collision situations.
  • Evaluating safe maneuvering options.
  • Monitoring the result of an avoidance action.
  • Escalating uncertain situations to human operators when required.
Important safety point: COLREGs are the international collision-prevention rules for vessels at sea. A USV's navigation and autonomy systems must be designed and assessed for applicable maritime rules and operating conditions. Installing AIS or radar alone does not prove that a vehicle can safely interpret every traffic situation.
07 / Communication Architecture

USV Communication Networks

Communication connects the surface vehicle with remote operators, support vessels, shore facilities, and sometimes underwater platforms.

Radio Communication

Radio links can support communication between a USV and a nearby shore station or support vessel.

Their range and reliability depend on antenna height, frequency, transmit power, terrain, interference, and line-of-sight conditions.

Cellular Networks

Cellular communication may be used when the USV operates within suitable network coverage.

It can support telemetry, remote monitoring, and data exchange, but offshore coverage cannot be assumed.

Satellite Communication

Satellite links can support long-distance communication between offshore USVs and remote control facilities.

Depending on the service and equipment, they can carry telemetry, mission updates, selected sensor data, and other operational information.

Wi-Fi and Local Networks

Wi-Fi and other local wireless technologies may be used for setup, maintenance, configuration, and data transfer when the vehicle is near a support facility.

Communication Between USVs and Underwater Vehicles

Some USVs can cooperate with AUVs and ROVs as part of a larger marine robotic system.

The USV may provide a surface communication gateway, navigation support, acoustic tracking, or a location for transferring underwater vehicle data.

An acoustic modem can exchange information between underwater equipment and the surface platform. The USV can then use its own above-water communication link to relay selected information to a remote operator.

Communication is not guaranteed at all times. A robust USV should have a defined response to communication loss, such as entering a safe operating mode, attempting reconnection, holding a safe state where possible, or following a pre-approved recovery procedure. The exact response depends on the vessel design and mission risk assessment.
08 / Onboard Systems

Computing, Software and Payload Integration

The onboard electronics and software coordinate navigation, control, communication, propulsion, power management, and mission-specific equipment.

Onboard Computer

The onboard computer executes the software responsible for mission management, navigation, sensor processing, and communication with other subsystems.

  • Processes navigation measurements.
  • Executes route-following instructions.
  • Coordinates propulsion and steering commands.
  • Monitors equipment status.
  • Stores mission information and sensor data.
  • Communicates with remote control systems.

Control Electronics

Electronic controllers provide interfaces between the onboard computer and physical equipment.

  • Motor controllers.
  • Steering actuators.
  • Power distribution controllers.
  • Navigation sensor interfaces.
  • Communication modules.
  • Monitoring and safety circuits.

Data Logging and Storage

USVs may record navigation information, sensor measurements, mission events, communication status, and equipment performance.

Data storage supports later analysis, troubleshooting, mission reporting, and scientific quality control.

System Integration

Subsystems need compatible interfaces and reliable data exchange.

Engineers must consider communication protocols, timestamps, coordinate frames, data formats, power requirements, and software compatibility.

09 / Sensors and Payloads

USV Sensors and Mission Equipment

A USV's payload is selected according to the information required by the mission. Sensors may measure the atmosphere, sea surface, water column, seabed, or surrounding maritime environment.

Multibeam Echosounder

Sends multiple acoustic beams toward the seabed and measures their return times to estimate water depth across a swath beneath the vessel.

Bathymetry Hydrographic Survey

Side-Scan Sonar

Produces acoustic imagery of the seabed and can help identify objects, structures, and seabed features.

Seafloor Imaging Object Detection

CTD Sensors

Measure conductivity, temperature, and pressure. These measurements can be used to derive salinity and depth-related information.

Oceanography Water Properties

ADCP

An Acoustic Doppler Current Profiler uses Doppler measurements to estimate water-current velocity at different depths or along selected measurement cells.

Current Measurement Water Column

Weather Sensors

Measure atmospheric conditions such as wind speed, wind direction, air temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure.

Meteorology Weather Monitoring

Oceanographic Sensors

Depending on the mission, a USV may measure sea-surface temperature, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll, carbon dioxide, salinity, and other environmental variables.

Environmental Monitoring Marine Science

Radar and AIS

Radar provides information about detected objects, while AIS can provide identification and navigation reports from transmitting vessels.

Maritime Awareness Traffic Monitoring

Optical and Thermal Cameras

Cameras collect visual information about the sea surface, nearby vessels, floating objects, and selected environmental features.

Visual Monitoring Image Analysis

Acoustic and Specialized Sensors

Additional acoustic instruments, positioning equipment, and mission-specific payloads may be installed when required.

Specialized Payload Marine Robotics

Sensor Calibration and Data Quality

Installing a sensor does not automatically guarantee accurate measurements. Sensors must be selected, calibrated, mounted, and operated correctly.

Their measurements may be affected by temperature, vessel motion, vibration, sensor placement, water flow, biofouling, interference, and equipment aging.

Data processing may therefore include calibration corrections, motion compensation, time synchronization, quality checks, and georeferencing.

10 / Operational Workflow

How Does a USV Mission Work?

A USV mission involves planning, equipment preparation, deployment, navigation, remote supervision, data collection, and post-mission review.

PHASE 01

Mission Definition

Identify the mission objective, survey area, required sensors, operating duration, and expected data.

PHASE 02

Route Planning

Define waypoints, operating boundaries, speed limits, environmental constraints, and planned recovery location.

PHASE 03

System Preparation

Inspect the hull, propulsion, batteries, navigation equipment, sensors, communication links, and onboard software.

PHASE 04

Deployment

Launch the vehicle from a suitable location, establish communications, and verify its navigation status.

PHASE 05

Mission Execution

The USV follows its mission instructions, collects sensor data, and monitors its operating condition.

PHASE 06

Remote Supervision

Operators monitor position, heading, speed, equipment health, communications, and surrounding maritime conditions.

PHASE 07

Recovery

The vessel returns to its designated recovery area or follows the appropriate mission-ending procedure.

PHASE 08

Data Processing

Engineers retrieve and process the collected measurements, apply quality checks, and prepare useful survey or research products.

PHASE 09

Maintenance and Review

Inspect the vessel, review mission performance, maintain equipment, and prepare the next deployment.

12 / Real-World Engineering

Real USV Platforms in Operation

Different USV platforms demonstrate how hull design, propulsion, sensors, communication, and autonomy can be configured around different missions.

Case Study 01 — Saildrone Explorer

Saildrone Explorer is a wind-propelled uncrewed surface vehicle designed for long-duration oceanographic and atmospheric data collection.

Its design combines a slender surface hull with a rigid wing used for wind propulsion. Solar energy supports onboard electrical systems and scientific equipment.

The platform carries different sensors depending on mission requirements. These may include instruments for measuring wind, air temperature, humidity, sea-surface temperature, wave conditions, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and other environmental variables.

Satellite communication supports the transfer of selected information to remote facilities, allowing mission teams to monitor the vehicle and receive scientific measurements.

The Explorer is an example of a USV designed to prioritize long endurance and persistent environmental observations rather than high-speed operation.

Wind Propulsion Solar-Assisted Power Ocean Monitoring Long-Endurance Missions

Official technical information: Saildrone — Explorer Platform

Case Study 02 — DriX USV

DriX is a motorized uncrewed surface vessel used for marine survey and mapping operations. NOAA describes it as a diesel-powered platform capable of both remote-controlled and supervised-autonomous operation.

Its hull is designed as a streamlined surface vessel with an extended submerged portion. Its configuration supports the installation of different sensors according to survey requirements.

DriX can carry hydrographic and oceanographic equipment and may work alongside larger research or survey vessels.

NOAA has described its use in mapping operations and its ability to work with underwater vehicles using acoustic communication and tracking arrangements.

This example demonstrates how a USV can function as an independent survey platform and as part of a larger integrated marine robotic operation.

13 / Technical Challenges

Engineering Challenges in USV Operations

Operating without people onboard introduces engineering challenges related to safety, environmental conditions, system reliability, communication, and recovery.

Harsh Sea Conditions

Waves, wind, currents, and changing weather can affect vessel stability, propulsion demand, sensor measurements, and route execution.

Collision Avoidance

USVs must identify and respond to other vessels and obstacles while operating within a dynamic maritime environment.

Communication Loss

Radio, cellular, and satellite links may become unavailable or unreliable. The vehicle needs a predefined safe response to communication failure.

Energy and Endurance

Limited fuel or battery capacity, variable renewable energy, and changing propulsion loads can affect mission duration.

Navigation Uncertainty

GNSS interference, sensor errors, incorrect calibration, and environmental disturbances can reduce navigation accuracy.

Water Ingress and Corrosion

Seals, connectors, exposed electronics, and mechanical components must withstand seawater exposure, vibration, and weather.

Sensor Reliability

Biofouling, contamination, poor mounting, vibration, and calibration errors can reduce data quality.

Remote Recovery

A failed propulsion system, damaged steering equipment, or severe weather can make retrieval difficult.

Cybersecurity

Remote communication and connected control systems introduce risks involving unauthorized access, data integrity, and disruption of mission operations.

Software and Integration Failures

Errors in navigation software, sensor interfaces, control logic, or communication protocols can affect the entire platform.

Critical operational lesson: A USV must be designed not only to perform its planned task, but also to manage abnormal conditions safely. Redundant critical sensors, fault monitoring, communication-loss procedures, and human intervention arrangements are important parts of a reliable design.
14 / Future Developments

The Future of Uncrewed Surface Vehicles

Advances in artificial intelligence, sensor technology, renewable energy, navigation, and cooperative robotics are expanding the potential capabilities of USVs.

AI-Assisted Situational Awareness

Machine learning can support the analysis of camera and sensor data to identify selected objects, classify observations, and assist operators in understanding the surrounding environment.

AI classifications require validation. They should not be treated as infallible collision-avoidance decisions.

Advanced Autonomous Navigation

Improved sensor fusion and decision-making algorithms may help USVs navigate more complex environments and adapt to changing mission conditions.

Renewable Energy Integration

Improved solar systems, wind propulsion, energy storage, and power management may increase endurance and reduce dependence on conventional fuel.

Multi-USV Cooperation

Multiple USVs may coordinate survey routes, distribute measurements, and share information to cover larger areas.

USV-AUV Cooperation

Surface and underwater vehicles may operate as coordinated teams, sharing navigation information, communications, and mission data.

Digital Twins and Predictive Maintenance

Digital models and equipment monitoring may help engineers understand system behavior, identify developing faults, and plan maintenance.
