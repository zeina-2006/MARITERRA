Maritime Data Collection

Understanding how ships acquire, measure, validate, and organize operational data from onboard sensors, navigation equipment, communication systems, and external information sources.

Home / Maritime Technology / Technology Integration / Data Collection
01 / INTRODUCTION

What Is Maritime Data Collection?

Maritime data collection is the process of acquiring information from sensors, navigation equipment, machinery systems, communication devices, crew inputs, and external services used in maritime operations.

Modern vessels generate information continuously. This information can describe the ship's position, speed, heading, depth, engine condition, fuel consumption, weather conditions, cargo status, and surrounding traffic.

Collected data supports navigation, machinery monitoring, operational planning, safety procedures, maintenance, and communication between vessels and shore-based systems.

Important distinction: Data collection does not automatically mean that information is accurate or useful. Measurements must be checked for quality, timing, source reliability, and correct interpretation before they are used in operational decisions.
02 / DATA SOURCES

Where Does Maritime Data Come From?

Maritime data can originate from several independent sources. Each source measures or provides a different aspect of vessel operations.

Navigation Sensors

GNSS receivers, gyrocompasses, speed logs, echo sounders, and other navigation instruments provide position, heading, speed, and depth information.

Machinery Sensors

Temperature, pressure, vibration, flow, and rotational-speed sensors monitor engines, pumps, generators, and auxiliary machinery.

Environmental Sensors

Weather instruments and environmental measurement systems provide information about wind, atmospheric pressure, temperature, and other surrounding conditions.

Communication Systems

AIS, satellite communication, radio systems, and shore-based services can provide or exchange vessel and operational information.

Human-Entered Information

Crew members and operators may enter observations, maintenance records, voyage information, and operational reports.

External Data Services

Weather forecasts, navigational warnings, port information, and other authorized external services can supplement onboard measurements.

03 / SENSORS AND MEASUREMENTS

How Sensors Acquire Information

A sensor detects a physical condition and converts it into a measurable signal. Depending on its design, this signal may be electrical, digital, or another form that can be processed by an instrument or control system.

Instrument / Sensor Measured Information Operational Use
GNSS Receiver Position and time information Positioning and navigation support
Gyrocompass Heading information Heading reference for navigation systems
Speed Log Speed information Speed monitoring and navigation
Echo Sounder Water depth beneath the vessel Under-keel and depth awareness
Temperature Sensor Temperature of a measured component or fluid Machinery condition monitoring
Pressure Sensor Pressure in a system or pipeline Equipment and system monitoring
Vibration Sensor Mechanical vibration Condition assessment and fault investigation
Anemometer Wind speed Environmental awareness and operations
Engineering consideration: A sensor measures a particular physical quantity. It does not necessarily determine the complete operational meaning of that measurement. Interpretation depends on calibration, context, processing, and the system receiving the data.
04 / COLLECTION WORKFLOW

How Does the Collection Process Work?

A simplified data acquisition workflow can be divided into four stages. Actual vessel architectures may include additional processing, redundancy, and safety mechanisms.

STAGE 01

Measurement

A sensor or information source measures or provides an operational parameter.

STAGE 02

Acquisition

An instrument or acquisition unit receives the signal or digital information.

STAGE 03

Validation

The system checks whether the information is plausible, complete, and correctly timed.

STAGE 04

Distribution

Validated information is made available to authorized systems or applications.

In practice, validation may involve checking measurement ranges, timestamps, communication status, sensor diagnostics, and consistency with independent sources.

05 / DATA CHARACTERISTICS

Types of Maritime Data

Real-Time Data

Information delivered with sufficiently low delay for a particular operational purpose. What counts as real-time depends on the application and its timing requirements.

Historical Data

Previously recorded measurements and operational records used for trend analysis, reporting, maintenance, and investigation.

Continuous Data

Measurements acquired repeatedly over time, such as engine temperature or vibration.

Event-Based Data

Information recorded when a defined event occurs, such as an alarm, equipment fault, or system state change.

06 / DATA QUALITY

Why Is Data Quality Critical?

Data quality determines whether collected information can be trusted for its intended purpose. A technically connected system may still produce misleading results if its input data is inaccurate, delayed, incomplete, duplicated, or incorrectly identified.

Accuracy

How closely a measurement represents the actual value of the quantity being measured.

Completeness

Whether the required data fields and observations are available.

Timeliness

Whether information arrives early enough for the intended operational decision.

Consistency

Whether values and formats remain understandable and compatible across systems.

Validity

Whether data follows defined rules, acceptable ranges, and expected formats.

Traceability

Whether the origin, timestamp, and processing history of information can be identified.

Example: If a navigation system receives an old position measurement but treats it as current, the value may look technically valid while being operationally misleading. A timestamp and freshness check are therefore essential.
07 / DATA INTERFACES

How Is Collected Data Transferred?

After acquisition, information may be transferred between onboard instruments, processing units, monitoring systems, and authorized external services. The interface must be suitable for the data type, required speed, network architecture, and safety requirements.

Serial Interfaces

Serial communication is used by maritime electronic instruments to exchange structured information. IEC 61162-1:2024 describes a single-talker, multiple-listener interface for one-way data transmission.

Ethernet-Based Interfaces

Shipboard Ethernet networks support communication between multiple connected equipment systems. IEC 61162-450:2024 addresses high-speed communication between maritime navigation and radiocommunication equipment and other ship systems.

Wireless and Satellite Links

Wireless and satellite communication can transfer selected information between vessels, shore facilities, and external services. Availability and latency depend on the communication service and environment.

Data Exchange Platforms

Digital platforms can collect and exchange information between ships and port authorities. The IMO Maritime Single Window is an example of a centralized digital information exchange approach for port calls.

Standards matter: IEC 61162-1 is not a universal high-bandwidth network standard. It is designed for a specific serial communication arrangement. Radar imagery, video, and intensive file transfers require other suitable technologies and interfaces.
08 / MARITIME APPLICATIONS

Where Is Data Collection Used?

Navigation Support

Position, heading, speed, and depth information help navigation systems present operational information to bridge teams.

Engine Monitoring

Machinery measurements help operators monitor operating conditions and identify abnormal changes that may require investigation.

Condition-Based Maintenance

Recorded temperature, vibration, pressure, and other measurements can support maintenance decisions when interpreted using appropriate engineering methods.

Voyage Analysis

Historical operational data can help evaluate speed, fuel consumption, voyage performance, and operational patterns.

Port Operations

Electronic ship and port information exchange can support arrival, stay, and departure clearance processes.

Safety Investigation

Recorded data may support incident analysis, event reconstruction, and the investigation of operational conditions.

09 / TECHNICAL CHALLENGES

Challenges in Maritime Data Collection

Sensor Failure

A faulty sensor may generate missing, unstable, or misleading measurements. Diagnostics and independent checks can help identify the problem.

Calibration Drift

Sensor readings may gradually deviate from expected values. Calibration and verification procedures help manage this risk.

Communication Loss

Network interruptions, damaged cables, interference, or equipment faults can interrupt data transfer.

Incompatible Formats

Different devices may use different interfaces, message structures, units, or data conventions.

Time Synchronization

Inconsistent timestamps can make it difficult to correlate measurements from different equipment.

Cybersecurity Risks

Unauthorized access, manipulated data, compromised devices, and disrupted communication can affect the trustworthiness and availability of collected information.

10 / CYBERSECURITY

Protecting Maritime Data Collection

Connected sensors and acquisition systems can introduce cybersecurity risks. Protecting the collection process requires more than securing the central computer: the devices, interfaces, networks, access privileges, and data pathways must also be considered.

  • Restrict access to authorized users and devices.
  • Separate networks according to operational and security requirements.
  • Monitor unusual communication and unexpected data changes.
  • Maintain secure configurations and update equipment where supported.
  • Validate critical measurements against independent sources when appropriate.
  • Maintain backups and documented recovery procedures.
  • Preserve logs and timestamps to support investigation and traceability.
Critical principle: A connected sensor is not automatically a trustworthy sensor. Data integrity, device security, and operational validation must be considered together.
11 / FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

The Future of Maritime Data Collection

Maritime data collection is evolving alongside digitalization, connected ship systems, and advanced analytical technologies.

Intelligent Data Analytics

Analytical systems may identify patterns and anomalies in large operational datasets. Their results still depend on data quality, suitable models, and human review.

Digital Twins

Digital representations of vessels or equipment can use collected data to support simulation, monitoring, and engineering analysis.

Improved Interoperability

Standardized interfaces and data exchange approaches can improve communication between shipboard systems and shore-based platforms.

Stronger Data Security

Security-by-design, better monitoring, and improved system resilience will remain important as more maritime equipment becomes digitally connected.
