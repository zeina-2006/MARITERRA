01 / INTRODUCTION

What Are Maritime Communication Networks?

Maritime communication networks are the systems, technologies, equipment, and communication links that allow ships, shore stations, port authorities, other vessels, and satellite services to exchange information.

These networks support different operational needs. A vessel may use radio communication for navigation and safety, satellite communication for long-distance connectivity, and onboard digital networks to exchange information between navigation and machinery systems.

Communication requirements differ according to distance, data volume, urgency, reliability, coverage, and the type of information being exchanged.

Important distinction: Maritime communication is not one single network. It is an environment containing several communication systems with different purposes, ranges, technical characteristics, and regulatory requirements.
02 / COMMUNICATION CATEGORIES

Main Types of Maritime Communication

Ship-to-Ship Communication

Communication directly between vessels. It supports bridge-to-bridge coordination, navigational safety, and operational information exchange.

Ship-to-Shore Communication

Communication between a vessel and coastal stations, ports, authorities, or shore-based operational centers.

Shipboard Communication

Communication between equipment and systems installed on the same vessel, including navigation, monitoring, and control systems.

Satellite Communication

Communication using satellite infrastructure to provide connectivity over large geographical areas, depending on service coverage.

03 / RADIO COMMUNICATION

Maritime Radio Communication

Radio communication is a fundamental part of maritime operations. It allows vessels and coastal stations to exchange voice and digital information over designated radio frequencies.

VHF — Very High Frequency

VHF is commonly used for short-range maritime communication, including bridge-to-bridge communication, port operations, and distress and safety communication.

Its coverage is generally influenced by antenna height, propagation conditions, and obstructions.

MF — Medium Frequency

MF radio supports medium-range maritime communication. It is used within the appropriate GMDSS arrangements for distress, urgency, safety, and other communications.

HF — High Frequency

HF radio can support long-range communication through skywave propagation under suitable conditions. Its performance can vary with frequency, time, ionospheric conditions, and other environmental factors.

Digital Selective Calling (DSC)

DSC is a digital calling system used in maritime radio services. It supports automated calling and distress alerting functions within the GMDSS framework.

Technical note: VHF, MF, and HF are frequency bands, not three interchangeable communication services. Their propagation characteristics and operational roles differ. The suitable choice depends on the required coverage and applicable radio arrangements.
04 / MARITIME SAFETY

Global Maritime Distress and Safety System

The Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS) is an internationally coordinated system that uses terrestrial and satellite communication technologies to support distress alerting, maritime safety information, and general communication.

GMDSS requirements are included in Chapter IV of the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS).

Distress Alerting

Provides mechanisms for transmitting distress alerts to relevant rescue coordination services and stations.

Maritime Safety Information

Supports the reception of navigational warnings, meteorological warnings, forecasts, and other safety information.

Search and Rescue Support

Communication and locating equipment support search and rescue coordination and the location of vessels or survival craft.

General Communication

Supports authorized operational and other communication services in addition to distress and safety functions.

Regulatory distinction: GMDSS is a coordinated safety system, not simply another name for satellite internet or ordinary commercial communication. Its equipment and functional requirements are governed by international maritime regulations.
05 / SATELLITE COMMUNICATION

Satellite Communication at Sea

Satellite communication allows ships to exchange information with remote facilities beyond the practical coverage of many terrestrial radio services.

Depending on the satellite service and terminal, applications may include safety communication, operational data exchange, voice services, internet connectivity, and remote support.

Geostationary Satellites (GEO)

GEO satellites orbit above the equator with an orbital period matching Earth's rotation. They appear nearly stationary relative to a point on Earth, which can support continuous regional coverage.

Low Earth Orbit (LEO)

LEO satellites operate at much lower altitudes than GEO satellites. They move relative to the Earth's surface and may require a constellation and satellite handover to provide continuous service.

Satellite Terminals

Shipboard terminals establish communication links with satellite networks. Antenna design, visibility, equipment configuration, and service availability affect performance.

Coverage and Latency

Satellite coverage and delay depend on orbital characteristics, network design, service provider, and geographical conditions.

Engineering consideration: A satellite connection does not guarantee identical performance everywhere. Coverage gaps, antenna obstruction, weather effects on certain frequency bands, network congestion, and service limitations can affect connectivity.
06 / VESSEL INFORMATION EXCHANGE

Automatic Identification System (AIS)

AIS is a maritime radio communication system used to exchange vessel-related information between equipped ships and, where available, shore-based stations.

AIS uses VHF radio channels and a time-division multiple access mechanism to organize transmissions. The information exchanged can include vessel identity, position, course, speed, and other voyage-related data.

Static Information

Examples include vessel identity, name, and other relatively stable ship information.

Dynamic Information

Examples include position, course over ground, speed over ground, and heading, depending on the available equipment inputs.

Voyage-Related Information

Information such as destination and estimated time of arrival may be entered by the crew and can become outdated if not maintained.

AIS Limitations

AIS information can be missing, inaccurate, delayed, incorrectly entered, or unavailable. It should not be treated as a replacement for radar, visual lookout, or professional navigational judgment.

Safety principle: AIS is an aid to situational awareness. A vessel should not rely on AIS alone to assess collision risk or determine the complete traffic situation.
07 / ONBOARD NETWORKS

Shipboard Digital Communication Networks

Modern ships use internal digital networks to transfer information between navigation equipment, monitoring systems, automation platforms, and other connected devices.

Serial Communication

Serial interfaces transfer data sequentially. They are used in several maritime equipment arrangements and may follow specific IEC 61162 interface requirements.

Ethernet Networks

Ethernet supports higher-capacity digital communication between connected systems. IEC 61162-450:2024 provides a framework for high-speed data transfer between relevant shipboard equipment.

Network Switches

Network switches connect devices within a local network and forward traffic according to network addressing and switching behavior.

Gateways

Gateways connect different networks or communication environments and may perform protocol conversion or controlled data forwarding.

Important distinction: A communication interface defines how devices exchange information. It does not automatically guarantee that the receiving application interprets every message correctly or that the network is secure.
08 / COMMUNICATION WORKFLOW

How Does a Maritime Communication Link Work?

A simplified communication process can be represented by four stages. The exact architecture depends on whether the communication is radio-based, satellite-based, or internal to the vessel.

STAGE 01

Information Source

A device, operator, or application creates the information to be transmitted.

STAGE 02

Transmission

The communication system encodes and sends information through a suitable communication medium.

STAGE 03

Reception

The receiving equipment detects and processes the incoming signal or digital message.

STAGE 04

Interpretation

The receiving application interprets the information and makes it available for its intended purpose.

09 / NETWORK PERFORMANCE

Factors Affecting Communication Performance

Coverage

The geographical area in which a communication service can be received or transmitted reliably.

Latency

The time required for information to travel through a communication system. It matters for applications that require timely responses.

Bandwidth

The capacity available for transmitting information. Applications with large data volumes may require higher capacity.

Reliability

The ability of a communication service to perform consistently under its intended operating conditions.

Interference

Unwanted signals or electromagnetic conditions can reduce communication quality or disrupt reception.

Redundancy

Alternative equipment or communication paths can improve resilience when a primary component or link fails.

10 / NETWORK SECURITY

Cybersecurity in Maritime Communication

Communication networks connect equipment, operational systems, and external services. This connectivity creates security risks that must be considered during system design and operation.

Unauthorized Access

Attackers may attempt to access network devices, management interfaces, or connected systems without authorization.

Message Manipulation

Altered or misleading information can affect the integrity of data exchanged between systems.

Denial of Service

Excessive traffic or malicious activity may reduce the availability of communication services.

Network Segmentation

Separating networks according to their operational functions and security requirements can limit the spread of an incident.

  • Use access controls and strong authentication where supported.
  • Separate operational technology and business networks appropriately.
  • Monitor network traffic and investigate unusual communication patterns.
  • Maintain secure configurations for network devices.
  • Control remote access and verify authorized service providers.
  • Maintain incident response and recovery procedures.
  • Consider communication availability and integrity in safety planning.
11 / TECHNICAL CHALLENGES

Challenges in Maritime Communication Networks

Remote Operating Environments

Ships may operate far from terrestrial infrastructure, making communication dependent on radio and satellite services.

Environmental Conditions

Weather, antenna obstruction, sea conditions, and electromagnetic interference may affect communication performance.

Equipment Compatibility

Different manufacturers and equipment generations may use different interfaces, message formats, and network configurations.

Network Availability

Equipment faults, power interruptions, service outages, or physical damage may interrupt communication.

Cybersecurity Exposure

Connected systems may introduce additional attack paths if access, configuration, and network boundaries are not managed.

Regulatory Compliance

Ships must consider applicable international requirements, equipment approvals, operating procedures, and radio regulations.

12 / FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

The Future of Maritime Communication

Hybrid Connectivity

Combining different communication services may improve connectivity options as vessels move between coverage areas and operating environments.

Advanced Satellite Networks

Evolving satellite constellations and services may expand connectivity options and support new maritime applications.

Digital Maritime Services

Continued e-navigation development aims to harmonize the collection, integration, exchange, presentation, and analysis of marine information.

Cyber-Resilient Networks

Security-aware network architectures, monitoring, and recovery capabilities will remain important as connectivity expands.
