AUTONOMOUS SHIPS / TOPIC 03

Autonomous Ship Architecture

Discover the technical structure that enables an autonomous vessel to perceive its environment, process information, make operational decisions, communicate with external systems, and control its physical equipment.

SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE INTEGRATED TECHNOLOGIES 10 QUESTIONS
01 / Introduction

What Is Autonomous Ship Architecture?

Autonomous ship architecture refers to the organized structure of hardware, software, sensors, communication networks, control systems, and human interfaces that work together to enable a vessel to perform selected functions with reduced or no continuous human interaction.

An autonomous ship is not simply a conventional vessel with an autopilot or a collection of advanced sensors. It requires several interconnected systems that can collect information, understand the operating situation, evaluate possible actions, and execute suitable commands.

These systems must also communicate with one another, monitor their own condition, detect failures, and support safe operation under changing maritime conditions.

Core concept: Autonomy emerges from the integration of multiple systems. A highly capable sensor, navigation computer, or AI model alone cannot make a vessel safely autonomous.

Why Is Architecture Important?

A clear architecture defines how information moves through the vessel, how different systems interact, which system has authority over a particular function, and what happens when a component fails.

System Integration

Connects navigation, propulsion, communication, monitoring, and decision-making systems into a coordinated operating environment.

Operational Reliability

Helps identify dependencies, prevent conflicting commands, and establish backup arrangements for critical functions.

Safety Management

Supports monitoring, fault detection, emergency responses, and defined operating limits.

Scalability

Allows new sensors, software, communication technologies, and control capabilities to be integrated through a planned system design.

An autonomous vessel can be understood through a functional architecture. The following model is a simplified educational representation, not a single mandatory architecture prescribed for every ship.

03 / Sensing Layer

Sensors and Data Acquisition

Sensors provide the connection between the physical maritime environment and the vessel's digital systems. They collect measurements about the ship's position, movement, surrounding traffic, obstacles, weather, and machinery condition.

Sensor data is the foundation of situational awareness. If the input data is inaccurate, delayed, incomplete, or unavailable, later processing and decision-making may also become unreliable.

SENSOR 01

Radar

Detects and tracks surrounding objects by transmitting radio waves and analyzing reflected signals. It supports traffic monitoring and collision-risk assessment.

SENSOR 02

GNSS

Global Navigation Satellite Systems provide position and timing information using satellite signals. GNSS supports vessel localization and route monitoring.

SENSOR 03

AIS

The Automatic Identification System exchanges vessel information such as identity, position, course, and speed with other equipped stations.

SENSOR 04

Cameras

Optical and infrared cameras provide visual information that can support object recognition, situational awareness, and monitoring.

SENSOR 05

LiDAR

Light Detection and Ranging measures distances using laser pulses. It can generate detailed spatial information about nearby objects.

SENSOR 06

IMU

An Inertial Measurement Unit measures motion and orientation-related quantities, commonly including angular rates and acceleration.

SENSOR 07

Sonar and Echo Sounders

Use acoustic signals to provide information about underwater objects, water depth, and the seabed.

SENSOR 08

Environmental and Machinery Sensors

Monitor parameters such as temperature, pressure, vibration, fuel condition, humidity, and equipment performance.

Why Multiple Sensors Are Necessary

Different sensors have different strengths and limitations. Radar can provide useful information in darkness, while cameras can provide visual details. GNSS supports global positioning, whereas IMU data helps estimate short-term motion.

Combining complementary sources can improve the quality of the vessel's understanding of its surroundings. However, combining sensors does not automatically guarantee accurate information.

Example: If GNSS positioning becomes unreliable, an autonomous navigation system may need to evaluate other available information sources and determine whether it can continue safely.

04 / Perception Layer

Data Processing and Sensor Fusion

Sensors produce raw measurements, not a complete understanding of the maritime environment. The perception layer processes these measurements and transforms them into useful information.

What Happens During Data Processing?

  • Filtering: Reduces noise and unwanted measurement variations.
  • Time synchronization: Aligns measurements collected at different times.
  • Coordinate transformation: Converts information into compatible reference frames.
  • Object detection: Identifies relevant objects such as vessels, buoys, and obstacles.
  • Tracking: Estimates the movement of detected objects over time.
  • Sensor fusion: Combines information from multiple sources to produce a more consistent representation.

Understanding Sensor Fusion

Sensor fusion is the process of combining information from different sensors to improve estimates of the vessel's state and surrounding environment.

For example, radar may detect a nearby vessel, AIS may provide reported identification and movement information, and a camera may provide visual evidence. The system can compare these sources to build a more complete picture.

Critical limitation: AIS information is not a guaranteed representation of reality. It may be missing, delayed, inaccurate, or unavailable. An autonomous vessel must not depend on AIS alone for collision avoidance.

05 / Intelligence Layer

Decision-Making and Mission Planning

The decision-making layer evaluates the information generated by perception systems and determines what the vessel should do next.

It considers the planned mission, navigational constraints, nearby traffic, environmental conditions, vessel capabilities, and safety requirements.

Mission Planning

Defines the vessel's operational objectives, planned route, destination, and task sequence.

Route Planning

Selects a suitable route based on navigational constraints, weather, traffic, and operational goals.

Collision Avoidance

Evaluates potential encounters and selects suitable actions while considering applicable collision-prevention requirements.

Predictive Analysis

Estimates possible future positions, trajectories, and operational conditions to support safer decisions.

The Role of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence and machine learning can support perception, classification, prediction, anomaly detection, and decision-support functions. Their role depends on the system design and the operational task.

AI is not the entire autonomous architecture. It depends on sensors, reliable data, computing resources, navigation systems, control equipment, and safety mechanisms.

Design principle: A decision-making system must consider not only what action is desirable, but also whether that action is safe, feasible, and permitted under the vessel's operating conditions.

06 / Navigation and Control

Guidance, Navigation and Control (GNC)

Guidance, Navigation and Control is a fundamental engineering function in autonomous vessels. It connects the intended movement of the ship with the commands required to achieve that movement.

G — GUIDANCE

Guidance

Determines the desired path, heading, speed, or trajectory that the vessel should follow to complete its mission.

N — NAVIGATION

Navigation

Estimates the vessel's position, orientation, and movement relative to a defined reference frame.

C — CONTROL

Control

Calculates the commands needed to move the vessel toward its desired state while accounting for system dynamics and disturbances.

FEEDBACK

Continuous Correction

Compares actual vessel behavior with the desired behavior and adjusts commands when necessary.

Example: Maintaining a Planned Course

The guidance function provides a desired route. The navigation function estimates the vessel's current position and heading. The control function calculates steering and propulsion commands to reduce the difference between the desired and actual movement.

Feedback from sensors and vessel systems allows the controller to adjust its commands as wind, waves, currents, and other disturbances affect the ship.

Important distinction: Guidance decides where the vessel should go. Navigation estimates where it is. Control determines how to make it move toward the desired state.

07 / Physical Execution

Actuation and Shipboard Equipment

The actuation layer converts control commands into physical actions. It includes the equipment that directly affects the vessel's movement and operation.

Propulsion Systems

Electric motors, diesel engines, propellers, and other propulsion arrangements generate the thrust required to move the vessel.

Steering Systems

Rudders and steering machinery change the vessel's direction according to control commands.

Thrusters

Bow and stern thrusters provide additional maneuvering capability, particularly during low-speed operations and docking.

Machinery Systems

Engine, electrical, cooling, and auxiliary equipment support the vessel's operation and may be monitored or controlled automatically.

Why Actuator Feedback Matters

Sending a command does not guarantee that the equipment has executed it correctly. The system must monitor the actual response and compare it with the requested action.

For example, if a steering command is issued but the rudder does not reach the expected angle, the control system should detect the mismatch and initiate an appropriate response.

08 / Communication Layer

Communication and Remote Operations

Autonomous ships may exchange information with shore-based operating centres, other vessels, ports, traffic services, and external data providers. Communication supports remote monitoring, operational coordination, and selected remote control functions.

Satellite Communication

Supports long-distance data exchange between ships and shore facilities, including operations outside coastal communication coverage.

Radio Communication

Supports maritime voice and data communication, including operational coordination and safety-related exchanges.

Cellular Networks

May provide high-data-rate connectivity in areas where suitable terrestrial network coverage is available.

Remote Operations Centre

A shore-based facility where trained personnel can monitor vessel status, support decisions, or perform authorized remote-control functions.

What Information May Be Transmitted?

  • Vessel position, heading, and speed.
  • Navigation and surrounding traffic information.
  • Machinery condition and equipment alarms.
  • System health and diagnostic information.
  • Mission status and operational reports.
  • Authorized commands and control instructions.

Communication failure: An autonomous ship must have a defined response when communication is interrupted. Depending on its approved design and operating mode, it may need to maintain a safe condition, use local control capabilities, change its operating mode, or request assistance.

09 / Safety and Cybersecurity

Safety, Redundancy and Cybersecurity Architecture

An autonomous ship depends on interconnected electronic and software-based systems. A failure in one component may affect several other functions if dependencies are not properly managed.

Redundancy and Fault Tolerance

Redundancy means providing additional components or alternative capabilities so that a critical function can remain available when a primary component fails.

Hardware Redundancy

Alternative computers, sensors, power supplies, or communication equipment may support continuity of critical functions.

Software Monitoring

Health checks and diagnostic functions help identify abnormal software behavior and system faults.

Fail-Safe Design

Systems are designed to move toward a defined safe condition when specified failures occur.

Cybersecurity Protection

Security measures protect systems, communication links, software, and operational data against unauthorized access and malicious interference.

Examples of Cybersecurity Risks

  • Unauthorized access to remote-control interfaces.
  • Malware affecting shipboard computers.
  • Manipulation or disruption of navigation data.
  • Denial-of-service attacks against communication networks.
  • Compromised software updates or external connections.

Cybersecurity is not separate from operational safety. If an attacker compromises a navigation or control system, the result may affect the vessel's physical operation.

Engineering principle: Safety and cybersecurity should be considered during system design, integration, testing, operation, maintenance, and software updates—not added only after the system has been built.

10 / System Integration

How All Components Work Together

The different architectural layers continuously exchange information. Consider a vessel detecting another ship crossing its planned route.

01

Detection

Radar and other available sensors detect an object in the vessel's surrounding environment.

02

Data Processing

The system filters and processes measurements, associates relevant detections, and estimates object movement.

03

Situation Assessment

The system evaluates the relative movement of the vessels and estimates whether a dangerous encounter may develop.

04

Decision and Planning

A suitable maneuver is evaluated while considering navigational rules, surrounding traffic, and vessel limitations.

05

Control Command

The control system calculates the steering and propulsion commands needed to perform the selected maneuver.

06

Physical Execution

The steering and propulsion equipment responds to the commands, changing the vessel's movement.

07

Feedback and Verification

Sensors monitor the vessel's actual response. The system checks whether the maneuver is progressing as expected and updates its calculations.

Important: This is a simplified engineering example. Actual collision avoidance must comply with applicable navigation rules, including COLREGs, and must account for the vessel's approved operational capabilities. A software-generated maneuver is not automatically a safe or legally compliant maneuver.

11 / Engineering Challenges

Challenges in Designing Autonomous Ship Architecture

System Complexity

Integrating multiple systems from different manufacturers can create compatibility, timing, and coordination problems.

Environmental Uncertainty

Fog, heavy rain, waves, reflections, sea spray, and poor visibility may reduce sensor performance.

Data Quality

Incorrect, delayed, inconsistent, or missing information can affect the vessel's understanding of its situation.

Communication Limitations

Satellite latency, coverage gaps, interference, and network failures may affect remote operations.

Cybersecurity Threats

Connected systems introduce attack surfaces that require continuous protection and monitoring.

Testing and Validation

Complex autonomous functions must be evaluated under normal, abnormal, and emergency operating conditions.

12 / Regulatory Context

Architecture and the IMO MASS Code

The International Maritime Organization's MASS Code provides a regulatory framework for the design, approval, and operation of Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships within its scope.

The non-mandatory International Code of Safety for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships was adopted in May 2026 and came into effect on 1 July 2026. It applies to cargo ships covered by SOLAS Chapter I, with recommendations concerning smaller vessels.

Its technical and operational areas include system design, software principles, risk assessment, connectivity, remote operations, navigation safety, machinery, electrical installations, and human oversight.

The Code emphasizes that a vessel's different modes of operation must be described. This makes system architecture important for understanding which functions are performed onboard, remotely, or autonomously.

Regulatory insight: A technically advanced architecture does not automatically make a ship approved for autonomous operation. The vessel must satisfy the applicable approval, safety, operational, and certification requirements.

13 / Key Takeaways

What You Should Remember

  • Autonomous ship architecture is the organized integration of hardware, software, sensors, communication, and control systems.
  • Sensors collect information about the vessel and its surrounding environment.
  • Perception and sensor fusion transform raw measurements into useful situational information.
  • Decision-making systems evaluate information and select suitable operational actions.
  • Guidance, Navigation and Control connect planned movement with physical execution.
  • Actuators convert control commands into physical actions.
  • Communication enables information exchange with shore-based centres and external systems.
  • Redundancy, fault tolerance, and cybersecurity are essential architectural considerations.
  • All components must work together through continuous feedback and monitoring.
  • Regulatory approval and operational safety cannot be replaced by technological capability alone.
