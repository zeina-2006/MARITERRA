AUTONOMOUS SHIPS — TOPIC 08

Safety, Cybersecurity and Maritime Regulations

Explore the engineering principles, risk management methods, cybersecurity controls, emergency procedures, human responsibilities, and international regulatory frameworks required for safe autonomous ship operations.

Maritime Safety Cybersecurity Risk Engineering International Regulations

1. Introduction to Autonomous Ship Safety

Safety is one of the most important requirements in autonomous maritime operations. An autonomous ship may use sensors, artificial intelligence, navigation software, communication networks, and automated control systems to perform functions traditionally carried out by onboard personnel.

These technologies create new operational capabilities, but they also introduce risks involving software failures, incorrect sensor data, communication interruptions, cyberattacks, equipment faults, and unexpected environmental conditions.

Safety engineering aims to identify these risks, reduce their likelihood where possible, limit their consequences, and ensure that the vessel can respond appropriately when something goes wrong.

Operational Safety

Protects the vessel, people, cargo, and environment during normal and abnormal operations.

System Safety

Examines hardware, software, sensors, control systems, and their interactions to identify dangerous failures.

Cybersecurity

Protects digital systems, communication networks, and operational technology against cyber threats.

Key principle: Automation does not automatically make a vessel safer. Safety depends on system design, operational procedures, testing, human oversight, maintenance, and the ability to manage failures.

2. Understanding Maritime Hazards and Risks

A hazard is a condition or situation that has the potential to cause harm. Risk considers both the possibility of a harmful event and the severity of its consequences.

2.1 Common Hazards in Autonomous Ships

  • Navigation Hazards: Incorrect position estimates, unsafe routes, or failure to detect nearby vessels.
  • Sensor Hazards: Sensor failure, incorrect readings, obstruction, or inconsistent measurements.
  • Control Hazards: Unintended steering commands, propulsion failures, or unstable control behaviour.
  • Communication Hazards: Loss of connection, excessive latency, or unauthorised commands.
  • Software Hazards: Programming errors, incorrect decisions, or unexpected interactions between software components.
  • Machinery Hazards: Engine failure, electrical faults, overheating, or propulsion-system damage.
  • Environmental Hazards: Severe weather, waves, currents, restricted visibility, and ice where relevant.
  • Human-Related Hazards: Incorrect configuration, operator error, inadequate training, or delayed intervention.

2.2 Risk Assessment

Risk assessment is a structured process used to understand what can go wrong, how likely it may be, and what consequences could result.

A typical process includes:

02Analyse Risk
03Evaluate Risk
04Apply Controls
05Review and Monitor

2.3 Basic Risk Matrix

A risk matrix can help organise risks according to their likelihood and severity. It is a screening and prioritisation tool, not a complete substitute for engineering analysis.

Likely Major High

2.4 Risk Estimation

A simplified risk-scoring approach can multiply a likelihood rating by a severity rating.

Risk Score = Likelihood × Severity

For example, if a project uses a five-level likelihood scale and a five-level severity scale, a hazard rated 4 for likelihood and 5 for severity receives a score of 20.

Risk scores are not universal physical measurements. Different organisations use different scales and acceptance criteria. A low numerical score does not automatically prove that a safety-critical risk is acceptable.

3. Safety Engineering and Risk Reduction

Safety engineering focuses on preventing dangerous conditions and ensuring that systems behave safely when faults occur. It considers the complete vessel lifecycle, including design, construction, commissioning, operation, maintenance, and decommissioning.

3.1 Hazard Identification (HAZID)

HAZID is a structured method for identifying hazards early in a project. Engineers examine the vessel, its systems, operating environment, and possible abnormal situations.

For an autonomous ship, a HAZID workshop may examine sensor failures, navigation errors, loss of communication, unexpected human intervention, and machinery problems.

3.2 Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA)

FMEA examines how individual components or functions may fail and how those failures could affect the larger system.

For example:

Component Possible Failure Potential Effect Possible Control
GNSS Receiver Position signal unavailable Reduced positioning accuracy Alternative navigation sources
Radar Target detection failure Reduced awareness of nearby traffic Sensor redundancy and fault monitoring
Rudder System Actuator malfunction Loss of normal steering response Fault detection and contingency control
Communication Link Connection interruption Loss of shore supervision Alternative links and onboard procedures

3.3 Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)

Fault Tree Analysis works backwards from an unwanted event to identify combinations of failures that could cause it.

For example, engineers may investigate the possible causes of a loss of steering capability, including power failure, control-system failure, communication faults, or mechanical damage.

3.4 Safety Barriers

Safety barriers are measures designed to prevent a hazard from developing into an accident or to reduce the consequences if an accident occurs.

  • Preventive barriers reduce the probability of dangerous events.
  • Detective barriers identify abnormal conditions early.
  • Mitigative barriers reduce the consequences of an incident.
  • Recovery measures support restoration to a safe operating condition.

3.5 Defence in Depth

Defence in depth uses multiple independent or complementary safety measures. If one protection fails, other protections may still reduce the likelihood or consequences of an accident.

A safety-critical function should not depend on one unverified sensor, one communication link, or one software component when the consequences of failure could be severe.

4. Redundancy, Fault Tolerance and Fail-Safe Design

Autonomous vessels depend on many interconnected systems. A failure in one important component can affect navigation, propulsion, communication, or safety monitoring.

4.1 Redundancy

Redundancy means providing additional components or resources so that a system can continue performing a required function when a primary component becomes unavailable.

Examples include:

  • Multiple navigation sensors.
  • Backup electrical power sources.
  • Alternative communication links.
  • Redundant control computers.
  • Independent monitoring and alarm functions.

4.2 Fault Tolerance

Fault tolerance is the ability of a system to continue providing an acceptable level of service despite certain faults.

For example, if one navigation sensor fails, a system may use other validated sources to maintain a position estimate. This is only appropriate if the remaining information is sufficiently reliable for the current operation.

4.3 Fail-Safe Design

Fail-safe design aims to bring a system into, or maintain it in, a defined safe condition when a dangerous failure occurs.

The safe condition depends on the function and operating environment. A propulsion shutdown, for example, might reduce one machinery hazard but create a navigation hazard in a busy channel.

4.4 Fail-Operational Design

Fail-operational systems are designed to continue performing essential functions after specified failures. They may use redundancy, reconfiguration, or alternative control paths.

"Fail-safe" does not mean that a vessel should always stop, shut down, or disconnect. The safe response must be determined through vessel-specific risk assessment and operational analysis.

5. Emergency Response and Contingency Planning

Emergency planning prepares a vessel and its operators to respond to unexpected events that may threaten people, the ship, cargo, or the marine environment.

5.1 Possible Emergency Situations

  • Collision or grounding risk.
  • Fire or smoke detection.
  • Flooding or loss of watertight integrity.
  • Loss of propulsion or steering.
  • Electrical power failure.
  • Critical sensor malfunction.
  • Loss of communication with a shore centre.
  • Cyberattack affecting operational systems.
  • Severe weather beyond approved operating limits.

5.2 Emergency Response Process

01Detect Event
02Assess Situation
03Activate Response
04Protect and Recover
05Report and Review

5.3 Emergency Procedures

A contingency plan should identify the event, the required response, the responsible person or system, the communication procedure, and the conditions for returning to normal operation.

Procedures should be specific enough to guide action but flexible enough to account for different operating environments.

5.4 Search and Rescue

Autonomous and remotely operated vessels must be considered within the wider maritime search-and-rescue framework. Emergency arrangements may involve coastal authorities, rescue coordination centres, nearby vessels, and other relevant organisations.

5.5 Incident Reporting and Investigation

After an incident, operational data, system logs, sensor records, and communication history may help investigators understand what happened and identify contributing factors.

The objective is not only to identify an immediate technical fault but also to understand design, operational, organisational, and human factors that contributed to the event.

6. Cybersecurity in Autonomous Ships

Autonomous ships depend on digital technologies, software, communication networks, sensors, and connected operational systems. These technologies create potential entry points for cyber threats.

Maritime cybersecurity protects the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information and systems that support safe vessel operations.

6.1 Information Technology (IT) and Operational Technology (OT)

Information Technology systems generally process, store, and communicate information. Operational Technology systems monitor or control physical equipment and industrial processes.

IT Systems OT Systems
Email and office networks Propulsion control
Business databases Steering control systems
Administrative applications Machinery automation
Document management Industrial sensors and controllers

The distinction is important because a cyber incident affecting an operational control system may have direct physical consequences for the vessel.

6.2 Common Maritime Cyber Threats

  • Malware: Malicious software that can damage, disrupt, or compromise systems.
  • Ransomware: Malware that blocks access to systems or data until a demand is made.
  • Phishing: Deceptive messages designed to steal credentials or deliver malicious content.
  • Unauthorised Access: Access to systems by individuals without valid permission.
  • Denial of Service: Attacks that make communication or digital services unavailable.
  • Data Manipulation: Unauthorised alteration of information used by navigation or control systems.
  • GNSS Spoofing: Transmission of deceptive satellite-navigation signals that may cause incorrect position or timing information.
  • GNSS Jamming: Interference that prevents or degrades reception of satellite-navigation signals.
  • Supply Chain Attacks: Compromise through software, hardware, or service providers.

6.3 Cybersecurity Protection Measures

  • Multi-factor authentication for sensitive access.
  • Role-based access control and least privilege.
  • Network segmentation between IT and OT environments.
  • Encryption for sensitive data and communication.
  • Secure configuration and software-update procedures.
  • Continuous monitoring and security logging.
  • Vulnerability management and controlled patching.
  • Backup systems and tested recovery procedures.
  • Security awareness training for personnel.
  • Incident response plans and regular exercises.

6.4 Network Segmentation

Network segmentation divides a network into separate zones according to function and security requirements. It can help limit the spread of an attack from an office network to safety-critical operational systems.

6.5 Access Control

Access control determines who can use a system and what actions they are permitted to perform. Remote access to ship systems should be restricted, authenticated, authorised, monitored, and reviewed.

Cybersecurity is not achieved by installing antivirus software alone. It requires technical controls, secure system design, operational procedures, personnel training, monitoring, and incident recovery.

7. Artificial Intelligence Safety and Software Assurance

Autonomous ships may use AI and machine-learning systems for object detection, route planning, decision support, anomaly detection, and other functions.

However, AI systems can make incorrect predictions or behave unexpectedly when they encounter situations that differ from their training or testing conditions.

7.1 AI-Related Safety Risks

  • Incorrect object detection or classification.
  • Unreliable predictions in unfamiliar environments.
  • Biased or incomplete training data.
  • Unexpected behaviour after software updates.
  • Difficulty explaining certain model decisions.
  • Adversarial inputs or manipulated sensor data.
  • Overdependence on automated recommendations.

7.2 Verification and Validation

Verification checks whether a system has been developed according to its specified requirements. Validation checks whether the system performs its intended function in the intended operational context.

Verification Validation
Checks implementation against specifications Checks suitability for intended operation
May include code reviews and software tests May include realistic operational trials
Examines whether the system was built correctly Examines whether the correct system was built

7.3 Testing Methods

  • Simulation: Tests system behaviour in virtual environments.
  • Software Testing: Checks software functions and expected outputs.
  • Hardware-in-the-Loop: Connects real hardware to a simulated environment.
  • Integration Testing: Evaluates communication and interaction between subsystems.
  • Sea Trials: Evaluate vessel behaviour under controlled real-world conditions.
  • Operational Monitoring: Reviews system performance after deployment.

7.4 Software Updates and Change Management

Software updates may change how an autonomous vessel detects objects, plans routes, or controls equipment. Updates should therefore be reviewed, tested, authorised, documented, and installed according to controlled procedures.

Passing a software test does not prove that an AI system will behave correctly in every possible maritime situation. Testing must be supported by operational limits, monitoring, safety barriers, and appropriate human oversight.

8. Human Oversight and Responsibility

Autonomous technology changes the way maritime work is performed, but it does not remove the importance of human responsibility. People may remain involved in planning, monitoring, supervision, remote operation, maintenance, and emergency response.

8.1 Human Oversight

Human oversight means that qualified personnel can monitor system performance, understand important decisions, and intervene when required by the operational arrangement.

8.2 Remote Operators

Remote operators may supervise vessel functions or control selected operations from a shore facility. Their responsibilities, competence requirements, and authority should be clearly defined.

8.3 The Role of the Master

The role and responsibilities of the master must be addressed within the applicable legal and operational framework. Remote operation does not automatically eliminate the master's responsibilities or transfer them to an automated system.

8.4 Human Factors

  • Situational awareness.
  • Operator workload and fatigue.
  • Alarm prioritisation and alarm fatigue.
  • Training and professional competence.
  • Clear communication and control handover.
  • Understanding automation limitations.

8.5 Control Handover

When control transfers between an onboard system, an onboard operator, or a shore-based operator, the transition must be clearly managed. The system should confirm who has authority and prevent conflicting commands.

Human oversight is effective only when operators have sufficient information, appropriate training, enough time to respond, and clear authority to take action.

9. International Maritime Regulatory Framework

Autonomous ships operate within a regulatory environment that includes international conventions, codes, national laws, flag-state requirements, coastal-state rules, and applicable technical standards.

9.1 International Maritime Organization (IMO)

The International Maritime Organization is a specialised United Nations agency responsible for developing international standards and conventions related to maritime safety, security, environmental protection, and shipping operations.

9.2 International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS)

SOLAS is one of the most important international maritime safety conventions. It establishes minimum requirements for the construction, equipment, and operation of ships covered by its provisions.

Autonomous ship frameworks must be considered alongside SOLAS and other applicable instruments. The introduction of automation does not automatically remove a vessel's existing legal obligations.

9.3 International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs)

COLREGs establish internationally recognised rules designed to prevent collisions between vessels. They address matters such as lookout, safe speed, risk of collision, conduct in different encounter situations, lights, shapes, and sound signals.

Autonomous navigation systems must be designed to support compliance with applicable collision-prevention requirements. Automated decision-making does not create permission to ignore these rules.

9.4 International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping (STCW)

STCW establishes international minimum standards for training, certification, and watchkeeping for seafarers covered by its provisions.

Autonomous and remote operations raise additional questions about the competence, training, and responsibilities of personnel performing functions from shore.

9.5 International Safety Management (ISM) Code

The ISM Code provides an international standard for the safe management and operation of ships and for pollution prevention. It requires companies to establish and maintain a Safety Management System.

Risk management, emergency preparedness, maintenance, reporting, and continuous improvement are important elements of effective safety management.

9.6 International Ship and Port Facility Security (ISPS) Code

The ISPS Code establishes a framework for ship and port facility security within its scope. It supports the identification and management of security threats and the implementation of appropriate security measures.

9.7 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS)

UNCLOS establishes a broad international legal framework governing the use of the oceans and seas. It addresses maritime zones, navigation rights, duties of states, and other important aspects of the law of the sea.

These instruments have different legal scopes and applicability. A vessel's regulatory obligations depend on its type, size, voyage, flag, operating area, and the relevant national implementation.

10. The IMO MASS Code

The International Maritime Organization developed a specific framework to address the challenges created by Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS).

10.1 What Is MASS?

MASS stands for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ship. It refers to a ship that can operate independently of human interaction to varying degrees, according to its operational design and level of autonomy.

A vessel is not automatically classified as a MASS simply because it uses advanced automation. The IMO distinguishes between ordinary enhanced automation and systems that replace or support functions normally carried out by onboard crew.

10.2 Adoption of the MASS Code

The IMO adopted the non-mandatory International Code of Safety for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS Code) through resolution MSC.595(111) in May 2026.

The Code came into effect on 1 July 2026 as a non-mandatory international framework. It supports the safe integration of remotely controlled and autonomous commercial ships into international shipping.

10.3 Scope of the Code

The Code applies to cargo ships covered by SOLAS Chapter I, generally ships of 500 gross tonnage and above on international voyages. Governments are encouraged to apply it, as far as practicable, to smaller MASS vessels as well.

The Code is designed to supplement SOLAS and other applicable mandatory IMO instruments rather than replace them.

10.4 Main Areas Addressed

  • Risk assessment and approval arrangements.
  • System design and software principles.
  • Safe management of autonomous operations.
  • Alert management and human oversight.
  • Manning, training, and watchkeeping considerations.
  • Safety of navigation.
  • Connectivity and remote operations.
  • Fire safety and machinery-related requirements.
  • Search and rescue considerations.
  • Security and other ship safety functions.

10.5 Experience-Building Phase and Future Development

The non-mandatory Code provides an opportunity for governments and industry to gain experience and identify areas that may require further development.

The IMO roadmap anticipates development of a mandatory MASS Code in 2028, with expected adoption by 1 July 2030 and entry into force on 1 January 2032. These are planned milestones and may be affected by future regulatory decisions.

Important regulatory distinction: As of November 2026, the MASS Code is non-mandatory. Its entry into effect does not mean that it has already become a universally binding mandatory SOLAS code. Applicable mandatory conventions and national requirements continue to matter.

11. Certification, Approval and Operational Documentation

Before an autonomous ship can operate under an applicable regulatory framework, its design and operational arrangements may need to undergo assessment, approval, inspection, and certification.

11.1 Design Review

Design review examines whether the vessel's architecture, equipment, software, safety systems, and operational concept meet applicable requirements.

11.2 Risk Assessment Documentation

Risk assessment records should identify hazards, evaluate consequences, document protective measures, and explain how remaining risks are managed.

11.3 Operational Concept

An operational concept describes how the vessel is intended to work. It may identify autonomy functions, remote control arrangements, operating areas, restrictions, human responsibilities, and contingency procedures.

11.4 Testing and Trials

Testing may include simulation, equipment testing, software verification, integration testing, and controlled sea trials. The test programme should provide evidence that the system performs as intended under its defined operating conditions.

11.5 Certificates and Approvals

Required certificates and approvals depend on the applicable regulatory framework, ship type, flag, voyage, and operational design. The IMO MASS framework includes an approval process and a MASS Safety Certificate for vessels within its applicable scope.

Certification is not a permanent guarantee that a vessel will remain safe under every condition. Systems must be maintained, monitored, reassessed, and operated within their approved limits.

12. Environmental Protection and Maritime Security

Autonomous ship safety also includes protecting the marine environment and preventing security incidents.

12.1 Pollution Prevention

Autonomous systems must support safe operation of machinery, fuel systems, cargo systems, and other equipment in ways that comply with applicable environmental requirements.

12.2 MARPOL

The International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL) is the principal international convention addressing pollution from ships caused by operational or accidental activities within its scope.

12.3 Environmental Monitoring

Sensors and automated monitoring systems may help detect abnormal equipment conditions, monitor selected emissions or discharges, and support environmental reporting.

12.4 Maritime Security

Maritime security involves protecting vessels, people, cargo, ports, and maritime infrastructure from unlawful acts and other security threats.

Autonomous ships must consider physical security, cyber threats, unauthorised access, and the protection of sensitive operational systems.

13. Practical Engineering Scenario

Consider an autonomous cargo vessel operating on an international voyage. During navigation, its primary positioning system begins providing inconsistent data. At the same time, the vessel experiences a temporary communication interruption with its shore control centre.

Step 1 — Fault Detection

The navigation monitoring system identifies a disagreement between the primary positioning source and other available navigation measurements.

Step 2 — Risk Assessment

The onboard safety system evaluates the reliability of the available information and determines whether the vessel remains within its approved operating limits.

Step 3 — Protective Response

The vessel applies its predefined contingency procedure. It does not rely on an unverified position estimate for safety-critical decisions.

Step 4 — Communication Recovery

The communication system attempts to restore the primary connection or use an authorised alternative link. Essential status information is prioritised.

Step 5 — Human Oversight

Once communication becomes available, the shore operator receives updated information and reviews the vessel's status. Control authority is confirmed before any remote command is issued.

Step 6 — Incident Documentation

The system records the sensor disagreement, communication interruption, decisions, alarms, and recovery actions. Engineers can review these records to improve reliability and risk controls.

This scenario shows that safe autonomous operation requires more than a navigation algorithm. It depends on fault detection, independent information sources, contingency planning, cybersecurity, human oversight, and validated system behaviour.

14. Engineering Challenges and Future Developments

Current Challenges

  • Managing software and hardware failures in complex systems.
  • Maintaining reliable navigation in degraded environments.
  • Protecting connected operational systems from cyberattacks.
  • Establishing clear responsibility and control authority.
  • Validating AI behaviour in rare and unexpected situations.
  • Designing effective emergency and communication-loss procedures.
  • Developing consistent international regulatory approaches.
  • Maintaining safety across different vessel types and operating areas.

Future Developments

  • Advanced Safety Monitoring: More capable systems for detecting abnormal behaviour and predicting potential failures.
  • Resilient Navigation: Improved methods for maintaining positioning when satellite signals are degraded or unavailable.
  • AI Assurance: Better methods for evaluating the reliability, limitations, and safety of AI-based functions.
  • Cyber Resilience: Stronger protection, monitoring, and recovery capabilities for maritime operational technology.
  • Digital Twins: More advanced virtual models to support testing, risk assessment, and maintenance.
  • Regulatory Development: Continued international work to adapt maritime rules to autonomous and remote operations.

The future of autonomous shipping will depend not only on making ships more intelligent, but also on proving that their systems are dependable, secure, understandable, and safe throughout their operational lifecycle.

15. Key Takeaways

  • Safety engineering identifies hazards and reduces operational risks.
  • Risk assessment considers likelihood, consequences, and protective measures.
  • HAZID, FMEA, and Fault Tree Analysis support systematic hazard analysis.
  • Redundancy and fault tolerance help manage selected system failures.
  • Fail-safe behaviour must be defined according to the vessel's operating context.
  • Emergency procedures must be tested and appropriate for the vessel.
  • Cybersecurity protects both information and physical operational systems.
  • AI-based functions require verification, validation, monitoring, and safety controls.
  • Human oversight and clearly defined responsibilities remain important.
  • SOLAS, COLREGs, STCW, ISM, ISPS, MARPOL, and UNCLOS have different but relevant regulatory roles.
  • The IMO MASS Code was adopted in May 2026 as a non-mandatory framework effective from 1 July 2026.
  • Certification and operational approval depend on the applicable regulatory requirements.
