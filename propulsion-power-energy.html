01 / FOUNDATIONS

Understanding Marine Propulsion & Energy

Every marine robotic platform needs a reliable way to move and an energy system capable of supporting its mission.

What Are Propulsion, Power & Energy Systems?

Marine propulsion, power, and energy systems are the interconnected engineering components responsible for generating thrust, supplying electricity, distributing power, and maintaining the operation of a marine vehicle.

A propulsion system converts an energy source into mechanical force that moves a vehicle through water. The electrical power system supplies the energy required by propulsion motors, sensors, computers, communication equipment, navigation instruments, and other onboard components.

Their design directly affects vehicle speed, maneuverability, mission duration, payload capacity, reliability, and operational safety.

Engineering distinction: Propulsion is only one consumer of energy. A marine robot also needs electricity for computing, navigation, sensing, communication, thermal management, and control.

02 / FOUNDATIONS

Fundamental Principles of Marine Propulsion

Propulsion systems generate thrust by interacting with surrounding water.

How Does Propulsion Work?

A marine propulsion system generates a force that moves a vehicle through water. In many electrically powered systems, an electric motor rotates a propeller or impeller, accelerating water and producing thrust.

According to Newton's third law, when a propeller accelerates water in one direction, an opposing force acts on the propeller and vehicle.

The amount of thrust depends on several factors, including propeller geometry, rotational speed, water density, inflow velocity, and operating conditions.

Thrust and Resistance

A moving marine vehicle experiences hydrodynamic resistance. This resistance acts opposite to its direction of motion.

During steady movement, the available thrust must balance the total resistance. If thrust is greater than resistance, the vehicle can accelerate.

Net Force = Thrust − Drag Simplified representation of the forces along the direction of motion.

Drag is affected by vehicle shape, surface roughness, speed, water properties, and flow conditions. Increasing speed can significantly increase the power needed to overcome hydrodynamic resistance.

Propeller Fundamentals

A propeller consists of blades attached to a rotating hub. As the blades rotate, they transfer momentum to the surrounding water and generate thrust.

  • Diameter: Influences the volume of water interacting with the propeller.
  • Pitch: Describes the theoretical distance a propeller advances in one revolution under idealized conditions.
  • Blade geometry: Influences hydrodynamic performance and efficiency.
  • Rotational speed: Affects thrust, torque, power consumption, and noise.

Propeller selection requires balancing thrust requirements, efficiency, available space, operating speed, and cavitation risk.

Hydrodynamic Efficiency and Cavitation

Propulsion efficiency describes how effectively input power is converted into useful movement. Losses can occur through turbulence, friction, mechanical resistance, and electrical conversion.

Cavitation occurs when local pressure around a propeller blade falls below the vapor pressure of water, producing vapor cavities that may collapse as pressure increases.

Cavitation can cause noise, vibration, reduced performance, and damage to propeller surfaces.

03 / ENGINEERING SYSTEMS

Types of Marine Propulsion Systems

Different marine platforms require different propulsion architectures according to their mission, size, operating environment, and maneuverability requirements.

01. Fixed-Pitch Propellers

Fixed-pitch propellers have blades with a constant geometric pitch. Their thrust is generally controlled by changing the motor's rotational speed.

They are mechanically straightforward and commonly used in underwater vehicles and small marine platforms.

02. Ducted Propellers

A ducted propeller operates inside a surrounding nozzle or duct. The duct changes the flow around the propeller and can improve thrust performance in some operating conditions.

Ducts can also provide physical protection around rotating blades.

03. Electric Thrusters

Electric thrusters combine an electric motor with a propeller or impeller assembly to generate controlled thrust.

They are widely used in ROVs and other remotely or autonomously controlled marine vehicles.

04. Waterjet Propulsion

Waterjet systems draw water through an inlet and accelerate it through a pump and nozzle. The resulting high-velocity water jet produces thrust.

Waterjets are used in some surface vessels where maneuverability and reduced exposed propeller hardware are useful.

05. Azimuth Thrusters

Azimuth thrusters can rotate their thrust direction around a vertical axis. This allows a vessel to direct thrust in different horizontal directions.

They are particularly useful in vessels requiring precise maneuvering and positioning.

06. Hybrid Propulsion

Hybrid propulsion architectures combine multiple propulsion or energy technologies, such as battery-electric systems and combustion-based generators.

Their purpose may include improving operational flexibility, endurance, or energy efficiency.

No propulsion technology is universally superior. The correct selection depends on the mission profile, vehicle geometry, required thrust, environmental conditions, maintenance requirements, and energy budget.
04 / ENGINEERING SYSTEMS

Electric Thrusters and Vehicle Movement

Thrusters allow underwater robots to control their position, orientation, and movement in three dimensions.

What Is an Electric Thruster?

An electric thruster is a propulsion unit that converts electrical energy into mechanical rotation and then into hydrodynamic thrust.

A typical thruster assembly may include:

  • Electric motor.
  • Propeller or impeller.
  • Motor controller or electronic drive.
  • Housing and mechanical support.
  • Seals or pressure-resistant construction.
  • Electrical and control connections.

Thruster Configurations in ROVs

ROVs often use multiple thrusters arranged in different directions. A control system combines their individual thrust outputs to produce the requested vehicle movement.

A vehicle may use horizontal thrusters for forward, backward, and sideways motion, while vertical thrusters control ascent and descent.

The exact configuration depends on vehicle design. Some ROVs have more thrusters or different arrangements to provide additional control authority.

Six Degrees of Freedom

A fully maneuverable underwater vehicle can be described using six degrees of freedom.

Movement Meaning Axis
Surge Forward and backward movement Longitudinal
Sway Sideways movement Lateral
Heave Upward and downward movement Vertical
Roll Rotation around the longitudinal axis Longitudinal
Pitch Nose-up and nose-down rotation Lateral
Yaw Rotation to the left or right Vertical

The vehicle's control system determines how much thrust each thruster should produce to achieve a requested movement.

Thruster Allocation

Thruster allocation is the process of converting a desired vehicle force and torque into individual thruster commands.

A mathematical model describes how each thruster's direction and position contribute to the total force and moment acting on the vehicle.

The control system uses this relationship to calculate suitable thrust commands while respecting motor limits and available power.

05 / ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING

Electric Motors and Motor Controllers

Electric motors are key components in modern electrically powered marine propulsion systems.

How Electric Motors Convert Energy

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy through electromagnetic interactions between its components.

Electrical current produces magnetic fields that interact to create rotational torque. The motor shaft transfers this mechanical output to a propeller, pump, or other mechanical component.

Brushless DC Motors (BLDC)

Brushless DC motors use electronic commutation instead of mechanical brushes. They are widely used in compact propulsion systems because of their power density and controllability.

  • Electronic commutation.
  • High power-to-weight potential.
  • Reduced brush-related maintenance.
  • Require suitable electronic controllers.

Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors

Permanent magnet synchronous motors use permanent magnets in the rotor and a controlled stator magnetic field.

  • Precise speed and torque control.
  • High efficiency in suitable designs.
  • Common in advanced electric drives.
  • Require appropriate drive electronics.

Induction Motors

Induction motors generate rotor current through electromagnetic induction. They are widely used in industrial and marine applications.

  • Robust construction.
  • No permanent magnets required.
  • Variable-speed operation is possible.
  • Efficiency depends on motor design and load.

Motor Controllers

A motor controller regulates the electrical power delivered to a motor and controls its operating behavior.

  • Speed regulation.
  • Torque control.
  • Direction control where supported.
  • Current and temperature protection.
  • Fault monitoring and shutdown.
A motor and its controller must be selected as a compatible system. Voltage, current, cooling, communication interfaces, environmental protection, and operating limits all matter.
06 / POWER SYSTEMS

Marine Energy Sources and Batteries

Energy sources determine how long a vehicle can operate and what equipment it can support.

Battery Systems

Batteries store chemical energy and convert it into electrical energy when a circuit is connected. They are widely used in autonomous underwater vehicles and electrically powered marine robots.

Battery selection depends on energy capacity, voltage, discharge rate, mass, volume, operating temperature, safety, charging requirements, and mission duration.

Lithium-Ion Batteries

Lithium-ion batteries are rechargeable electrochemical energy storage devices widely used in portable electronics, electric vehicles, and marine robotics.

  • High energy density in many cell designs.
  • Rechargeable operation.
  • Require battery management and protection.
  • Thermal and electrical safety are essential.

Lithium Primary Batteries

Primary lithium batteries are non-rechargeable batteries designed for applications that require high stored energy and long shelf life.

  • High energy density in suitable chemistries.
  • Useful for some long-duration missions.
  • Cannot be recharged like secondary cells.
  • Require appropriate handling and disposal.

Lead-Acid Batteries

Lead-acid batteries are established rechargeable batteries used in many industrial and marine electrical systems.

  • Well-established technology.
  • Relatively low energy density.
  • Heavy compared with many alternatives.
  • Require appropriate charging and maintenance.

Fuel Cells

Fuel cells generate electricity through electrochemical reactions using a fuel and an oxidizing agent.

  • Can provide sustained electrical generation.
  • May be used in specialized underwater systems.
  • Require fuel storage and supporting equipment.
  • System complexity and safety must be considered.

External Power for Tethered ROVs

Many ROVs receive electrical power from a surface vessel through an umbilical or tether. The surface power system supplies energy to the vehicle, sometimes through high-voltage transmission followed by onboard conversion.

This arrangement can support longer operations without relying entirely on onboard batteries. However, tether design introduces constraints involving cable length, electrical losses, handling, mechanical loading, and communication.

Some ROVs use onboard batteries or hybrid arrangements instead. The power architecture depends on the vehicle and its mission.

Energy Storage vs. Power Delivery

Energy capacity and power capability are different engineering properties.

  • Energy: The total amount of electrical work that can be supplied, often measured in watt-hours (Wh).
  • Power: The rate at which energy is delivered or consumed, measured in watts (W).
  • Voltage: The electrical potential difference, measured in volts (V).
  • Current: The rate of electrical charge flow, measured in amperes (A).
Electrical Power = Voltage × Current P = V × I
07 / POWER MANAGEMENT

Power Distribution and Energy Management

A marine robot needs more than a battery. It requires a controlled architecture for distributing energy safely and efficiently.

Power Distribution Architecture

A power distribution system transfers electrical energy from its source to different onboard subsystems.

A typical electrically powered architecture may include:

  • Battery pack or external power input.
  • Main power switch and protection devices.
  • Power distribution board.
  • DC-DC converters or other power converters.
  • Motor controllers and propulsion units.
  • Computers, sensors, and communication devices.
  • Monitoring and safety circuits.

Battery Management System (BMS)

A Battery Management System monitors and protects rechargeable battery packs. It helps ensure that the cells operate within their permitted electrical and thermal limits.

  • Voltage monitoring: Measures cell and pack voltage.
  • Current monitoring: Tracks charging and discharging current.
  • Temperature monitoring: Detects unsafe thermal conditions.
  • Cell balancing: Helps maintain appropriate cell voltage balance.
  • Protection: Can disconnect or limit operation when dangerous conditions are detected.

Energy Management System (EMS)

An Energy Management System supervises how energy is used across the vehicle. It can monitor consumption, estimate remaining energy, and help prioritize critical loads.

For example, a mission controller may reduce non-essential sensor activity or adjust propulsion commands when the remaining energy becomes limited.

The goal is to complete the mission safely while maintaining enough reserve energy for recovery and essential operations.

Power Budgeting

Engineers estimate the energy required by every major subsystem before deployment. This is called power or energy budgeting.

Subsystem Example Load Operating Pattern
Propulsion Thrusters and motors Variable / mission-dependent
Navigation INS, compass, depth sensor Continuous or periodic
Computing Onboard computer Continuous
Sensors Sonar, cameras, scientific sensors Continuous or scheduled
Communication Acoustic or radio modem Intermittent or scheduled
Auxiliary Lighting and other equipment Task-dependent
Energy = Power × Time E = P × t

If a device consumes 100 watts for 2 hours, its energy consumption is 200 watt-hours. Real mission calculations must also account for conversion losses, variable loads, battery limits, and reserve requirements.

08 / PERFORMANCE

Energy Efficiency and Mission Endurance

Efficient energy use helps marine robots operate longer and complete missions reliably.

What Determines Energy Efficiency?

Energy efficiency describes how much of the supplied energy is converted into useful output. In marine robotics, the overall result depends on several interacting subsystems.

  • Hydrodynamic shape and drag.
  • Propeller and thruster efficiency.
  • Motor and controller efficiency.
  • Electrical conversion losses.
  • Vehicle speed and mission profile.
  • Payload and auxiliary equipment demand.
  • Battery condition and operating temperature.

Mission Endurance

Mission endurance is the amount of time a vehicle can operate before it must be recovered, recharged, refueled, or otherwise serviced.

Endurance is not determined by battery capacity alone. It depends on average power consumption, usable stored energy, environmental conditions, mission tasks, and safety reserves.

Ideal Runtime = Usable Energy ÷ Average Power Runtime in hours = Energy in Wh / Power in W

Example: if a vehicle has 1,000 Wh of usable energy and its average load is 100 W, its ideal calculated runtime is 10 hours.

Actual runtime may be lower because of power conversion losses, battery discharge limits, temperature, aging, and changing mission loads.

Practical Energy-Saving Strategies

  • Optimize vehicle shape to reduce hydrodynamic drag.
  • Select propellers and motors suited to the expected operating range.
  • Avoid unnecessary acceleration and rapid changes in propulsion demand.
  • Schedule sensors and communication systems according to mission requirements.
  • Monitor battery condition and energy consumption.
  • Use mission planning to preserve energy for recovery and unexpected events.
A vehicle with a larger battery is not automatically more efficient. Extra battery mass can increase vehicle weight and influence buoyancy, trim, hydrodynamic drag, and structural requirements.
09 / PLATFORM APPLICATIONS

Propulsion and Energy Across Marine Robots

ROVs, AUVs, and USVs use different propulsion and power architectures because their missions and operating environments are different.

ROV Systems

Many ROVs use multiple electric thrusters for precise underwater movement.

Their power may be supplied from a surface vessel through a tether, through onboard batteries, or through a hybrid architecture.

The design must account for tether electrical losses, motor demand, depth, and vehicle maneuverability.

AUV Systems

AUVs commonly use onboard batteries to power propulsion, computing, navigation, and payload systems.

Some use a main propeller, while others use different propulsion arrangements depending on their mission.

Energy planning is particularly important because the vehicle operates without continuous physical power from a surface vessel.

USV Systems

Uncrewed Surface Vehicles may use electric motors, combustion engines, hybrid propulsion, or other configurations.

Their energy architecture may include batteries, fuel systems, solar panels, and generators.

The design depends on speed, payload, mission range, weather, and endurance.

Underwater Gliders

Underwater gliders primarily move by changing buoyancy and using wings to convert vertical movement into horizontal travel.

Their energy requirements differ from conventional propeller-driven AUVs. Electrical energy is still needed for control, instruments, and onboard systems.

11 / TECHNICAL CHALLENGES

Engineering Challenges and Reliability

Marine propulsion and energy systems operate in environments that impose demanding mechanical, electrical, and environmental constraints.

Water Ingress and Corrosion

Saltwater can damage electrical connections, metal surfaces, and electronic components. Pressure housings, seals, connectors, and suitable materials are essential.

Pressure and Depth

Hydrostatic pressure increases with depth. Housings, cables, connectors, and battery enclosures must be designed for their intended pressure conditions.

Thermal Management

Motors, controllers, batteries, and converters generate heat. Their temperatures must remain within specified operating limits.

Battery Safety

Electrical abuse, overheating, damaged cells, and incorrect charging can create serious battery hazards. Protection and monitoring are essential.

Mechanical Wear

Propellers, shafts, bearings, seals, and moving components may experience wear, vibration, and damage.

Energy Limitations

Limited stored energy can restrict speed, payload operation, mission duration, and recovery options.

Electrical Interference

Motor switching and high-current equipment can introduce electrical noise that affects sensitive sensors and communication systems.

System Failure

Motor controller faults, damaged wiring, battery degradation, or sensor failures may reduce propulsion performance or cause mission interruption.

12 / FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

Future of Marine Propulsion and Energy

New technologies are improving energy storage, propulsion efficiency, system monitoring, and autonomous mission management.

Advanced Battery Technologies

Research into improved battery chemistries, safer materials, higher energy density, and faster charging may increase the capabilities of marine robotic platforms.

Intelligent Energy Management

Advanced control algorithms can analyze mission requirements and energy consumption to help allocate power more effectively.

AI-Based Predictive Maintenance

Data from motors, bearings, batteries, and controllers can help identify abnormal operating patterns and predict potential maintenance needs.

High-Efficiency Propulsion

Improved propeller designs, motor control, hydrodynamic optimization, and advanced materials can improve overall propulsion performance.

Hybrid Energy Architectures

Combining batteries with generators, fuel cells, or renewable energy sources may provide greater flexibility for specific marine missions.

Autonomous Energy Optimization

Future marine robots may adapt their operating speed, sensor schedules, and mission priorities according to remaining energy and environmental conditions.

Future technologies should be evaluated by demonstrated performance, safety, cost, maintainability, and operational readiness—not by technological claims alone.
