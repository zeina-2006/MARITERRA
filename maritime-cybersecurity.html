MARITIME TECHNOLOGY / CYBERSECURITY

Maritime Cybersecurity & Reliability

Protecting connected ships, navigation systems, operational technology, and maritime information through cyber risk management, security controls, incident response, and operational resilience.

01 / INTRODUCTION

Understanding Maritime Cybersecurity

Modern vessels depend on interconnected digital systems for navigation, communication, propulsion, machinery monitoring, cargo operations, and administrative activities.

These technologies improve operational efficiency and information exchange. However, connectivity and automation can also introduce cybersecurity risks.

A cyber incident may affect the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information and systems. Depending on the affected equipment and operational context, the consequences may extend beyond information technology and affect ship operations, safety, or the environment.

Maritime cybersecurity refers to the practices, technologies, and procedures used to protect maritime digital assets from cyber threats.

Cyber resilience is the ability of an organization or system to continue operating appropriately, limit the impact of cyber incidents, and recover important functions after disruption.

Important distinction: Cybersecurity focuses on reducing the likelihood and impact of cyber threats. Cyber resilience also considers how the vessel and its systems will respond and recover when preventive controls fail.
02 / IT AND OT SYSTEMS

Information Technology and Operational Technology

Maritime cybersecurity requires understanding the difference between Information Technology (IT) and Operational Technology (OT).

IT SYSTEMS

Information Technology

IT systems manage information, business applications, administrative records, email, databases, file storage, and office networks.

OT SYSTEMS

Operational Technology

OT systems monitor or control physical equipment and industrial processes, including machinery, propulsion-related systems, and automation.

Comparison IT OT
Main purpose Manage information and business processes Monitor and control physical processes
Typical assets Servers, office computers, email systems Controllers, sensors, industrial networks
Important priorities Confidentiality, integrity, availability Safety, availability, integrity, operational continuity
Potential consequences Information exposure or business disruption Equipment disruption or unsafe physical conditions
Technical note: IT and OT are not always completely separate. Ships may have connected environments and shared information flows. Security architecture should account for these connections and their operational risks.
03 / CRITICAL SYSTEMS

Cybersecurity-Critical Shipboard Systems

A ship contains multiple digital systems that may introduce cybersecurity risks if they are compromised, misconfigured, or unavailable.

Bridge Systems

Navigation-related systems, electronic chart displays, position information, and connected bridge equipment.

Propulsion and Machinery

Engine monitoring, machinery control, power management, and automation-related systems.

Cargo Management

Digital systems supporting cargo handling, cargo monitoring, and associated operational information.

Communication Systems

Satellite communications, onboard networks, email, and ship-to-shore information exchange.

Access Control

Digital systems managing access to restricted areas, facilities, and selected shipboard services.

Administrative Networks

Office computers, servers, business applications, and information systems used by crew and shore-based personnel.

04 / CYBER THREATS

Common Maritime Cyber Threats

A cyber threat is a potential source of harm to digital systems or information. Threats may target administrative networks, connected equipment, communication systems, or operational technology.

Phishing

Fraudulent messages designed to trick users into revealing credentials, opening malicious files, or visiting deceptive websites.

Ransomware

Malicious software that may encrypt or otherwise prevent access to information and systems, sometimes accompanied by extortion.

Malware

Software designed to perform unauthorized actions, steal information, disrupt systems, or compromise devices.

Unauthorized Access

Access to systems or information without appropriate permission, potentially through compromised accounts or weak authentication.

Insider Threats

Risks arising from people with legitimate access who misuse privileges intentionally or make harmful mistakes.

Supply Chain Attacks

Attacks involving compromised suppliers, software updates, service providers, or equipment delivery processes.

Denial of Service

Attempts to make systems or services unavailable by overwhelming or disrupting their resources or communications.

Social Engineering

Manipulating individuals into disclosing sensitive information or performing unauthorized actions.

05 / VULNERABILITIES

Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities

A vulnerability is a weakness in a system, process, configuration, or security control that could be exploited by a threat.

Weak Authentication

Weak or reused passwords and inadequate authentication controls can make unauthorized access easier.

Unpatched Software

Systems running outdated software may contain known vulnerabilities that have not been addressed.

Misconfigured Networks

Unnecessary network services, excessive permissions, or poorly configured connections may increase exposure.

Legacy Equipment

Older systems may have limited security features or compatibility constraints that complicate updates and protection.

Insufficient Monitoring

Limited logging and monitoring can make suspicious activities harder to identify and investigate.

Human Error

Accidental disclosure, unsafe device use, or incorrect configuration can introduce avoidable security risks.

Remember: A threat and a vulnerability are different concepts. A threat is a potential source of harm; a vulnerability is a weakness that may be exploited. Risk depends on the likelihood and consequences of a harmful event.
06 / RISK MANAGEMENT

Maritime Cyber Risk Management

Cyber risk management is a structured process for identifying, analyzing, evaluating, and treating cybersecurity risks.

01

Identify Assets

Determine which systems, devices, data, networks, and services are important to the vessel's operations.

02

Identify Threats and Vulnerabilities

Examine potential threat sources and weaknesses that could affect identified assets.

03

Analyze Risk

Consider the likelihood of a cyber event and the possible consequences for safety, operations, security, and the environment.

04

Evaluate and Prioritize

Compare identified risks against established criteria and prioritize the risks requiring treatment.

05

Treat the Risk

Select appropriate controls to avoid, reduce, transfer, or accept a risk according to organizational policy and applicable requirements.

06

Monitor and Review

Reassess risks when systems, operations, threats, suppliers, or technical configurations change.

Operational principle: Risk management should be continuous. A one-time assessment cannot account for every future change in equipment, connectivity, threats, or operating conditions.
07 / SECURITY CONTROLS

Protecting Maritime Digital Systems

Security controls are safeguards used to reduce the likelihood or consequences of cyber incidents. Effective protection combines technical controls, procedures, and people.

Network Segmentation

Dividing networks into controlled zones and restricting unnecessary communication between administrative and operational environments.

Firewalls

Applying network traffic rules to control permitted and denied communications between systems and network zones.

Multi-Factor Authentication

Requiring additional authentication evidence beyond a password for supported accounts and access paths.

Endpoint Protection

Using suitable security tools and configuration controls to protect supported computers and devices.

Patch Management

Evaluating and applying software updates through controlled procedures that consider operational and safety requirements.

Access Management

Applying least privilege so users and services receive only the access needed for their responsibilities.

Secure Backups

Maintaining protected backups and verifying that critical information can be restored when required.

Security Awareness

Training crew and shore-based personnel to recognize phishing, suspicious devices, unsafe behavior, and reporting procedures.

Important OT consideration: Network segmentation does not mean that every connection should be disconnected. Operational communication requirements must be understood and controlled. Changes to safety-related systems should follow approved engineering and operational procedures.
08 / MONITORING & DETECTION

Cybersecurity Monitoring and Threat Detection

Monitoring helps organizations identify unusual activities, suspicious connections, unexpected changes, and potential security incidents.

Log Monitoring

Reviewing system and network records to identify unusual access, authentication failures, and unexpected events.

Network Monitoring

Observing network communications and identifying unexpected traffic patterns or unauthorized connections.

Intrusion Detection

Using security tools and detection rules to identify activities that may indicate malicious behavior.

Asset Monitoring

Tracking connected devices, software versions, configuration changes, and relevant system status information.

Security Alerts

Generating notifications when defined security conditions or suspicious patterns are detected.

Security Analysis

Correlating relevant events to help analysts understand whether multiple observations are related.

Detection limitation: A monitoring system may generate false positives or fail to identify an unfamiliar attack. Alerts require context, investigation, and appropriate response procedures.
09 / INCIDENT RESPONSE

Responding to Maritime Cyber Incidents

A cyber incident response plan defines how an organization prepares for, handles, documents, and recovers from cybersecurity events.

01

Preparation

Establish responsibilities, communication procedures, incident plans, backup arrangements, and personnel training before an incident occurs.

02

Identification

Recognize suspicious activity, determine affected systems, collect relevant information, and assess the potential operational impact.

03

Containment

Limit the spread or impact of the incident using controlled actions that consider vessel safety and operational requirements.

04

Eradication

Remove confirmed malicious components and address the underlying causes according to approved technical procedures.

05

Recovery

Restore affected systems and services through verified recovery procedures, then monitor their operation carefully.

06

Lessons Learned

Review the incident, document findings, improve controls, and update training and risk assessments.

Safety comes first: Containment actions on a ship must be coordinated with responsible personnel. Abruptly disconnecting or shutting down an operational system can itself create safety risks.
10 / RELIABILITY & RESILIENCE

System Reliability and Cyber Resilience

Reliability and cyber resilience are related but different concepts.

Reliability concerns the ability of a system to perform its required function under specified conditions for a specified period.

Cyber resilience concerns the ability to anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to cyber-related disruption.

Redundancy

Providing alternative components or paths so that a single failure does not necessarily stop an essential function.

Fault Detection

Identifying system failures and abnormal conditions through monitoring and diagnostics.

Backup and Restoration

Maintaining suitable backup copies and testing restoration procedures for critical information and configurations.

Recovery Planning

Defining how essential services can be restored after disruption, including responsibilities and recovery priorities.

Fail-Safe Design

Designing systems so that certain failures lead to a safer state, where appropriate to the equipment and engineering requirements.

Continuous Improvement

Updating risk assessments, security procedures, and recovery plans as technologies and threats evolve.

Do not confuse the concepts: A highly reliable system is not automatically cyber-resilient. A system may rarely fail under normal conditions but still be vulnerable to a deliberate cyberattack.
11 / SUPPLY CHAIN SECURITY

Third-Party and Supply Chain Security

Ships depend on equipment manufacturers, software developers, maintenance contractors, communication providers, and other external suppliers.

These relationships can introduce risks when suppliers have access to shipboard systems or provide software, updates, equipment, and technical services.

Supplier Assessment

Evaluating suppliers' security practices, technical capabilities, and access requirements.

Remote Access Control

Restricting remote connections to approved users, systems, purposes, and time periods.

Software Verification

Applying controlled procedures to verify software sources, integrity, and suitability before installation.

Maintenance Security

Managing service activities, temporary accounts, portable devices, and configuration changes.

12 / PRACTICAL SCENARIO

Maritime Cyber Incident: A Practical Example

Consider a hypothetical vessel where a crew member receives an email containing a malicious attachment. The attachment is opened on an administrative computer.

Incident Scenario

  • An employee opens a suspicious attachment on an office computer.
  • Security monitoring detects unusual activity on that computer.
  • Investigation identifies suspicious communication attempts.
  • The security team evaluates whether other systems or accounts are affected.
  • Appropriate containment measures are coordinated with responsible ship personnel.
  • Affected systems are investigated, cleaned, and restored through approved recovery procedures.
  • The organization reviews the incident and improves its security controls.
Key lesson: An incident that begins on an administrative computer should not be assumed to affect every shipboard system. The actual impact depends on network architecture, access controls, system connections, and the evidence collected during investigation.
13 / STANDARDS & GUIDELINES

International Maritime Cybersecurity References

Several international guidelines and technical requirements address maritime cyber risk and the resilience of shipboard systems.

Reference Subject Purpose
IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 Maritime Cyber Risk Management Guidelines Provides high-level guidance for managing cyber risks in shipping.
IMO Resolution MSC.428(98) Cyber Risk Management in Safety Management Systems Addresses cyber risk management within established safety management systems.
IACS UR E26 Rev.1 Cyber Resilience of Ships Addresses cyber resilience at the ship level.
IACS UR E27 Rev.1 Cyber Resilience of On-board Systems and Equipment Addresses cybersecurity requirements for onboard systems and equipment.
IEC 62443 Series Industrial Automation and Control Systems Security Provides a cybersecurity framework and requirements for industrial automation and control systems.
Applicability matters: IACS UR E26 and E27 Rev.1 have an implementation date of 1 July 2024 for applicable new ships contracted for construction on or after that date. Their applicability depends on vessel scope and the relevant classification and regulatory context. They should not be presented as automatically applying to every existing vessel.
