Autonomous Navigation and Collision Avoidance

Explore how autonomous ships plan routes, estimate collision risks, interpret maritime traffic situations, and select safe navigation actions while operating under maritime rules and environmental constraints.

01 / Introduction

What Is Autonomous Navigation?

Autonomous navigation is the ability of a vessel to determine and follow a suitable route while responding to changes in its environment. An autonomous ship uses information from navigation sensors, electronic charts, positioning systems, environmental measurements, and perception technologies to support its decisions.

Traditional navigation depends heavily on human officers who interpret information and make decisions. Autonomous navigation systems aim to perform selected navigation functions using computer-based sensing, planning, decision-making, and control.

These systems must consider more than the shortest distance between two points. They must account for navigational safety, traffic density, water depth, weather, vessel manoeuvrability, restricted areas, and applicable maritime regulations.

Core Objective

The objective is to navigate from an initial position to a destination while maintaining safe separation from other vessels and obstacles, respecting navigational constraints, and responding appropriately to changing conditions.

02 / System Architecture

Main Functions of Autonomous Navigation

Autonomous navigation is a coordinated process. Different subsystems provide information and perform specific tasks before a navigation action is executed.

STAGE 01 Position Estimation
STAGE 02 Route Planning
STAGE 03 Risk Assessment
STAGE 04 Guidance and Control

1. Position Estimation

The system estimates the ship's current position, heading, speed, and movement. GNSS, inertial sensors, radar observations, and other navigation sources may contribute to this estimate.

2. Route Planning

The system determines a route from the departure point to the destination. It considers navigable waters, charted hazards, restricted zones, and voyage requirements.

3. Situational Assessment

The ship evaluates nearby traffic, potential obstacles, weather, and other factors that may affect safe navigation.

4. Decision-Making

The decision-making system evaluates possible actions, such as maintaining course, reducing speed, or altering course when appropriate.

5. Guidance and Control

Guidance determines the desired movement, while control systems translate the required movement into steering, propulsion, and other vessel commands.

Important distinction: Route planning establishes a suitable voyage path. Collision avoidance responds to immediate or developing hazards. Both are necessary, but they operate at different levels and time scales.

03 / Route Planning

Autonomous Route Planning

Route planning is the process of selecting a path between two or more geographic locations. An autonomous ship must select a route that is not only efficient but also suitable for its physical characteristics and the current maritime environment.

Factors Affecting Route Planning

FACTOR 01

Geographical Constraints

Coastlines, islands, narrow channels, shallow waters, and other geographical features limit where a ship can safely navigate.

FACTOR 02

Electronic Navigational Charts

Electronic charts provide information about depths, navigational marks, restricted areas, and other charted features used during route planning.

FACTOR 03

Weather and Sea Conditions

Wind, waves, currents, visibility, and storms can influence route suitability, speed, fuel consumption, and vessel safety.

FACTOR 04

Vessel Characteristics

Draft, length, beam, turning ability, stopping distance, propulsion performance, and manoeuvring limitations affect which routes are appropriate.

FACTOR 05

Maritime Traffic

Traffic separation schemes, shipping lanes, port approaches, and nearby vessels must be considered during route selection.

FACTOR 06

Operational Requirements

Arrival time, fuel efficiency, cargo requirements, port instructions, and voyage priorities may influence the final route.

Global and Local Route Planning

Global Route Planning: Determines a broad route from the departure location to the destination. It considers large-scale geographical and navigational constraints.

Local Route Planning: Adjusts the ship's immediate path to respond to nearby vessels, obstacles, changing conditions, and short-term safety requirements.

Example: A global planner may select a route through a designated shipping corridor. A local planner may then recommend a safe adjustment because another vessel is approaching.

04 / Navigation Algorithms

Algorithms Used in Route Planning

Computer algorithms help autonomous vessels search for suitable paths and evaluate alternative navigation options. Different algorithms are appropriate for different planning problems and operating environments.

ALGORITHM 01

Dijkstra's Algorithm

Dijkstra's algorithm finds a shortest path in a graph with non-negative edge costs. It can be used when navigable areas are represented as connected nodes and route segments.

Its result depends on how the graph and travel costs are defined. The shortest graph path is not automatically the safest maritime route.

ALGORITHM 02

A* Search

A* combines the cost already incurred with a heuristic estimate of the remaining cost. It can search for efficient paths through a map or planning graph.

Its performance and guarantees depend on the graph, cost function, and properties of the heuristic.

ALGORITHM 03

Artificial Potential Fields

This method represents the destination as an attractive influence and obstacles as repulsive influences. The combined field guides the ship toward a candidate path.

It can be useful for local planning, but may suffer from local minima and oscillatory behaviour.

ALGORITHM 04

Model Predictive Control (MPC)

MPC repeatedly predicts future vessel behaviour over a limited time horizon and optimizes a sequence of control actions.

It can account for constraints and vessel dynamics, but depends on model quality, computation, and reliable input information.

ALGORITHM 05

Sampling-Based Planning

Methods such as rapidly exploring random trees (RRT) explore possible paths by sampling points in a planning space.

They can be useful in complex spaces, although the generated path may require smoothing and additional safety validation.

ALGORITHM 06

Learning-Based Planning

Machine learning and reinforcement learning approaches can learn navigation policies from data or simulated experience.

Their use in safety-critical navigation requires careful validation, robustness testing, and appropriate safeguards.

Engineering note: No single algorithm solves every maritime navigation problem. Practical systems may combine graph search, optimization, prediction, control, and rule-based safety constraints.

05 / Collision Risk Assessment

Understanding Collision Risk

Collision avoidance begins with identifying whether another vessel or obstacle may create a dangerous situation. The system must evaluate relative position, relative motion, distance, time, and the limitations of its own vessel.

Relative Motion

Relative motion describes how one vessel moves in relation to another. Instead of considering only the absolute speed of each vessel, the system examines their combined movement and how their separation changes over time.

Closest Point of Approach (CPA)

Closest Point of Approach is the predicted minimum separation between two moving vessels if their current courses and speeds remain unchanged.

Time to Closest Point of Approach (TCPA)

Time to Closest Point of Approach estimates how long it will take to reach that predicted minimum separation, assuming the current relative motion continues.

Basic Relative Motion Model

r(t) = r₀ + vᵣt

r₀ = initial relative position; vᵣ = relative velocity; t = elapsed time.

For a simplified constant-velocity model, the time of closest approach can be estimated using:

tCPA = − (r₀ · vᵣ) / |vᵣ|²

This expression assumes non-zero relative velocity and a constant-velocity model.

The predicted relative position at that time can be used to estimate the closest separation:

dCPA = |r₀ + vᵣ × tCPA|

The expression represents the magnitude of the predicted relative position at closest approach.

How CPA and TCPA Are Used

  • CPA estimates the predicted minimum separation.
  • TCPA estimates when that separation may occur.
  • Small CPA values may indicate a potentially close encounter.
  • Short TCPA values may indicate that a situation is developing quickly.
  • Both values must be interpreted with the operational context and uncertainty.

Critical limitation: CPA and TCPA are predictions based on assumptions about movement. They do not independently determine whether a collision is certain, nor do they replace COLREGs, good seamanship, or a complete risk assessment. A vessel may change course or speed, and sensor estimates may be uncertain.

06 / Maritime Regulations

COLREGs and Autonomous Ships

The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea, commonly known as COLREGs, establish internationally recognized rules intended to prevent collisions between vessels.

Autonomous navigation systems must be designed to support compliance with applicable collision-prevention requirements. This is a complex engineering challenge because the rules involve observation, judgement, interpretation, and appropriate action in changing circumstances.

Important COLREG Rules

RULE 05

Look-out

Requires a proper look-out by sight and hearing, as well as all available appropriate means. Autonomous systems must support effective observation of the surroundings.

RULE 06

Safe Speed

Requires vessels to proceed at a safe speed so that appropriate and effective action can be taken to avoid collision and the vessel can be stopped within an appropriate distance.

RULE 07

Risk of Collision

Requires appropriate means to determine whether a risk of collision exists. Systems must not rely on incomplete or misleading observations.

RULE 08

Action to Avoid Collision

Describes action taken to avoid collision. Such action should be positive, made in ample time, and carried out with due regard to the requirements of the rules.

RULES 13–15

Encounter Situations

These rules address overtaking, head-on situations, and crossing situations, including the responsibilities of vessels in those encounters.

RULES 16–17

Give-Way and Stand-On Actions

These rules describe the actions expected from give-way and stand-on vessels, subject to the circumstances and other applicable rules.

RULE 18

Responsibilities Between Vessels

Establishes responsibilities between different vessel categories and their relative obligations under the collision regulations.

RULE 19

Restricted Visibility

Addresses conduct of vessels in or near areas of restricted visibility when vessels are not in sight of one another.

Common Encounter Types

Head-On Situation: Two power-driven vessels meet on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses, creating a risk of collision. Rule 14 addresses this situation.

Crossing Situation: Two power-driven vessels cross in a way that may create a risk of collision. Rule 15 establishes the give-way responsibility in the applicable circumstances.

Overtaking Situation: A vessel approaches another from a direction more than 22.5 degrees abaft its beam. Rule 13 addresses the overtaking vessel's responsibility.

Important legal distinction: These descriptions are introductory summaries, not a substitute for the full wording and interpretation of COLREGs. The rules contain detailed definitions, exceptions, responsibilities, and requirements that must be considered in actual navigation.

07 / Collision Avoidance

Collision Avoidance Strategies

Collision avoidance is the process of selecting and carrying out appropriate actions to reduce the risk of collision. The system must evaluate the situation, consider available manoeuvres, and determine whether an action is safe and operationally appropriate.

1. Course Alteration

Changing the vessel's heading may create greater separation from another vessel or obstacle. The direction and timing of the alteration must be evaluated in relation to the encounter situation and applicable rules.

2. Speed Adjustment

Reducing or otherwise adjusting speed can change the timing and geometry of an encounter. It may also improve the vessel's ability to respond to developing hazards.

3. Combined Manoeuvres

Some situations may require coordinated changes in course and speed. The system must evaluate the resulting movement rather than considering each adjustment in isolation.

4. Replanning the Local Route

A local planner may generate an alternative path around a detected obstacle while keeping the overall voyage objective in view.

5. Monitoring the Manoeuvre

After a manoeuvre is initiated, the system must monitor the resulting motion and reassess the situation. A previously suitable action may become insufficient if another vessel changes course or new information appears.

Key principle: Avoidance is not complete merely because a command has been issued. The system must verify the vessel's actual response and continue monitoring the developing situation.

08 / Intelligent Decision-Making

How Does an Autonomous Ship Choose an Action?

An autonomous navigation system may evaluate several possible actions before selecting one. The decision process should consider safety, regulatory requirements, vessel dynamics, environmental conditions, and the reliability of available information.

Decision-Making Inputs

  • Own-ship position, heading, speed, and manoeuvring state.
  • Detected target positions and estimated movement.
  • CPA and TCPA estimates with their uncertainty.
  • Electronic chart information and navigational restrictions.
  • Weather, visibility, wind, waves, and currents.
  • Applicable COLREGs responsibilities and operational instructions.
  • Propulsion, steering, and control-system limitations.

Possible Decision Options

  • Maintain the current course and speed when appropriate.
  • Alter course in accordance with the developing situation.
  • Adjust speed to improve the safety of the encounter.
  • Generate an alternative local route.
  • Request remote operator intervention when required by the system design.
  • Enter a predefined safe state if reliable navigation cannot be maintained.

Safety Constraints

A navigation algorithm should not optimize only for speed, distance, or fuel consumption. Safety-related constraints must limit the actions that the system is allowed to select.

These constraints may include minimum safe separation requirements, navigable-water boundaries, vessel turning limitations, speed restrictions, and operational procedures. Their exact implementation depends on the vessel and its approved operating concept.

Critical issue: A mathematically optimal route is not necessarily a legally compliant or operationally safe route. Navigation algorithms must be evaluated against maritime rules and real vessel behaviour, not just computational performance.

09 / Practical Application

Practical Scenario: Two Vessels Approaching Each Other

Imagine an autonomous cargo ship navigating through a busy shipping area. Its perception system detects another vessel approaching from a direction that may create a collision risk.

Navigation System Response

  1. Sensors detect the approaching vessel and estimate its position, course, and speed.
  2. The tracking system updates the target's movement using successive observations.
  3. The navigation system estimates relative motion, CPA, and TCPA.
  4. The system evaluates whether a risk of collision exists and identifies the applicable encounter situation.
  5. It considers the relevant COLREGs requirements and the manoeuvring capabilities of both vessels.
  6. Candidate actions are evaluated, including course alteration, speed adjustment, or a combination of actions.
  7. A suitable action is selected and sent to the guidance and control systems.
  8. The vessel's actual movement is monitored. The system reassesses the situation as new information becomes available.

If the situation becomes uncertain or the system cannot maintain reliable awareness, the vessel may need to follow its predefined contingency procedures and involve a remote operator when required.

Learning point: Collision avoidance is a continuous process. Detecting another vessel, calculating a predicted closest approach, selecting an action, and verifying the result are all necessary parts of a complete response.

10 / Engineering Challenges

Challenges in Autonomous Navigation

1. Complex Maritime Traffic

Busy ports, narrow channels, fishing areas, and shipping lanes may contain many vessels moving in different directions and at different speeds.

2. Unpredictable Human Behaviour

Other vessels may change course or speed unexpectedly. Their actions may be influenced by operational needs, weather, local conditions, or human judgement.

3. Environmental Uncertainty

Wind, waves, currents, and restricted visibility can affect both vessel motion and the accuracy of navigation information.

4. Vessel Manoeuvrability

Large ships may require considerable distance and time to change course or reduce speed. Navigation systems must account for real physical limitations.

5. COLREGs Interpretation

Translating collision regulations into software is difficult because encounter situations may be ambiguous or change while a manoeuvre is being carried out.

6. Sensor and Positioning Errors

Inaccurate position estimates, delayed measurements, missed detections, or incorrect target tracking can affect collision-risk calculations.

7. Verification and Validation

Systems must be tested under many different conditions, including rare and difficult situations. Simulation alone cannot establish that a system is safe in every real-world operating environment.

8. Human Oversight and Responsibility

The role of onboard personnel and remote operators depends on the vessel's operating model and applicable requirements. Clear procedures are needed for intervention, communication, and emergency response.

11 / Future Developments

The Future of Autonomous Navigation

AI-Assisted Route Optimization

Advanced algorithms may help evaluate large numbers of route alternatives while considering weather, traffic, fuel efficiency, and navigational constraints.

Predictive Collision Avoidance

Future systems may improve the prediction of other vessels' movements and evaluate multiple possible future encounter scenarios.

Cooperative Navigation

Better information exchange between vessels and shore-based services may support broader traffic awareness and coordinated navigation.

Digital Twins and Simulation

Digital models of ships and maritime environments can help engineers test navigation algorithms, investigate failure scenarios, and evaluate performance before deployment.

Explainable Navigation Decisions

More transparent decision systems may help remote operators and safety assessors understand why a navigation action was selected and what information influenced the decision.

12 / Key Takeaways

What You Should Remember

Autonomous navigation combines positioning, planning, decision-making, guidance, and control.
Route planning selects a suitable path while considering geographical and operational constraints.
Global planning and local planning solve different navigation problems.
Algorithms such as A*, Dijkstra, and MPC can support navigation planning.
CPA estimates predicted minimum separation, while TCPA estimates the time to reach it.
COLREGs provide essential international collision-prevention requirements.
Collision avoidance requires continuous assessment and monitoring after a manoeuvre.
A mathematically optimal action is not automatically safe or compliant.
Sensor uncertainty and vessel manoeuvrability must be considered.
Testing, validation, and appropriate human oversight are essential to safe operation.
