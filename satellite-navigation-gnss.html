FOUNDATIONS — 01

Understanding Satellite Navigation

Satellite navigation is a positioning technology that uses signals transmitted by satellites orbiting Earth. A receiver collects these signals and calculates its position by measuring the time required for each signal to travel from a satellite to the receiver.

In maritime operations, satellite positioning provides important information about a vessel's geographical location. It supports navigation systems, electronic charts, route monitoring, vessel tracking, and many other onboard applications.

The term GNSS stands for Global Navigation Satellite System. It describes the broader family of satellite constellations that provide positioning, navigation, and timing services.

Important Terminology

GPS is one specific satellite navigation system operated by the United States. GNSS is the general term that includes GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou, and other compatible satellite navigation services.

Position, Navigation and Timing (PNT)

GNSS provides three closely related services known as Positioning, Navigation and Timing (PNT).

  • Positioning: determining geographical coordinates such as latitude, longitude, and height.
  • Navigation: using position and movement information to support route guidance and navigation decisions.
  • Timing: providing precise time references that can synchronize navigation equipment and other technical systems.
ENGINEERING PRINCIPLES — 02

How Satellite Navigation Works

GNSS positioning is based on accurate time measurement, known satellite positions, radio signal transmission, and mathematical distance estimation.

Navigation satellites continuously broadcast coded radio signals containing timing information and navigation data. The receiver uses these signals to estimate its distance from multiple satellites.

Signal Travel Time

Radio signals travel at approximately the speed of light. By estimating how long a signal takes to reach the receiver, the system can calculate an approximate distance between the satellite and the receiver.

Distance = Signal Speed × Travel Time The basic relationship used to estimate satellite to receiver distance.

The measured distance is called a pseudorange, because it includes errors caused by receiver clock offset, atmospheric delays, satellite clock and orbit errors, and other measurement effects.

Trilateration

GNSS uses a mathematical method called trilateration. The receiver estimates its distance from satellites whose positions are known. Combining these distance measurements allows the receiver to calculate its location.

A simplified explanation uses three satellite distances to illustrate how position can be constrained. In a practical standalone three-dimensional GNSS fix, at least four satellite measurements are normally needed to solve for three position coordinates and the receiver clock offset.

Why Are Four Satellites Normally Needed?

The receiver must estimate latitude, longitude, height, and its own clock error. Four independent satellite measurements provide the basic observations needed to solve these four unknowns, assuming suitable satellite geometry.

Satellite Geometry

The spatial arrangement of satellites affects the quality of the position solution. Satellites spread across different directions generally provide a stronger geometric arrangement than satellites clustered together in a small part of the sky.

This effect is commonly described using Dilution of Precision (DOP). Lower DOP values generally indicate more favorable satellite geometry, while higher values indicate that measurement errors may have a greater effect on the calculated position.

SYSTEM DESIGN — 03

GNSS System Architecture

A satellite navigation system is not made up of satellites alone. It requires space infrastructure, ground control facilities, and user equipment.

1. Space Segment

The space segment consists of navigation satellites distributed across carefully designed orbital constellations.

  • Broadcast navigation signals.
  • Transmit precise timing information.
  • Broadcast orbital information used to calculate satellite positions.
  • Provide global or regional coverage according to the constellation design.

2. Control Segment

The control segment consists of ground infrastructure responsible for monitoring and maintaining the satellite constellation.

  • Monitor satellite health and performance.
  • Estimate satellite orbital parameters.
  • Monitor and correct satellite clock information.
  • Upload updated navigation data and control information.

3. User Segment

The user segment includes GNSS receivers and the equipment that processes satellite signals. In maritime environments, these receivers may be installed on ships, offshore platforms, buoys, survey equipment, and other marine systems.

  • Receive satellite signals through an antenna.
  • Decode navigation information.
  • Calculate position, velocity, and time.
  • Send positioning information to connected navigation equipment.

Engineering Insight

A GNSS receiver does not simply read a location transmitted directly by a satellite. It processes signals from multiple satellites and calculates its own position using timing measurements and satellite navigation data.

POSITION COMPUTATION — 04

How a GNSS Receiver Calculates Position

The receiver follows a sequence of signal-processing and mathematical operations to produce a position solution.

01

Signal Acquisition

The receiver searches for available satellite signals and identifies the signals it can process.

02

Signal Tracking

The receiver tracks signal timing and carrier information to maintain reliable measurements.

03

Navigation Data Decoding

The receiver obtains satellite timing, orbital information, and other navigation parameters.

04

Pseudorange Measurement

Signal travel time is used to estimate the distance between the receiver and each satellite.

05

Position Solution

The receiver combines satellite measurements to estimate its position and clock offset.

06

Output and Validation

The calculated position, velocity, time, and quality indicators are provided to connected equipment.

Position Output

A GNSS receiver may provide several outputs, depending on the receiver design and configuration:

  • Latitude and longitude.
  • Ellipsoidal height or other configured height reference.
  • Speed over ground (SOG).
  • Course over ground (COG).
  • Time and synchronization information.
  • Position quality and satellite information.

COG Is Not the Same as Heading

Course over Ground describes the direction of movement relative to the Earth's surface. Heading describes the direction the vessel's bow is pointing. Wind, waves, and currents can cause these values to differ.

GLOBAL SYSTEMS — 05

Major Global Navigation Satellite Systems

Several independent satellite constellations provide global navigation services. A multi-constellation receiver can use signals from more than one system, subject to receiver compatibility and signal availability.

System Operator / Region Main Characteristics
GPS United States Global positioning, navigation, and timing services.
Galileo European Union European global satellite navigation constellation.
GLONASS Russia Global satellite navigation and positioning services.
BeiDou China Global navigation satellite system with regional service capabilities.

Multi-GNSS Receivers

Modern receivers may process compatible signals from multiple constellations. This can increase the number of available satellites and improve positioning availability in challenging environments.

However, using more satellites does not automatically guarantee a more accurate position. Signal quality, satellite geometry, receiver design, measurement errors, and interference must also be considered.

PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS — 06

GNSS in Maritime Navigation

GNSS is a major source of positioning information in modern maritime operations. Its output can be integrated with other navigation systems to support vessel awareness and voyage execution.

1. Vessel Positioning

GNSS receivers provide geographical coordinates that help bridge teams determine the vessel's estimated location and monitor its movement.

2. Electronic Chart Integration

Position information can be supplied to an Electronic Chart Display and Information System (ECDIS), allowing the vessel's calculated position to be displayed in relation to charted features, routes, and navigational hazards.

3. Route Monitoring

GNSS position updates can be compared with a planned route to help identify cross-track deviations, progress, and changes in vessel position.

4. Speed and Movement Monitoring

GNSS-derived speed over ground and course over ground provide useful movement information. These values can support navigation calculations and situational awareness.

5. Vessel Tracking and AIS

Many AIS installations use GNSS-derived position information as part of the vessel's transmitted navigation data. This helps other equipped vessels and shore stations build a picture of maritime traffic.

6. Surveying and Offshore Operations

High-precision GNSS equipment can support marine surveying, offshore construction, hydrographic positioning, and the positioning of surface survey vessels.

Practical Example

During a coastal voyage, a vessel's GNSS receiver calculates its position. The position is supplied to ECDIS, where the bridge team can compare the vessel's location with the planned route and charted navigation information. Radar, visual observations, and other instruments provide additional information for cross-checking.

GNSS and Underwater Vehicles

GNSS is useful for surface vessels and for underwater vehicles when they are at the surface and their antenna can receive satellite signals.

However, conventional GNSS signals do not normally penetrate seawater sufficiently for direct positioning of a submerged vehicle. Underwater platforms therefore use other methods, including inertial navigation, Doppler Velocity Logs, and acoustic positioning.

TECHNICAL PERFORMANCE — 07

GNSS Accuracy and Sources of Error

Position accuracy describes how close the calculated position is to the true position. It depends on satellite signals, receiver capabilities, the surrounding environment, and the quality of measurement processing.

1. Satellite Clock Errors

Small timing differences between the satellite's clock and the reference time can introduce errors into the estimated signal travel time and therefore the calculated distance.

2. Orbit Errors

If the satellite's estimated orbital position differs from its actual position, the receiver's distance calculations may be affected.

3. Ionospheric Delay

The ionosphere contains charged particles that affect the propagation of radio signals. This can introduce frequency-dependent delays into GNSS measurements.

4. Tropospheric Delay

Atmospheric conditions in the lower atmosphere, including temperature, pressure, and water vapor, can change the propagation time of satellite signals.

5. Multipath Effects

Multipath occurs when a signal reaches the receiver through reflected paths in addition to the direct path. Reflections from nearby structures or surfaces can distort measurements.

6. Poor Satellite Geometry

When satellites are concentrated in similar directions, the position solution may become more sensitive to measurement errors.

7. Receiver and Antenna Limitations

Receiver quality, antenna placement, signal tracking performance, interference protection, and processing algorithms all influence the final position solution.

Accuracy Is Not a Single Universal Number

A receiver's accuracy cannot be guaranteed simply by knowing which GNSS constellation it uses. Actual performance depends on operating conditions, receiver specifications, satellite geometry, and the correction methods available.

PRECISION TECHNOLOGIES — 08

Methods for Improving GNSS Accuracy

Several techniques can improve GNSS positioning performance by correcting or reducing measurement errors.

Differential GNSS (DGNSS)

DGNSS uses reference stations at known locations to estimate common GNSS errors. Corrections are transmitted to nearby receivers so they can improve their position calculations.

Real-Time Kinematic (RTK)

RTK uses carrier-phase measurements and correction data from reference stations to provide high precision positioning. It can achieve centimeter-level results under suitable conditions, including adequate correction communication and reliable satellite tracking.

Precise Point Positioning (PPP)

PPP uses precise satellite orbit and clock products, together with detailed measurement processing, to improve positioning without requiring a nearby local reference station in the same way as conventional RTK.

Satellite-Based Augmentation Systems (SBAS)

SBAS uses ground monitoring networks and geostationary satellite broadcasts to distribute correction and integrity information over supported service regions.

Method Principle Important Consideration
DGNSS Reference-station corrections Correction coverage and quality
RTK Carrier-phase positioning Correction link and ambiguity resolution
PPP Precise orbit and clock products Convergence and correction availability
SBAS Satellite-broadcast corrections Service coverage and system compatibility

Engineering Distinction

Higher positioning precision and navigation integrity are related but different concepts. Precision describes the quality of the position estimate. Integrity concerns whether the system can provide timely warnings when its information should not be trusted.

TECHNICAL CHALLENGES — 09

GNSS Vulnerabilities and Maritime Reliability

Satellite signals arrive at the Earth's surface with very low power. This makes GNSS receivers vulnerable to interference and other disruptions. A ship must therefore avoid treating GNSS as an infallible source of navigation information.

Jamming

Jamming occurs when interference overwhelms or disrupts GNSS signals, preventing the receiver from obtaining reliable measurements.

Spoofing

Spoofing involves transmitting deceptive signals intended to make a receiver calculate an incorrect position or time.

Jamming vs Spoofing

Jamming disrupts the ability to receive usable satellite signals. Spoofing attempts to deceive the receiver by presenting false navigation signals. The two threats require different detection and response strategies.

Operational Consequences

  • Incorrect vessel position information.
  • Loss of satellite-derived speed or timing data.
  • Incorrect position display on connected equipment.
  • Navigation alarms and degraded system performance.
  • Potential disruption to systems that depend on GNSS time.

Resilience and Cross-Checking

A reliable maritime navigation arrangement should not depend on a single source of position information. Bridge teams and system designers should use independent information sources where appropriate.

  • Compare GNSS position with radar and visual observations where possible.
  • Monitor receiver integrity and signal-quality indicators.
  • Use alternative positioning methods when satellite information becomes unreliable.
  • Maintain operational procedures for GNSS degradation or loss.
  • Investigate inconsistent data rather than assuming the displayed position is correct.

Cybersecurity Considerations

GNSS receivers and their connected interfaces are part of a larger electronic navigation environment. System design should consider secure configuration, software maintenance, access control, data validation, and the protection of navigation equipment from unauthorized changes.

FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS — 10

The Future of Satellite Navigation

Satellite navigation continues to evolve through new signals, advanced receiver architectures, improved correction services, and integration with other positioning technologies.

Multi-Constellation and Multi-Frequency Receivers

Receivers capable of using several constellations and signal frequencies can improve availability and help reduce certain measurement errors.

Sensor Fusion

Combining GNSS with inertial sensors, speed logs, radar observations, and other independent sources can help maintain a more consistent navigation solution when individual sensors become unreliable.

Intelligent Integrity Monitoring

Advanced algorithms can analyze signal behavior, receiver measurements, and independent sensor information to identify anomalies that may indicate degraded or deceptive GNSS data.

Resilient Positioning, Navigation and Timing

Future maritime systems are expected to emphasize resilience: maintaining safe operations when satellite signals are unavailable, inaccurate, or potentially manipulated.

Key Engineering Perspective

The future of maritime positioning is not simply about making GNSS more accurate. It is also about knowing when satellite data can be trusted, detecting when it cannot, and maintaining safe navigation through complementary systems.

LEARNING REVIEW

Key Knowledge to Remember

  • GNSS is the general family of global satellite navigation systems; GPS is one member of it.
  • GNSS receivers calculate position using satellite signals, timing measurements, and known orbital data.
  • A typical standalone three-dimensional solution needs at least four satellite measurements to estimate position and receiver clock offset.
  • Satellite geometry, atmospheric effects, multipath, and interference can affect accuracy.
  • DGNSS, RTK, PPP, and SBAS are different methods for improving positioning performance.
  • Jamming disrupts signals, while spoofing attempts to deceive the receiver.
  • GNSS signals do not normally provide direct positioning to submerged underwater vehicles.
  • Reliable maritime navigation requires cross-checking and alternative sources of information.
