AUTONOMOUS SHIPS — TOPIC 07

Remote Operations and Communication

Explore how remote operators supervise autonomous vessels, how shore control centres interact with ships, and how maritime communication networks support monitoring, decision-making, and operational safety.

Maritime Communication Remote Ship Operations Shore Control Centres Cybersecurity

1. Introduction to Remote Ship Operations

Remote ship operations involve monitoring, supervising, or controlling a vessel from a location outside the ship. This location may be a shore control centre, a remote operations facility, or another authorised control station.

Remote operations are particularly important in maritime automation because they allow qualified personnel to monitor vessel performance, review operational information, support decision-making, and intervene when necessary.

The level of remote involvement varies. Some ships may operate with onboard crews while receiving remote technical support. Other systems may allow shore-based operators to supervise specific functions or directly control certain vessel operations.

Remote Monitoring

Observing vessel position, machinery status, alarms, sensor information, and operational performance from shore.

Remote Assistance

Providing information, recommendations, and technical support to onboard personnel or autonomous systems.

Remote Control

Sending authorised commands from a remote location to control selected vessel functions.

Important distinction: Remote monitoring, remote assistance, and remote control are not the same thing. A vessel can be remotely monitored without being remotely controlled.

2. Shore Control Centres (SCCs)

A Shore Control Centre is a land-based facility used to monitor or control vessels operating at sea. It may provide operators with access to navigation information, machinery data, communication systems, alarms, and operational tools.

Depending on the operational design, a centre may supervise one vessel or coordinate the activities of several vessels. The number of vessels an operator can supervise safely depends on system complexity, workload, operational conditions, and the support available.

2.1 Main Components of a Shore Control Centre

  • Operator Workstations: Interfaces used to monitor vessel information and issue authorised commands.
  • Navigation Displays: Present position, routes, electronic charts, traffic information, and alerts.
  • Communication Systems: Maintain data and voice communication with the vessel and relevant services.
  • Monitoring Platforms: Display machinery performance, equipment status, and system health.
  • Alarm Management: Identifies abnormal conditions and helps operators prioritise responses.
  • Cybersecurity Infrastructure: Protects communication channels, systems, and operational access.
  • Backup Facilities: Support continuity during equipment or facility failures.

2.2 Operator Responsibilities

Remote operators may be responsible for reviewing vessel status, monitoring alarms, evaluating abnormal situations, communicating with the ship or other authorities, and initiating approved responses.

Their responsibilities must be clearly defined. The system should specify who has authority to issue commands, who is responsible for operational decisions, and how control is transferred when necessary.

2.3 Human-Machine Interface (HMI)

The Human-Machine Interface is the part of the system through which operators interact with vessel technology. A good HMI should present important information clearly and help operators understand the current state of the vessel.

  • Clear navigation and machinery displays.
  • Prioritised alarms and warnings.
  • Visible communication and connection status.
  • Clear indication of control authority.
  • Command confirmation and feedback.
  • Recorded actions and operational events.

A large number of screens does not necessarily create a better control centre. Poorly organised information can increase operator workload and delay important decisions.

3. Maritime Communication Architecture

Remote operations depend on communication links that allow information to travel between the vessel, shore control centre, and other authorised systems.

Communication architecture defines how data is collected, transmitted, received, protected, and delivered to the systems that need it.

3.1 Ship-to-Shore Communication

Ship-to-shore communication transfers information from the vessel to land-based facilities. Examples include position reports, equipment status, sensor information, alarms, operational records, and video feeds where supported.

3.2 Shore-to-Ship Communication

Shore-to-ship communication transfers information in the opposite direction. It may include operational instructions, route updates, authorised control commands, software updates, and requests for additional information.

Information collected onboard is processed and prepared for transmission. The communication network carries the information to the shore centre, where authorised personnel or software systems interpret it and decide whether further action is needed.

If an authorised command is sent back to the vessel, the onboard system must verify that the command is valid, permitted, and consistent with the vessel's safety requirements.

Communication should not be treated as an unrestricted path from a shore computer to a ship's actuators. Authentication, authorisation, command validation, and onboard safety protections are essential.

4. Maritime Communication Technologies

Ships operate across different geographic areas and environments. No single communication technology provides every capability under all conditions. Maritime communication systems therefore use different technologies according to coverage, capacity, reliability, and operational requirements.

4.1 Satellite Communication

Satellite communication enables ships to exchange information over large distances, including in areas far from coastal communication infrastructure.

Satellite services may support operational data, internet connectivity, voice communication, monitoring information, and other authorised applications.

Satellite systems differ in orbit, coverage, latency, capacity, cost, and service characteristics.

4.2 Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO)

Geostationary satellites orbit above the equator at an altitude of approximately 35,786 kilometres. Their orbital period matches Earth's rotation, making them appear stationary relative to a point on the Earth's surface.

GEO systems can provide broad coverage, but the long signal travel distance introduces noticeable communication latency.

4.3 Low Earth Orbit (LEO)

Low Earth Orbit satellites operate much closer to Earth than GEO satellites. Their shorter distance can reduce signal travel time, although coverage requires a constellation of satellites and coordination as satellites move across the sky.

4.4 Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)

Medium Earth Orbit satellites operate between LEO and GEO altitudes. They provide another option for satellite communication and navigation-related services, depending on the system design.

4.5 Terrestrial Communication

Near coastlines and in ports, terrestrial networks may provide communication through cellular systems, coastal radio stations, or other land-based infrastructure.

Cellular technologies can provide useful data connectivity when coverage is available. However, they cannot be assumed to provide continuous service across open oceans.

4.6 Maritime Radio Systems

Marine radio systems support ship-to-ship and ship-to-shore communication. Depending on the equipment and operating area, these may include VHF, MF, HF, and other radio services.

The Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS) uses designated communication and alerting arrangements to support maritime distress, urgency, safety, and search-and-rescue communication.

Different communication technologies are not automatically interchangeable. A system must be designed around its operational purpose, required availability, coverage, and applicable maritime requirements.

5. Communication Performance: Latency, Bandwidth and Reliability

A remote operation system must consider more than whether a communication connection exists. The quality and timing of transmitted information can directly affect operational decisions.

5.1 Latency

Latency is the time required for information to travel from its source to its destination. In remote ship operations, latency can affect how quickly an operator receives information and how quickly a command reaches the vessel.

Round-Trip Time (RTT) = Time from sending a message until its response is received

For example, if a remote operator sends a request and receives confirmation after a delay, the operator must know whether the information is still current enough to support the intended action.

5.2 Bandwidth

Bandwidth describes the capacity of a communication link to carry data over time. Video streams and large sensor datasets generally require more bandwidth than short status messages.

5.3 Reliability and Availability

Reliability concerns whether a communication system performs correctly over time. Availability concerns whether the service is operational and accessible when needed.

5.4 Packet Loss

Packet loss occurs when some transmitted data packets fail to reach their destination. Significant packet loss can interrupt video, delay information, or affect data exchange.

5.5 Jitter

Jitter is variation in packet arrival time. Even if the average delay is acceptable, inconsistent timing can affect real-time communication and control applications.

5.6 Link Availability

A vessel may move between satellite coverage areas, coastal networks, or different communication services. The system must manage changes in available links without creating unsafe interruptions.

A communication connection that is technically active may still be unsuitable for time-critical remote control. Latency, data freshness, packet loss, and system health must all be considered.

6. Communication Redundancy and Network Management

Communication redundancy means providing alternative ways to exchange information if a primary communication path becomes unavailable or unreliable.

6.1 Multiple Communication Links

A vessel may use a combination of satellite, cellular, and radio communication systems. The available combination depends on vessel design and operational requirements.

6.2 Automatic Link Switching

Some communication architectures can switch between available networks according to predefined policies. This process is often called failover or link switching.

The switching process must consider whether the alternative connection is authenticated, sufficiently reliable, and suitable for the data being transmitted.

6.3 Data Prioritisation

When bandwidth is limited, systems may prioritise information according to operational importance.

  • Critical safety alerts and emergency information.
  • Essential vessel status and navigation information.
  • Operational commands and confirmations.
  • Machinery monitoring information.
  • Non-essential video and general data.

6.4 Store-and-Forward Communication

Some non-time-critical information can be stored onboard and transmitted later when a suitable connection becomes available. This approach is useful for logs, reports, and selected operational records.

However, delayed transmission is not appropriate for every type of data. Time-critical safety information requires a different handling strategy.

6.5 Network Monitoring

Network management systems can monitor connection status, latency, bandwidth, packet loss, and other performance indicators. These measurements help determine whether a communication path remains suitable for its intended use.

7. Remote Control and Operational Authority

Remote control requires more than transmitting commands. The vessel and shore centre must have a clearly defined arrangement for control authority, command validation, acknowledgement, and emergency response.

7.1 Control Authority

Control authority identifies which person or system is currently authorised to control a particular vessel function. This may be an onboard operator, an automated onboard system, or an authorised remote operator.

7.2 Command Authentication

The vessel should verify the identity of the source sending a command. Authentication helps prevent unauthorised systems or individuals from issuing operational instructions.

7.3 Command Authorisation

Authentication alone is not enough. Authorisation determines whether the authenticated user or system has permission to perform the requested action.

7.4 Command Validation

The onboard system should evaluate whether a command is valid, correctly formatted, within permitted limits, and compatible with the vessel's current operating state.

7.5 Command Acknowledgement

The system should provide clear feedback indicating whether a command was received, accepted, rejected, or executed. An operator should not assume that a command has been successfully carried out merely because it was transmitted.

7.6 Transfer of Control

If control is transferred between onboard and remote systems, the transition must be clearly managed. The system should prevent conflicting commands and make the current control authority visible to relevant operators.

Control authority should be unambiguous. Two independent operators or systems issuing conflicting commands can create dangerous situations.

8. Loss of Communication and Safe Response

Communication loss is one of the most important failure scenarios in remote ship operations. It can occur because of equipment failure, coverage gaps, severe weather, interference, power problems, or cyber incidents.

8.1 Detecting Communication Failure

The vessel and shore centre may use connection monitoring, heartbeat messages, timeout thresholds, and link-health indicators to identify a failed or degraded connection.

8.2 Predefined Contingency Procedures

A vessel should have a defined response for different communication failure conditions. The appropriate action depends on the vessel's design, location, traffic situation, weather, operational mode, and available onboard capabilities.

8.3 Possible Safe Responses

  • Switch to an Alternative Link: Use another available communication channel if it is authorised and suitable.
  • Continue a Restricted Autonomous Mode: Continue only if the onboard system can safely manage the current situation under its approved operating limits.
  • Reduce Operational Complexity: Avoid starting non-essential manoeuvres or operations when supervision is unavailable.
  • Move to a Defined Safe Condition: Execute a pre-assessed response appropriate to the vessel and its environment.
  • Restore Contact: Attempt communication recovery through approved channels and procedures.

Critical safety point: There is no single universal response to communication loss. A vessel should not automatically stop in the middle of a traffic lane, continue its route indefinitely, or perform an arbitrary manoeuvre. Its response must be designed and validated for its operational context.

8.4 Graceful Degradation

Graceful degradation means that a system reduces or disables selected functions when important resources become unavailable, while attempting to preserve essential safety functions.

For example, if video transmission becomes unavailable, the vessel may still be able to send essential navigation status messages through a lower-bandwidth connection.

9. Cybersecurity in Remote Maritime Operations

Remote ship operations create communication paths between vessels, shore facilities, service providers, and external networks. These connections increase the importance of cybersecurity.

9.1 Common Cybersecurity Threats

  • Unauthorised Access: Attackers may attempt to access control systems or sensitive operational information.
  • Phishing: Personnel may be tricked into revealing credentials or opening malicious files.
  • Malware and Ransomware: Malicious software can disrupt computers, networks, and operational services.
  • Denial-of-Service Attacks: Attackers may attempt to make communication services unavailable.
  • Command Manipulation: Attackers may attempt to alter, inject, or replay unauthorised operational messages.
  • Data Interception: Sensitive information may be exposed if communication channels are not properly protected.

9.2 Cybersecurity Protection Measures

  • Strong authentication and multi-factor authentication.
  • Encryption for sensitive communication.
  • Network segmentation between operational and business systems.
  • Role-based access control and least privilege.
  • Secure software updates and vulnerability management.
  • Continuous monitoring and security logging.
  • Incident response and recovery procedures.
  • Regular cybersecurity training for personnel.

9.3 Operational Technology (OT) Security

Operational Technology refers to systems that monitor or control physical equipment and processes. In ships, OT may include propulsion control, steering systems, machinery automation, and other industrial control components.

OT security requires particular care because a cybersecurity action that is acceptable in an ordinary office network may disrupt a safety-critical vessel function.

Cybersecurity must protect not only information confidentiality but also the integrity and availability of systems that influence physical vessel operations.

10. Human Factors and Remote Operator Workload

Moving operational supervision from a ship to a shore centre changes how people interact with maritime systems. It does not remove the need for human judgement, training, and clearly defined responsibilities.

10.1 Situational Awareness

Situational awareness is the operator's understanding of what is happening, what it means, and what may happen next. Remote operators may have to build this understanding through electronic displays, sensor information, video, alarms, and communication with the vessel.

10.2 Workload and Fatigue

Monitoring multiple vessels or complex systems can create high mental workload. Long periods of monitoring may also lead to fatigue and reduced attention.

10.3 Alarm Fatigue

Alarm fatigue occurs when operators receive excessive, repetitive, or poorly prioritised alarms. Important warnings may be overlooked when they are mixed with large numbers of less important notifications.

10.4 Training and Competence

Remote operators require appropriate maritime knowledge, technical training, familiarity with control interfaces, and clear procedures for abnormal situations.

10.5 Human-Machine Teamwork

Autonomous systems and human operators must have clearly defined roles. Operators need to understand system capabilities, limitations, confidence levels, and the conditions under which human intervention is required.

Remote operation does not mean removing humans from maritime safety. It changes where human supervision takes place and how people interact with vessel systems.

11. Regulatory and Operational Considerations

Remote ship operations must consider applicable international conventions, flag-state requirements, coastal-state rules, classification requirements, and the vessel's approved operational arrangements.

11.1 IMO and Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships

The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has been developing an international regulatory framework for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS).

The IMO's non-mandatory MASS Code was adopted in May 2026 and is intended to provide a framework addressing safety and related considerations for autonomous ship operations. Its scope and applicability must be checked against the relevant ship type, voyage, and regulatory provisions.

Remote operation arrangements may involve questions about the role of remote operators, responsibility, communication reliability, control authority, and the relationship between onboard and shore-based functions.

11.2 GMDSS and Emergency Communication

The Global Maritime Distress and Safety System is an international maritime safety communication framework. It supports distress alerting, safety information, and communication arrangements for ships covered by the relevant requirements.

Remote operational communication should not be assumed to replace required distress and safety communication equipment or procedures.

11.3 Responsibility and Accountability

Operational arrangements must clearly address who is responsible for decisions and actions. Remote operation does not automatically transfer every legal responsibility from the shipowner, master, operator, or other relevant party to a shore control centre.

Regulatory requirements evolve. A technical learning page is not a substitute for checking current IMO instruments, flag-state rules, and the vessel's applicable approvals before real-world operation.

12. Practical Engineering Scenario

Consider an autonomous cargo vessel travelling on an international voyage. Its shore control centre monitors its route, machinery condition, and communication status.

Step 1 — Normal Operation

The vessel transmits position reports, navigation status, equipment information, and selected sensor data to the shore centre.

Step 2 — Communication Degradation

The primary satellite link experiences increasing latency and packet loss. The communication monitoring system identifies that the connection is becoming unreliable.

Step 3 — Network Response

The communication system evaluates whether an alternative authorised link is available. Essential operational information is prioritised.

Step 4 — Operator Awareness

The shore operator receives a warning that the primary connection is degraded and reviews the vessel's latest available status information.

Step 5 — Safe Operational Response

If the connection cannot be restored, the vessel follows its approved contingency procedure. Its onboard systems continue only those functions that are safe and supported under the defined operating conditions.

Step 6 — Recovery and Verification

After communication is restored, the shore centre and vessel verify connection integrity, exchange updated operational information, and confirm the current control authority before resuming any remote control activity.

This scenario demonstrates why remote operations require communication redundancy, onboard autonomy, clear control authority, and tested contingency procedures.

13. Engineering Challenges and Future Developments

Current Engineering Challenges

  • Maintaining reliable connectivity over long maritime distances.
  • Managing latency and limited communication capacity.
  • Protecting communication networks from cyber threats.
  • Maintaining clear control authority during system transitions.
  • Designing safe responses to communication loss.
  • Reducing remote operator workload and alarm fatigue.
  • Integrating equipment from different manufacturers.
  • Meeting technical and regulatory requirements across jurisdictions.

Future Developments

  • Hybrid Satellite Networks: Combining different satellite services and terrestrial networks to improve connectivity.
  • AI-Assisted Monitoring: Helping operators identify abnormal patterns and prioritise important events.
  • Predictive Communication Management: Anticipating network degradation and preparing alternative communication paths.
  • Advanced Digital Twins: Providing remote operators with richer models of vessel condition and behaviour.
  • Improved Cybersecurity: Applying stronger identity management, monitoring, and secure command validation.
  • Human-Centred Control Centres: Improving interfaces, alarm design, workload management, and operator decision support.

14. Key Takeaways

  • Remote operations allow authorised personnel to monitor, support, or control vessels from shore.
  • Remote monitoring is different from remote control.
  • Shore Control Centres provide interfaces and facilities for vessel supervision.
  • Ship-to-shore and shore-to-ship communication support information exchange.
  • Satellite, terrestrial, and radio systems provide different communication capabilities.
  • Latency, bandwidth, packet loss, jitter, and availability affect communication quality.
  • Redundant links and network monitoring can improve communication resilience.
  • Control authority and command validation must be clearly defined.
  • Communication-loss procedures must be designed for the vessel's actual operating context.
  • Cybersecurity and human factors are essential parts of safe remote operations.
