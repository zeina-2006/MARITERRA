01 / Understanding the Topic

What Is AI-Based Predictive Maintenance?

AI-based predictive maintenance uses data collected from machinery and intelligent analytical models to identify patterns associated with equipment deterioration and estimate when maintenance may be required.

Moving from Reactive Repairs to Data-Driven Maintenance

Marine vessels depend on complex mechanical and electrical systems. Main engines, auxiliary engines, pumps, compressors, generators, cooling systems, and propulsion components must operate reliably, often under demanding environmental conditions.

A machinery failure at sea can cause operational delays, expensive repairs, safety risks, and in serious cases, loss of propulsion or essential services.

Traditional maintenance plans often rely on fixed time intervals, equipment hours, or inspections. Predictive maintenance adds another source of information: the actual condition and behavior of the equipment.

AI models analyze measurements such as vibration, temperature, pressure, oil condition, electrical current, and operating load. They may detect unusual patterns, classify possible faults, or estimate how equipment condition is changing.

The Main Objective

The purpose is not simply to predict that a machine will fail. A useful predictive maintenance system should help engineers answer practical questions:

What is changing? Is the equipment behaving differently from its normal operating condition?

Where is the problem? Which component or subsystem may be responsible for the abnormal behavior?

How serious is it? Does the observation indicate a minor deviation or a potentially critical condition?

What should happen next? Should the engineer inspect, monitor, reduce load, or schedule maintenance?

Predictive maintenance does not eliminate failures or replace engineering inspections. Its value is in improving the timing and quality of maintenance decisions using additional evidence.
02 / Maintenance Strategies

Understanding the Different Maintenance Approaches

Predictive maintenance is easier to understand when compared with other maintenance strategies used in marine engineering.

1. Reactive Maintenance

Reactive maintenance is performed after equipment fails or becomes unable to perform its required function.

For example, if a seawater cooling pump stops working unexpectedly, engineers may need to repair or replace the pump after the failure.

This approach may be reasonable for low-cost, non-critical items, but it can be risky when applied to essential vessel systems.

2. Preventive Maintenance

Preventive maintenance is performed at predetermined intervals based on time, operating hours, manufacturer recommendations, or established maintenance schedules.

An auxiliary engine may receive a scheduled inspection after a specified number of operating hours, even if no abnormal condition has been detected.

Preventive maintenance helps reduce unexpected failures, but it may lead to unnecessary maintenance if the equipment remains in good condition.

3. Condition-Based Maintenance

Condition-based maintenance uses actual equipment condition information to determine when maintenance should be considered.

Engineers may monitor vibration, temperature, pressure, oil condition, or other measurements and schedule inspection when the readings indicate deterioration.

4. Predictive Maintenance

Predictive maintenance uses condition information and analytical methods to estimate future equipment behavior, potential faults, or maintenance needs.

AI can identify complex patterns that may be difficult to detect using simple thresholds, particularly when several sensor measurements interact.

03 / Marine Engineering Systems

Which Ship Systems Can Be Monitored?

Predictive maintenance can be applied to many onboard systems. The selected monitoring variables depend on the equipment, its failure modes, operating conditions, and safety importance.

⚙️

Main Propulsion Engine

Monitoring engine temperature, cylinder pressure, vibration, lubrication, exhaust gas conditions, and operating load.

🔌

Auxiliary Engines & Generators

Monitoring generator load, voltage, current, bearing condition, temperature, and vibration.

💧

Pumps & Piping Systems

Monitoring flow rate, pressure, motor current, vibration, and signs of leakage or reduced performance.

❄️

Cooling Systems

Monitoring inlet and outlet temperatures, pressure differences, flow rates, and heat exchanger performance.

🌀

Propulsion & Rotating Equipment

Monitoring shaft vibration, bearing temperature, alignment, rotational speed, and mechanical loads.

🛢️

Lubrication Systems

Monitoring oil pressure, temperature, contamination, viscosity-related indicators, and wear particles.

Example: Main Engine Bearing Monitoring

Bearings support rotating shafts and help reduce friction. Abnormal lubrication, misalignment, excessive load, contamination, or mechanical wear may contribute to bearing deterioration.

A monitoring system may collect vibration and temperature measurements while the engine operates. An analytical model can compare the measurements with expected behavior under similar operating conditions.

A persistent abnormal pattern may trigger an inspection recommendation. Engineers then use additional measurements, operating history, and physical inspection to determine the actual condition.

04 / Data Acquisition

Sensors & Machinery Condition Data

Sensors are the foundation of condition monitoring. They transform physical operating conditions into measurable signals that can be analyzed by monitoring systems and predictive models.

Vibration Sensors

Accelerometers measure vibration and acceleration. Their signals can help engineers investigate imbalance, misalignment, looseness, bearing defects, and other mechanical conditions.

Frequency-domain analysis, including Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), can reveal frequency components associated with rotating machinery. However, interpreting these signals requires knowledge of machine speed, bearing geometry, installation location, and operating load.

Temperature Sensors

Temperature measurements can reveal overheating, cooling inefficiency, excessive friction, or abnormal operating conditions.

A temperature reading should be interpreted in context. An engine operating under high load may have a different normal temperature range from an engine operating at low load.

Pressure & Flow Sensors

Pressure and flow measurements are important for monitoring lubrication, cooling, fuel delivery, hydraulic, and pumping systems.

Unexpected pressure loss or a change in flow rate may indicate blockage, leakage, pump degradation, or a change in operating conditions.

Oil Condition Monitoring

Lubricating oil can provide information about machinery health. Oil analysis may identify contamination, changes in properties, and wear-related particles.

Laboratory analysis and onboard oil monitoring methods can complement sensor-based predictive systems.

Electrical Measurements

Voltage, current, power, and frequency measurements can support monitoring of electric motors, generators, and electrical distribution systems.

Motor current signature analysis is one technique that can help investigate certain motor and mechanical abnormalities.

Sensor readings are not direct proof of a specific failure. Sensor placement, calibration, sampling rate, environmental noise, and operating conditions all influence the quality of the collected information.
05 / Artificial Intelligence Techniques

How AI Detects Machinery Problems

1. Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection identifies measurements or patterns that differ significantly from expected operating behavior.

For example, a pump may normally produce a stable vibration pattern under a specific operating load. A new abnormal pattern may indicate a developing problem.

Anomaly detection is useful when historical examples of every possible failure type are not available.

2. Classification Models

Classification models assign observations to predefined categories.

A trained model may classify machinery data into categories such as normal operation, possible imbalance, suspected bearing issue, or abnormal temperature condition.

The reliability of the classification depends on the quality, representativeness, and correct labeling of the training data.

3. Regression Models

Regression estimates numerical values. In predictive maintenance, it may be used to estimate remaining useful life, future vibration levels, or the expected progression of a condition indicator.

4. Neural Networks

Neural networks can learn complex relationships from sensor measurements. They may analyze combinations of vibration, temperature, pressure, and operating load.

Deep learning methods can process complex signals, but they usually require substantial representative data and careful validation.

5. Autoencoders

Autoencoders are neural network architectures that learn to represent and reconstruct input data.

In some anomaly detection applications, an autoencoder is trained primarily on normal operating data. Unusual inputs may produce larger reconstruction errors.

Reconstruction Error = Difference between Input and Reconstructed Output

A high reconstruction error can be a warning signal, but it does not automatically identify the exact mechanical fault.

6. Hybrid Diagnostic Systems

A hybrid approach combines engineering knowledge, statistical analysis, and machine learning.

For example, engineering rules may identify unsafe pressure limits, while an anomaly detection model investigates more subtle changes in vibration patterns.

Combining these approaches can improve practical usefulness, provided that the system is validated and its outputs are interpreted correctly.

Compare predictions with actual maintenance findings.

Why Maintenance Feedback Matters

After a maintenance activity, engineers should record the actual findings. For example, a suspected bearing problem may be confirmed by inspection, or the inspection may reveal that the warning was caused by a loose sensor.

These records help evaluate the model and improve future analysis. Without feedback, the system may continue producing warnings that are not useful in practice.

07 / Maritime Applications

Predictive Maintenance Across Ship Operations

Main Engine Health Monitoring

AI models may analyze cylinder-related measurements, exhaust gas temperature, lubrication pressure, vibration, and engine load to identify abnormal operating patterns.

This can help engineers investigate possible combustion, lubrication, cooling, or mechanical issues.

Auxiliary Generator Monitoring

Generators are essential for supplying electrical power to onboard equipment. Monitoring electrical and mechanical measurements can help identify unusual operating conditions before they lead to unexpected interruptions.

Pump and Cooling System Monitoring

AI-based monitoring can analyze pressure, flow, temperature, and motor behavior to identify potential deterioration in pumps and cooling systems.

Propulsion System Monitoring

Rotating components, bearings, shafts, and propulsion equipment can be monitored through vibration and other condition indicators.

Early detection of abnormal behavior may help engineers plan detailed inspections and reduce the risk of unexpected operational disruption.

Shipboard Electrical Systems

Electrical monitoring can support the detection of unusual current, temperature, voltage, and power behavior in selected motors, generators, and distribution equipment.

Fleet-Wide Maintenance Planning

When information from several vessels is available, fleet operators may compare equipment performance, maintenance histories, and recurring problems.

This can support maintenance planning and spare-parts management. However, comparisons must account for differences in vessel design, equipment configuration, and operating profile.

08 / Prognostics

Remaining Useful Life (RUL) Estimation

What Is Remaining Useful Life?

Remaining Useful Life, commonly abbreviated as RUL, is an estimate of the remaining operating time or usage before an asset or component reaches a defined failure or end-of-life condition.

For example, a model may estimate that a monitored component has approximately 500 operating hours remaining before it reaches a specified maintenance threshold.

This estimate is not a guaranteed failure date. It depends on the definition of failure, the component's condition, operating conditions, maintenance actions, and model uncertainty.

Degradation-Based Estimation

Some components gradually deteriorate. A model may track a condition indicator over time and estimate when it will reach a predefined threshold.

Estimated RUL = Predicted Threshold Time − Current Time

This simplified relationship illustrates the concept. Real RUL estimation may require probabilistic models, degradation physics, historical failure data, and uncertainty analysis.

Factors Affecting RUL

Operating load: Higher or fluctuating loads may influence component wear.

Operating temperature: Thermal conditions can affect material behavior and lubrication performance.

Maintenance history: Repairs, adjustments, and replacements change the condition of equipment.

Environmental conditions: Corrosion, humidity, salt exposure, and contamination may influence deterioration.

Operating profile: Different vessels and routes may expose similar components to different stresses.

An RUL estimate must not be treated as permission to continue operating unsafe machinery. Mandatory inspections, alarm limits, manufacturer instructions, and safety procedures remain applicable.
09 / Critical Engineering Issues

Challenges & Limitations

📂

Limited Failure Data

Critical equipment may fail infrequently, making it difficult to collect enough labeled failure examples.

🌊

Harsh Marine Conditions

Vibration, humidity, salt exposure, temperature, and motion can affect sensors and measurement quality.

🔧

Different Equipment Types

Similar components may behave differently because of design, age, load, or maintenance history.

🚨

False Alarms

Excessive incorrect warnings can reduce trust in the system and waste maintenance resources.

🔐

Cybersecurity

Connected monitoring infrastructure must be protected from unauthorized access and data manipulation.

🧠

Explainability

Engineers need understandable evidence behind warnings and recommendations.

False Positives and False Negatives

A false positive occurs when a system reports a fault or abnormal condition even though the equipment does not have the suspected problem.

A false negative occurs when a real problem is present but the system fails to identify it.

Both can have serious consequences. False positives may lead to unnecessary inspections and reduced trust, while false negatives may allow developing faults to remain undetected.

Model Drift and Maintenance Changes

A model trained on one operating profile may become less reliable after equipment replacement, major repairs, changes in engine settings, or changes in operating conditions.

Predictive maintenance systems therefore require performance monitoring, engineering review, and controlled model updates.

10 / The Next Generation

The Future of Intelligent Marine Maintenance

Digital Twins for Marine Machinery

Digital twins can combine equipment models with information from physical machinery. When connected to appropriate sensor data, they can help represent equipment condition and explore possible operating scenarios.

Edge AI Onboard Ships

Edge AI processes data close to the equipment instead of sending every measurement to a remote server.

This can reduce communication delays and support monitoring when satellite connectivity is limited. However, onboard hardware, power, reliability, and cybersecurity must be considered.

Integrated Fleet Intelligence

Future systems may combine machinery health, maintenance records, vessel operating data, and fleet-level analytics to support more coordinated maintenance planning.

Human-Centered AI Maintenance

More advanced systems may explain why a warning was generated, identify the measurements that influenced it, and present relevant maintenance history to engineers.

The objective is to strengthen engineering judgment, not to remove professional responsibility.
