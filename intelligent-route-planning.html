Smart Navigation / Topic 05

Intelligent Route Planning

Discover how maritime route planning combines navigational data, weather forecasts, ocean conditions, vessel performance, and intelligent algorithms to support safer and more efficient voyages.

01 / INTRODUCTION

What Is Intelligent Route Planning?

Intelligent Route Planning is the process of selecting and evaluating a vessel's intended route using navigational information, environmental conditions, operational requirements, and digital decision-support systems.

Traditional route planning depends heavily on nautical charts, publications, the navigator's experience, and established procedures. Modern systems can enhance this process by analyzing large amounts of data and comparing alternative routes before and during a voyage.

The purpose is not simply to find the shortest distance. A suitable route must consider navigational safety, vessel limitations, weather, traffic, fuel consumption, schedule requirements, and environmental protection.

Core principle: Route optimization is a decision-support process. The technology can identify options and risks, but it does not replace the master's responsibility or the navigator's professional judgment.

02 / OBJECTIVES

Main Objectives of Route Planning

Navigation Safety

Identify shallow water, underwater obstructions, restricted areas, traffic separation schemes, and other navigational hazards before the vessel reaches them.

Time Efficiency

Evaluate possible routes and vessel speeds to meet arrival windows while accounting for weather, currents, traffic, and operational restrictions.

Fuel Optimization

Reduce unnecessary fuel consumption by considering distance, speed, wind resistance, wave conditions, currents, and engine performance.

Environmental Protection

Consider emission reduction, sensitive marine areas, protected zones, and measures that help prevent pollution or environmental damage.

Operational Planning

Account for port approaches, pilot boarding arrangements, reporting requirements, vessel traffic services, and expected operational constraints.

Risk Management

Identify uncertain conditions and prepare alternative routes, safe waiting areas, and contingency actions when the original plan becomes unsuitable.

03 / VOYAGE PLANNING

The Voyage Planning Process

A voyage plan should cover the complete passage from berth to berth. The IMO Guidelines for Voyage Planning describe four connected stages: appraisal, planning, execution, and monitoring.

01 Appraisal Collect and evaluate relevant information.
02 Planning Prepare and verify the intended route.
03 Execution Navigate according to the approved plan.
04 Monitoring Continuously check progress and conditions.

Appraisal: Information Collection

The planning team gathers information relevant to the voyage, including:

  • Updated nautical charts and nautical publications.
  • Notices to Mariners and navigational warnings.
  • Weather forecasts and oceanographic information.
  • Tides, tidal streams, and ocean currents.
  • Ship dimensions, draught, manoeuvring characteristics, and limitations.
  • Traffic conditions, routeing systems, and reporting requirements.
  • Port information, pilotage, and emergency arrangements.

Planning: Route Development

The intended route is developed using appropriate charts and relevant information. Each leg should be evaluated for its navigational and operational suitability.

  • Define waypoints and planned courses.
  • Set suitable safety parameters and margins.
  • Identify no-go areas and navigational hazards.
  • Consider safe speeds and under-keel clearance.
  • Identify reporting points and pilotage areas.
  • Prepare contingency options.

Execution: Putting the Plan into Practice

The vessel follows the verified voyage plan while the bridge team considers changing conditions and maintains effective communication.

Monitoring: Continuous Verification

The vessel's actual position and progress are compared with the intended route. Deviations, changing hazards, and new information must be assessed promptly.

Important: A route should not be treated as permanently safe simply because it was checked before departure. Weather, traffic, navigational warnings, and operational circumstances can change during the voyage.

04 / ROUTE DATA

Data Used in Intelligent Route Planning

Geographical and Hydrographic Data

Electronic Navigational Charts (ENCs), water depths, coastlines, seabed information, wrecks, obstructions, navigational aids, and restricted areas help determine where a vessel can safely navigate.

Weather Data

Wind speed and direction, atmospheric pressure, visibility, precipitation, storm forecasts, and wave conditions influence route selection and vessel performance.

Oceanographic Data

Tidal streams, ocean currents, water temperature, and sea-state information help estimate the effect of the marine environment on the vessel's movement.

Vessel Performance Data

Draught, displacement, speed, propulsion characteristics, fuel consumption, manoeuvring capability, and operational limits influence the suitability of each route.

Traffic and Routeing Information

Traffic Separation Schemes (TSS), recommended tracks, deep-water routes, areas to be avoided, ship reporting systems, and Vessel Traffic Services (VTS) must be considered where applicable.

Operational and Regulatory Data

Port schedules, pilot boarding arrangements, local restrictions, environmental protection measures, and applicable international and national requirements affect route decisions.

05 / WEATHER ROUTING

Weather Routing and Ocean Conditions

Weather routing is the use of meteorological and oceanographic information to evaluate routes that may reduce exposure to adverse conditions or improve voyage efficiency.

Wind and waves can increase resistance and affect vessel motion. Ocean currents can either assist or oppose the vessel's movement. Their combined effects influence voyage time, fuel consumption, comfort, and operational safety.

Wind and Wave Effects

Head winds and waves may increase resistance and reduce speed. Beam and following seas can affect vessel motion differently depending on hull design, loading condition, and stability characteristics.

Ocean Current Effects

A favourable current may increase speed over ground, while an adverse current may reduce it. Current forecasts are estimates and should be checked against observed conditions.

Storm Avoidance

Weather routing tools can compare forecast conditions along alternative routes and help identify areas where severe weather exposure may be reduced.

Forecast Uncertainty

Weather forecasts are not exact predictions. Their reliability changes with forecast range, location, model quality, and the development of weather systems.

A longer route may sometimes be preferable if it avoids severe weather or reduces operational risk. However, avoiding bad weather must be assessed against the full voyage plan, vessel limitations, and available alternatives.

06 / ECDIS AND DIGITAL TOOLS

ECDIS and Digital Route Planning

The Electronic Chart Display and Information System (ECDIS) supports route planning and monitoring by displaying official electronic chart information together with the vessel's navigational data.

Route Creation

Navigators can create a route by defining waypoints and connecting them with straight or curved route segments, depending on the system's capabilities.

Safety Parameters

ECDIS settings can include safety depth, safety contour, cross-track distance (XTD), and other parameters used to assess the planned route.

Route Checking

The route-checking function can identify charted dangers and areas that may conflict with configured safety parameters along the checked route corridor.

Route Monitoring

During the voyage, ECDIS can display the planned route, vessel position, and relevant warnings or indications to support the bridge team.

A critical limitation: An ECDIS route check is not a guarantee that a route is safe. Its result depends on chart data, chart quality, selected parameters, route corridor width, and system configuration. A thorough visual review and professional assessment remain necessary.

07 / OPTIMIZATION

Route Optimization Methods

Route optimization compares possible routes against selected objectives and operational constraints. The result depends on the quality of the input data and the priorities assigned to each objective.

Shortest-Distance Routing

Searches for a route that minimizes geographical distance. The shortest route is not automatically the safest, fastest, or most economical option.

Minimum-Time Routing

Evaluates routes and speeds to reduce estimated travel time while considering environmental effects, vessel performance, and navigational constraints.

Fuel-Efficient Routing

Evaluates the expected fuel required for different route and speed combinations. It can consider propulsion performance, weather, and currents.

Weather-Optimized Routing

Compares routes based on forecast wind, waves, currents, and expected vessel responses to reduce exposure to undesirable conditions.

Multi-Objective Optimization

Considers more than one objective, such as safety, time, fuel consumption, emissions, and arrival requirements.

Constrained Routing

Searches for suitable routes while respecting navigational restrictions, vessel draught, environmental rules, traffic measures, and operational limitations.

Route Quality ≠ Shortest Distance

A route is evaluated against multiple objectives and constraints, not distance alone.

Example: Comparing Route Priorities

Planning Objective Main Priority Important Consideration
Shortest route Distance May pass through difficult or restricted areas.
Fastest route Travel time Depends on speed, currents, weather, and vessel performance.
Fuel-efficient route Fuel consumption May require a longer distance or different speed.
Safety-focused route Risk reduction Must account for hazards and vessel limitations.
Multi-objective route Balanced performance Requires carefully defined priorities and constraints.
08 / ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Artificial Intelligence in Route Planning

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning can support maritime route planning by processing complex datasets, recognizing patterns, estimating conditions, and comparing possible decisions.

Predictive Analysis

Machine learning models can analyze historical and current data to estimate fuel consumption, voyage duration, or selected environmental conditions.

Intelligent Route Search

Search and optimization algorithms can evaluate multiple route alternatives while accounting for defined constraints and objectives.

Dynamic Replanning

Decision-support systems can evaluate whether a revised route may be appropriate when new weather, traffic, or navigational information becomes available.

Anomaly Detection

Data analysis can help identify unusual patterns in vessel performance, estimated fuel use, or environmental information that may require investigation.

Examples of AI and Optimization Techniques

Machine Learning

Learns patterns from historical and operational data to support prediction and estimation tasks.

Genetic Algorithms

Explore candidate solutions through selection, combination, and mutation to search for improved route options.

Graph Search Algorithms

Represent navigable areas as connected nodes and edges, then search for paths that satisfy selected costs and constraints.

Predictive Models

Estimate possible future conditions or vessel performance to help compare route alternatives.

AI recommendations can be wrong when input data is outdated, incomplete, biased, or outside the model's training conditions. An AI-generated route must be validated against current charts, official information, applicable regulations, and professional judgment.

09 / SAFETY AND RISK

Safety, Regulations, and Risk Assessment

Collision Avoidance

Route planning must account for traffic conditions and applicable collision-prevention rules. A planned track does not remove the need for continuous lookout and collision-risk assessment.

Under-Keel Clearance

Available water depth must be evaluated in relation to the vessel's draught, tidal conditions, squat, water density where relevant, and required safety margins.

Restricted and Protected Areas

The route must consider prohibited areas, areas to be avoided, traffic separation schemes, marine protected areas, and other applicable restrictions.

Contingency Planning

Alternative routes, safe anchorages, places of refuge, and emergency arrangements should be considered according to the voyage and its risks.

Data Quality

Incorrect chart information, uncertain forecasts, poor sensor data, or outdated navigational warnings can affect route decisions. Data quality must be assessed, not simply assumed.

Human Oversight

The bridge team must understand the route, evaluate warnings, verify system settings, and respond to changing circumstances. Automation does not remove professional responsibility.

Regulatory foundation: IMO Resolution A.893(21) provides Guidelines for Voyage Planning. Route planning must also consider applicable SOLAS requirements, COLREGs, ships' routeing measures, and relevant national and local requirements.

10 / PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS

Practical Maritime Applications

SCENARIO 01

Planning a Long-Distance Commercial Voyage

A cargo vessel is preparing for an international voyage. The planning team compares alternative routes using navigational charts, weather forecasts, current information, vessel performance data, and port requirements.

The selected plan must balance safety, estimated arrival time, fuel efficiency, and operational constraints.

SCENARIO 02

Responding to an Approaching Storm

During a voyage, updated forecasts indicate that a severe weather system may affect the planned track. The bridge team evaluates alternative routes and vessel responses before deciding whether a deviation is necessary.

Any change must be assessed, communicated, and handled in accordance with the ship's procedures and the master's authority.

SCENARIO 03

Reducing Fuel Consumption

A vessel operator compares different speed and route combinations. A route with a slightly greater distance may offer better current conditions or lower resistance, potentially reducing total fuel consumption.

The final decision must also consider schedule commitments, safety, machinery limitations, and forecast uncertainty.

SCENARIO 04

Approaching a Busy Port

A vessel approaching a congested port must consider traffic separation schemes, local navigation rules, pilot boarding arrangements, reporting points, tidal conditions, and available water depth.

The approach plan must be detailed and continuously monitored as the vessel enters a more constrained navigational environment.

11 / LIMITATIONS

Challenges and Limitations

Uncertain Forecasts

Weather and ocean forecasts contain uncertainty. A route that appears optimal using one forecast may become unsuitable as conditions change.

Incomplete or Outdated Data

Missing chart information, delayed warnings, incorrect vessel data, or unreliable sensor inputs can reduce the quality of route recommendations.

Model Limitations

Optimization models depend on assumptions about vessel behaviour, fuel use, environmental conditions, and operational constraints. Simplified models may not represent real-world conditions accurately.

System Integration

Different navigation, weather, and operational systems may use different data formats, update rates, or reference information.

Cybersecurity Risks

Connected systems may be exposed to data manipulation, unauthorized access, or service disruption. Navigation data and system integrity require protection.

Human Factors

Overreliance on automated recommendations, alarm overload, or misunderstanding system limitations can contribute to unsafe decisions.

12 / FUTURE DEVELOPMENT

The Future of Intelligent Route Planning

Maritime route planning is moving toward more connected, data-driven, and predictive decision-support environments. Improvements in artificial intelligence, satellite communications, digital charts, weather models, and vessel monitoring can make voyage planning more responsive.

  • Advanced AI: More capable prediction and route-comparison systems.
  • Digital Twins: Virtual vessel models that can help evaluate performance under different conditions.
  • Real-Time Data: Better integration of weather, traffic, and vessel information.
  • Autonomous Shipping: More advanced route-planning support for remotely operated and autonomous vessels.
  • Energy Optimization: Better analysis of fuel use, emissions, speed, and propulsion performance.
  • Decision Support: Systems that explain recommendations, uncertainty, and operational trade-offs more clearly.

Future systems may become increasingly autonomous, but safe deployment requires validated technology, reliable communications, cybersecurity, regulatory compliance, and clearly defined human responsibilities.
