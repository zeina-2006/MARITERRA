Navigation Sensors & Integrated Bridge Systems

Discover how maritime sensors measure vessel position, heading, speed, depth, and movement, and how Integrated Bridge Systems combine information from different onboard navigation systems to support safer and more coordinated maritime operations.

TOPIC 03 Navigation Sensors Integrated Bridge Systems
01 / Foundations

Understanding Maritime Navigation Sensors

Navigation sensors are devices that measure or detect physical and geographical information related to a vessel and its surrounding environment. Their measurements help navigation systems determine where a vessel is, how it is moving, and what conditions exist around it.

What Is a Navigation Sensor?

A navigation sensor is an instrument that collects information from the vessel or its environment and converts it into data that can be interpreted by navigators or processed by onboard electronic systems.

Different sensors measure different physical quantities. A GNSS receiver calculates geographical position from satellite signals, while a gyrocompass measures the vessel's orientation relative to true north.

Other instruments measure speed, depth, angular movement, and environmental conditions. These measurements can be displayed independently or distributed to other onboard equipment through suitable interfaces.

Why Are Navigation Sensors Important?

Position Awareness

Position sensors help determine the vessel's geographical location and support route monitoring.

Movement Monitoring

Heading, speed, and rate-of-turn information help officers understand how the vessel is moving.

Environmental Awareness

Depth and environmental measurements provide additional information about the surrounding maritime environment.

System Integration

Sensor outputs can be shared with navigation equipment to support coordinated operations.

Measured Data and Calculated Information

An important engineering distinction is that not every navigation value displayed on a bridge is a direct measurement.

  • Measured data: Information obtained directly from a sensor, such as angular velocity or water depth.
  • Calculated data: Information derived by processing one or more measurements, such as a calculated geographical position.
  • Derived navigation information: Values such as course over ground or estimated movement based on available sensor data.

Key concept: A displayed value is only useful when its source, meaning, accuracy, and current validity are understood.

02 / Position and Heading

Position and Heading Sensors

Position and heading are two fundamental categories of navigation information. They describe different aspects of a vessel's geographical location and orientation.

1. Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)

GNSS is a general term for satellite navigation constellations that provide positioning, navigation, and timing services.

Examples include GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, and BeiDou. A GNSS receiver processes signals from multiple satellites to estimate its geographical position and time.

The receiver normally calculates position by measuring signal travel-time information and solving for its location relative to known satellite positions.

GNSS Applications on Ships

  • Providing geographical position to navigation systems.
  • Supporting route monitoring and voyage planning.
  • Providing position information to compatible ECDIS equipment.
  • Supporting AIS position reporting when connected as the relevant position source.
  • Providing timing information to compatible onboard systems.

GNSS Limitations

Satellite positioning depends on receiving usable signals. Its performance can be affected by signal obstruction, atmospheric conditions, multipath reflections, interference, jamming, and spoofing.

For this reason, bridge teams should not assume that every displayed GNSS position is automatically correct. Independent cross-checks are important, especially in restricted waters.

2. Gyrocompass

A gyrocompass is a navigation instrument that determines true north using gyroscopic principles and the Earth's rotation.

It provides heading information that can be distributed to other navigation equipment, including radar, AIS, autopilot, and integrated bridge systems.

Unlike a magnetic compass, a gyrocompass is not based on the Earth's magnetic field. However, it still requires appropriate installation, calibration, maintenance, and monitoring.

3. Magnetic Compass

A magnetic compass uses the Earth's magnetic field to indicate magnetic north. It provides an independent heading reference and is an important part of traditional maritime navigation.

Its readings can be affected by magnetic variation, deviation caused by magnetic influences aboard the vessel, and nearby magnetic equipment.

True Heading, Magnetic Heading, COG and SOG

These navigation terms must not be treated as interchangeable. Each describes a different property of vessel movement or orientation.

Term Meaning Typical Source
True Heading The direction the vessel's bow points relative to true north. Gyrocompass
Magnetic Heading The vessel's bow direction relative to magnetic north. Magnetic compass
COG The actual direction of the vessel's movement over the Earth's surface. GNSS-derived information
SOG The vessel's speed over the Earth's surface. GNSS-derived information

Important example: A vessel can point north while moving northeast because of current or wind. Its heading and course over ground are therefore not necessarily identical.

03 / Speed and Movement

Speed, Distance and Motion Sensors

Speed and motion sensors help the bridge team understand how fast a vessel is travelling, how far it has moved, and how its direction or orientation is changing.

1. Speed Log

A speed log is a navigation instrument used to measure vessel speed. Different speed-log technologies use different physical principles.

Some systems measure speed relative to the surrounding water, while others are designed to determine speed relative to the seabed.

Speed Through Water (STW)

Speed through water describes the vessel's movement relative to the surrounding water.

It is useful for understanding the vessel's movement through the water mass and for certain navigational and operational calculations.

Speed Over Ground (SOG)

Speed over ground describes how fast the vessel moves relative to the Earth's surface.

It is commonly calculated from successive GNSS position measurements.

Why can they differ? Ocean currents can cause the vessel's speed through water to differ from its speed over ground. Wind and other environmental forces can also influence the vessel's actual movement.

2. Rate-of-Turn Indicator

A Rate-of-Turn Indicator (ROT) measures how quickly a vessel is changing its heading, normally expressed in degrees per minute.

This information helps officers assess turning behaviour and monitor manoeuvres, particularly in confined waters and during course alterations.

3. Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)

An Inertial Measurement Unit is an electronic sensor assembly that typically contains accelerometers and gyroscopes.

Accelerometers measure linear acceleration, while gyroscopes measure angular velocity around one or more axes.

IMU measurements can support motion monitoring, attitude estimation, stabilization, and navigation systems. An IMU alone does not automatically provide an accurate long-term geographical position.

Comparing Speed and Motion Sensors

Instrument Primary Measurement Navigation Use
Speed Log Speed through water or over ground, depending on technology. Speed and distance monitoring.
Rate-of-Turn Indicator Angular turning rate. Monitoring course alterations.
IMU Linear acceleration and angular velocity. Motion estimation and sensor integration.
GNSS Receiver Satellite-derived position and timing. Position, SOG and COG calculations.
04 / Environmental Sensors

Depth and Environmental Measurement

Environmental sensors provide additional information about the conditions surrounding a vessel. This information is important for safe navigation, operational planning, and understanding the maritime environment.

1. Echo Sounder

An echo sounder is an instrument used to measure water depth beneath a vessel by transmitting acoustic pulses and measuring the time taken for the returning echo.

The system normally uses a transducer installed in the hull or another suitable location. The transducer converts electrical energy into acoustic energy and receives returning acoustic signals.

How Echo Sounding Works

  1. The transducer generates an acoustic pulse.
  2. The pulse travels through the water toward the seabed.
  3. The seabed reflects part of the transmitted energy.
  4. The transducer receives the returning echo.
  5. The system measures the elapsed travel time.
  6. The instrument estimates depth using the assumed or configured speed of sound in water.

A simplified depth calculation is:

Depth = (Sound Velocity × Travel Time) ÷ 2

The division by two accounts for the acoustic pulse travelling to the seabed and returning to the transducer.

The displayed depth depends on the instrument's reference setting, such as transducer location or another configured reference. Navigators must understand which reference is being used.

2. Anemometer

An anemometer measures wind speed. Some marine systems also provide wind direction information through a combined wind sensor arrangement.

Wind measurements help bridge teams assess environmental forces that can affect vessel handling, berthing, and manoeuvring.

3. Weather and Environmental Sensors

Depending on vessel type and operational requirements, onboard environmental instruments may measure:

  • Air temperature.
  • Atmospheric pressure.
  • Relative humidity.
  • Wind speed and direction.
  • Sea temperature.
  • Other operational environmental parameters.

4. Water-Level and Current Information

Water-level and current information may be obtained through dedicated sensors, external data services, hydrographic products, or other navigation information systems.

These data sources can support the assessment of under-keel clearance, expected vessel movement, and local navigation conditions.

Technical limitation: An echo sounder measures depth beneath its transducer according to its configured reference. It does not independently provide a complete map of underwater terrain.

05 / Data Communication

Sensor Interfaces and Data Exchange

Navigation equipment must exchange information in a compatible format before integrated systems can use the data. Interfaces and communication protocols make this exchange possible.

What Is a Maritime Data Interface?

A data interface is a connection method that allows electronic devices to transmit and receive information. It defines how information is represented and exchanged between connected equipment.

Navigation sensors may provide information to displays, computers, alarms, autopilots, and other bridge equipment. Compatible interfaces allow these systems to interpret the transmitted data.

IEC 61162

The IEC 61162 series defines digital interfaces used by maritime navigation and radiocommunication equipment. Different parts of the series address different communication requirements.

IEC 61162-1

Addresses digital communication using a single talker and multiple listeners. It is associated with serial maritime data exchange.

IEC 61162-2

Addresses higher-speed serial data communication for suitable maritime applications.

IEC 61162-450

Addresses digital communication over Ethernet networks for maritime navigation and radiocommunication equipment.

IEC 61162-460

Adds safety and security-related requirements for suitable Ethernet-based maritime networks.

Talkers and Listeners

In a simple serial communication arrangement, a talker is a device that transmits information, while a listener receives and interprets that information.

For example, a GNSS receiver may transmit position information to several compatible navigation systems. Each receiving device can use the information for its own functions.

Data Quality and Communication Problems

Successful data exchange does not automatically mean that the information is accurate or suitable for use. Several technical conditions must be considered:

  • Compatibility: Connected equipment must support compatible interfaces and message formats.
  • Data integrity: Information must not be corrupted or altered during transmission.
  • Data latency: Information must arrive within a suitable time for its intended function.
  • Source identification: Systems should identify the source of information and its status.
  • Configuration: Incorrect input selection or interface settings can result in missing or misleading data.

Engineering principle: Data transmission and data accuracy are separate issues. A sensor can transmit information successfully even when the original measurement is wrong.

06 / Integrated Bridge Systems

Understanding Integrated Bridge Systems (IBS)

An Integrated Bridge System connects multiple onboard systems to provide centralized access to information and, where appropriate, command and control functions from bridge workstations.

What Is an IBS?

An Integrated Bridge System (IBS) is a combination of interconnected systems designed to improve the coordination and accessibility of onboard information and operational functions.

Rather than requiring officers to interpret every instrument independently, an IBS can bring selected information together through integrated displays and workstations.

The exact functions depend on the vessel's design, equipment, integration architecture, and applicable performance requirements.

Main Systems That May Be Integrated

ECDIS

Provides electronic chart information, voyage planning, and route-monitoring functions.

Radar and ARPA

Support target detection, tracking, and collision-risk assessment.

GNSS and Heading Systems

Supply position, heading, and related navigational information.

AIS

Provides information transmitted by equipped vessels and other AIS stations.

Autopilot and Track Control

Support automatic heading or track control when appropriately configured and operated.

Communication Systems

Support communication between the bridge, other shipboard departments, and external maritime stations.

IBS Architecture

An integrated bridge environment can be understood through several functional layers.

Layer 1 — Sensors and Data Sources GNSS receivers, gyrocompasses, speed logs, echo sounders, and other instruments collect information.
Layer 2 — Communication Interfaces Suitable serial and network interfaces transmit information between compatible onboard devices.
Layer 3 — Processing and Integration Computers and system components interpret, distribute, and coordinate information from different sources.
Layer 4 — Bridge Workstations Integrated displays present information and allow authorized operators to access relevant functions.
Layer 5 — Human Decision-Making Qualified officers interpret the information, evaluate conditions, and make operational decisions.

Benefits of IBS

  • Centralized access to selected navigation information.
  • Improved coordination between connected systems.
  • Reduced need to switch between separate equipment interfaces.
  • Better access to relevant information for bridge teams.
  • Support for monitoring, alarms, and selected control functions.
  • Potential improvement in operational efficiency when properly designed and used.

Important distinction: IBS does not mean that every navigation instrument becomes one device. Independent equipment may remain separate while sharing selected information through an integrated architecture.

07 / Integrated Navigation

Integrated Navigation Systems and Sensor Fusion

Integrated Navigation Systems and sensor-fusion techniques combine information from multiple navigation sources to provide a more consistent understanding of vessel movement and surrounding conditions.

What Is an Integrated Navigation System?

An Integrated Navigation System (INS) combines navigation functions and information from multiple sources to support a coordinated navigation environment.

Depending on its category and capabilities, an INS may provide position, speed, heading, decision-support information, and selected automatic control functions.

An INS and an IBS are related concepts, but they are not interchangeable terms. IBS can cover a broader integration of bridge operations, while INS focuses on integrated navigation functions.

What Is Sensor Fusion?

Sensor fusion is the process of combining measurements or information from multiple sensors to estimate a system's state more reliably than using a single information source alone.

For example, a navigation solution may combine GNSS position measurements with inertial measurements. This can help maintain useful motion information during short periods when satellite positioning becomes unavailable.

Examples of Sensor Fusion

  • GNSS + IMU: Combines satellite-derived position with inertial motion measurements.
  • Gyrocompass + GNSS: Allows comparison between heading and direction of movement.
  • Radar + AIS: Combines radar observations with transmitted target information.
  • GNSS + Speed Log: Allows comparison between speed over ground and speed through water.

Data Consistency and Integrity

Integrated systems must assess whether information from different sources is consistent and suitable for its intended use.

Sensor readings may differ because of measurement uncertainty, timing differences, installation errors, calibration problems, or different physical reference frames.

A robust system should consider the source and integrity of information before using it for safety-related functions or automatic control.

Critical concept: Combining multiple sensors does not automatically eliminate errors. If two sensors share the same incorrect reference or are affected by a common failure, their agreement may still be misleading.

08 / Reliability and Challenges

System Reliability, Failures and Human Factors

Integrated navigation environments must be designed and operated with attention to system availability, data integrity, fault detection, cybersecurity, and human performance.

1. Sensor Failure

A sensor may stop providing information because of hardware damage, power interruption, internal faults, calibration problems, or environmental conditions.

The resulting failure can affect any onboard system that depends on that sensor's information.

2. Incorrect or Misleading Data

A sensor may continue transmitting information even when its measurements are inaccurate. This can be more difficult to identify than a complete loss of data.

Data validation and comparison with independent sources can help detect abnormal measurements.

3. Communication and Network Failure

Faulty cables, damaged connectors, incompatible interfaces, network congestion, configuration errors, and communication interruptions can prevent information from reaching the correct equipment.

4. Power Supply Problems

Navigation equipment depends on suitable electrical power. Power interruptions or distribution faults can affect multiple systems, particularly when they share electrical supplies.

5. Common-Mode Failure

Common-mode failure occurs when multiple systems are affected by the same underlying cause.

For example, two navigation displays may appear independent but still rely on the same GNSS receiver or common power source. Losing that shared source can affect both displays.

6. Cybersecurity Risks

Network-connected navigation systems can be exposed to unauthorized access, malicious software, compromised update media, or manipulation of information sources.

Appropriate security measures include access control, network segmentation where suitable, approved software maintenance, monitoring, backup arrangements, and incident-response procedures.

7. Human Factors and Automation Dependence

Integrated displays and automation can reduce routine workload, but they may also create over-reliance on system-generated information.

Officers need to understand operating modes, alarm meanings, sensor sources, system limitations, and the conditions under which automatic functions should or should not be used.

Failure Detection and Operational Resilience

Good system design should make failures visible to operators and prevent one subsystem failure from unnecessarily disabling unrelated equipment.

The IMO's performance framework for IBS emphasizes fault indication and the ability to operate unaffected equipment independently when required.

Engineering principle: Integration should improve coordination without creating unnecessary dependence. A reliable bridge architecture must consider failure isolation, independent operation, and clear fault indications.

09 / Practical Applications

Navigation Sensors and IBS in Real Operations

Navigation sensors and integrated systems support a wide range of vessel operations, from routine coastal navigation to complex manoeuvres in restricted waters.

Coastal Navigation

GNSS, gyrocompass, radar, and ECDIS information help bridge teams monitor vessel position, heading, and nearby hazards.

Port Approaches

Speed, heading, rate-of-turn, and depth information support manoeuvring in confined waters.

Open-Sea Navigation

Integrated navigation equipment supports voyage monitoring and provides access to relevant navigational information.

Offshore Operations

Navigation sensors and integrated systems support vessel movement monitoring around offshore facilities and operational zones.

Practical Scenario: GNSS Failure During Navigation

A vessel is navigating through a coastal area when its primary GNSS receiver stops providing reliable position information.

The bridge team notices the loss of position data and checks the system alarms. Officers compare available information with independent navigation sources, examine radar observations, and follow the vessel's procedures for degraded navigation.

The team also verifies whether other systems are receiving information from the same failed source.

Learning point: The ability to identify the failed source and understand which systems depend on it is essential for safe integrated navigation.

Practical Scenario: Conflicting Speed Information

During a voyage, the speed log indicates a different speed from the GNSS-derived SOG. The bridge team must determine whether the difference is caused by current, measurement error, configuration, or another operational factor.

Learning point: Different sensors can report different values without either instrument necessarily being defective. The reference and physical quantity being measured must be understood first.

10 / Future Developments

The Future of Integrated Maritime Navigation

Advances in sensor technology, digital communication, data processing, and automation are creating new opportunities for more capable maritime navigation environments.

1. Advanced Sensor Fusion

Future navigation systems can use more sophisticated methods to combine GNSS, inertial measurements, radar observations, and other available information.

2. Improved Integrity Monitoring

Advanced monitoring methods can help identify inconsistent information, abnormal sensor behaviour, and potential failures before they affect critical navigation functions.

3. Digital Bridge Architecture

Network-based equipment and standardized data exchange can support more flexible integration between navigation systems and bridge workstations.

4. Intelligent Decision Support

Artificial intelligence and advanced analytics may help interpret large volumes of sensor data, identify unusual patterns, and support navigational risk assessment.

5. Autonomous and Remotely Operated Vessels

Advanced sensor integration is important for autonomous and remotely operated vessels, where navigation systems may need to interpret environmental information and support automated decision-making.

Such systems require careful validation, cybersecurity protection, dependable sensing, and clearly defined human oversight and operational responsibilities.

11 / Knowledge Summary

Key Concepts to Remember

  • Navigation sensors collect information about vessel position, heading, speed, depth, and movement.
  • GNSS provides satellite-derived positioning and timing information.
  • Gyrocompasses provide true-heading information, while magnetic compasses use the Earth's magnetic field.
  • Heading describes vessel orientation, while COG describes direction of movement over ground.
  • Speed through water and speed over ground use different reference frames.
  • Echo sounders estimate depth using acoustic pulse travel time.
  • IMUs measure acceleration and angular velocity.
  • IEC 61162 standards address digital data interfaces used by maritime equipment.
  • IBS connects multiple systems to provide coordinated access to information and functions.
  • INS focuses on integrated navigation functions and information.
  • Sensor fusion combines information from different sources, but does not automatically remove all errors.
  • System reliability requires fault detection, data integrity, independent operation, and human oversight.

Final concept: Reliable maritime navigation depends not only on having advanced sensors, but also on understanding what they measure, how their data is exchanged, and how integrated systems respond when information becomes unavailable or unreliable.
