AUTONOMOUS SHIPS — TOPIC 06

Guidance, Navigation and Control Systems

Discover how autonomous vessels determine their position, calculate desired movements, and control propulsion and steering systems to follow planned routes while responding to changing maritime conditions.

Marine Engineering Autonomous Navigation Control Engineering Advanced Maritime Systems

1. Introduction to GNC Systems

Guidance, Navigation and Control (GNC) is a fundamental engineering framework used to determine where a vessel is, decide where it should go, and control its physical movement to achieve the intended motion. It connects navigation sensors, onboard computers, decision-making software, propulsion equipment, and steering mechanisms.

In an autonomous ship, GNC systems help translate a planned route into real vessel movement. The system continuously compares the vessel's estimated state with its desired state and calculates appropriate control actions.

GNC is not a single device. It is an interconnected collection of hardware, software, mathematical models, algorithms, and feedback mechanisms.

Navigation

Estimates the vessel's current position, heading, speed, orientation, and motion using sensors and estimation methods.

Guidance

Determines the desired path, direction, speed, or motion needed to reach a target or follow a route.

Control

Commands propulsion and steering equipment to make the vessel's actual movement approach the desired movement.

Important distinction: Navigation answers "Where am I?", guidance answers "Where should I go and how should I move?", and control answers "How can I make the vessel move as intended?"

2. The Relationship Between Navigation, Guidance and Control

The three GNC functions operate as a continuous feedback system. Their outputs and inputs are closely connected rather than working as isolated processes.

First, sensors collect information about the vessel's motion and surrounding environment. The navigation function processes these measurements to estimate the vessel's state. Guidance compares this state with the intended route and produces desired motion references. The control system then converts these references into commands for the vessel's actuators.

New sensor measurements are continuously collected after the vessel moves. This allows the system to detect deviations and update its commands.

Feedback Control Loop

A feedback loop uses the difference between desired and measured behaviour to calculate corrective actions. This difference is commonly called the tracking error.

Tracking Error = Desired State − Estimated Actual State

For example, if a vessel should maintain a heading of 090 degrees but its estimated heading is 085 degrees, the heading error is approximately 5 degrees. The controller may request a steering correction, depending on the vessel's dynamics and operating conditions.

A small tracking error does not automatically mean that a vessel is safe. The system must also consider traffic, obstacles, environmental conditions, equipment limitations, and navigation rules.

3. Navigation: Estimating the Vessel's State

Navigation within a GNC architecture is concerned with estimating the vessel's state. This state may include position, velocity, heading, angular motion, and other variables required for guidance and control.

3.1 Position Estimation

Position estimation determines where the vessel is located relative to a geographic reference system. Satellite navigation receivers such as GNSS can provide position and velocity information.

However, GNSS signals may be degraded, obstructed, jammed, or spoofed. Therefore, autonomous vessels may combine satellite positioning with other navigation sources to improve reliability.

3.2 Heading and Orientation

Heading describes the direction in which the vessel's bow points relative to a reference, commonly true north. Heading sensors and inertial measurement units can contribute to orientation estimation.

Heading must not be confused with course over ground. A vessel may point in one direction while moving over the ground in another direction because of wind, waves, and current.

3.3 Velocity and Motion Estimation

Navigation systems estimate how fast the vessel moves and how its motion changes. Speed through water and speed over ground are different measurements and may vary significantly in strong currents.

3.4 Sensor Fusion and State Estimation

Sensor fusion combines measurements from different sources to produce a more useful estimate of the vessel's state. Common techniques include Kalman filtering and its variations.

  • GNSS: Provides satellite-based position and velocity measurements.
  • IMU: Measures acceleration and angular velocity.
  • Gyrocompass: Provides heading information.
  • Speed log: Measures speed relative to the surrounding water or another reference, depending on its type.
  • Radar: Supports observation of nearby objects and relative motion.

Sensor fusion does not make every measurement correct. Sensors have different errors, update rates, failure modes, and reference frames. Their quality and consistency must be monitored.

4. Guidance: Generating Desired Vessel Motion

Guidance transforms a navigation objective into desired motion references. These references may include a target heading, desired speed, course, waypoint, or path-following command.

4.1 Waypoint Guidance

A waypoint is a predefined geographic position along a planned route. Waypoint guidance calculates how the vessel should move toward the next waypoint.

The guidance system may calculate the bearing to the waypoint and compare it with the vessel's current heading to determine a heading reference.

Heading Error = Desired Heading − Current Heading

4.2 Path Following

Path following focuses on keeping the vessel close to a predefined geometric path. The vessel may travel at a suitable speed while the guidance algorithm continuously adjusts the desired direction.

4.3 Trajectory Tracking

Trajectory tracking follows a path that also specifies how the vessel should move over time. It can include desired position, heading, speed, and timing.

Guidance Method Main Objective Typical Application
Waypoint Guidance Reach successive target positions Route navigation
Path Following Remain close to a geometric path Autonomous transit
Trajectory Tracking Follow a time-dependent motion reference Precision manoeuvring
Dynamic Positioning Maintain a desired position and heading Offshore operations

4.4 Guidance Algorithms

Different guidance algorithms are selected according to the vessel, mission, environmental conditions, and required performance.

  • Line-of-Sight (LOS): Generates a desired heading toward a target point on the path.
  • Adaptive LOS: Adjusts guidance behaviour to help compensate for disturbances such as current.
  • Pure Pursuit: Uses a look-ahead target on the path to calculate a steering direction.
  • Model Predictive Guidance: Uses a predictive model to select suitable future motion references.

Path following and trajectory tracking are related but not identical. A vessel can follow the correct geometric path without meeting a specified time or speed profile.

5. Control: Managing Vessel Movement

The control function converts desired motion references into physical commands. It attempts to reduce the difference between the desired vessel behaviour and the actual estimated behaviour.

5.1 Heading Control

Heading control maintains or changes the vessel's heading by controlling steering equipment. The controller receives a desired heading and compares it with the estimated heading.

5.2 Speed Control

Speed control regulates propulsion to achieve a desired speed. It may adjust engine output, electric motor power, propeller commands, or other propulsion mechanisms.

5.3 Course and Track Control

Course control aims to maintain a desired direction of travel. Track control focuses on following a planned ground track. Because current and wind can move a vessel away from its intended track, track control may require additional corrections.

5.4 Dynamic Positioning

Dynamic Positioning (DP) is a specialised control capability that maintains a vessel's position and heading using computer-controlled thrusters and propulsion units.

DP systems are widely used in offshore operations where a vessel must remain near a specific location without relying solely on conventional anchoring.

5.5 Common Control Algorithms

Several control methods can be used in maritime systems. The appropriate choice depends on the vessel's dynamics, equipment, operating environment, and performance requirements.

  • PID Control: Uses proportional, integral, and derivative actions to reduce control error.
  • Model Predictive Control (MPC): Predicts future behaviour and optimises control actions while considering constraints.
  • Adaptive Control: Adjusts controller parameters when system behaviour or operating conditions change.
  • Robust Control: Aims to maintain acceptable performance despite model uncertainty and disturbances.
  • Sliding Mode Control: Uses a switching control strategy designed to provide robustness under certain uncertainties.

Understanding PID Control

PID is a widely used feedback-control approach. It combines three components that respond to the control error in different ways.

u(t) = Kp e(t) + Ki ∫ e(t) dt + Kd de(t)/dt
  • Proportional (P): Responds to the current error.
  • Integral (I): Responds to accumulated error over time.
  • Derivative (D): Responds to how quickly the error changes.

In a vessel heading controller, the PID output may contribute to the rudder command. The actual implementation must account for actuator limits, sensor noise, vessel dynamics, and safe operating constraints.

PID is not automatically the best controller for every ship. Poor tuning can cause oscillation, slow response, excessive actuator activity, or unstable behaviour.

6. Vessel Dynamics and Mathematical Modelling

A control system needs a representation of how the vessel responds to forces and control inputs. Vessel dynamics describe the relationship between external forces, propulsion, steering, and resulting motion.

6.1 Degrees of Freedom

A marine vessel can move in six degrees of freedom. These describe its translation and rotation in three-dimensional space.

Motion Description Axis
Surge Forward and backward movement Longitudinal
Sway Sideways movement Transverse
Heave Vertical movement Vertical
Roll Rotation around the longitudinal axis Longitudinal
Pitch Rotation around the transverse axis Transverse
Yaw Rotation around the vertical axis Vertical

For many horizontal-plane navigation and manoeuvring applications, surge, sway, and yaw are particularly important. However, waves and other operating conditions may require consideration of all six degrees of freedom.

6.2 Forces and Disturbances

Vessel motion is affected by several forces and environmental disturbances, including:

  • Propulsion forces generated by engines and propellers.
  • Hydrodynamic forces acting on the hull.
  • Rudder forces and steering effects.
  • Wind forces acting on exposed surfaces.
  • Wave-induced forces and motions.
  • Ocean currents and water movement.

6.3 A Simplified Dynamic Model

A common mathematical representation of marine craft dynamics expresses the relationship between vessel velocity, acceleration, forces, and environmental effects.

M ν̇ + C(ν)ν + D(ν)ν + g(η) = τ + τ_environment

In this representation:

  • M: Inertia and added-mass matrix.
  • ν: Vessel velocity vector.
  • ν̇: Rate of change of vessel velocity.
  • C(ν): Coriolis and centripetal effects.
  • D(ν): Hydrodynamic damping.
  • g(η): Restoring forces and moments, where relevant to the model.
  • τ: Control forces and moments generated by actuators.
  • τ_environment: Environmental forces and disturbances.

This is a generalised model. Its exact form, coordinate conventions, and included terms depend on the vessel and modelling assumptions. Engineers use vessel-specific parameters and validated models for real control-system design.

7. Actuators and Propulsion Interfaces

Actuators are the physical devices that execute control commands. They connect the digital control system to the vessel's mechanical and propulsion equipment.

Common Maritime Actuators

  • Rudder: Changes the hydrodynamic forces that influence vessel heading.
  • Main Propeller: Produces thrust for forward or reverse movement.
  • Azimuth Thruster: Can rotate its thrust direction to support manoeuvring.
  • Bow and Stern Thrusters: Provide lateral thrust for low-speed manoeuvring and positioning.
  • Waterjet Systems: Generate thrust by accelerating a water stream.
  • Electric Propulsion Motors: Convert electrical power into mechanical propulsion.

Actuator Constraints

Real actuators cannot respond instantly or produce unlimited force. Their physical limits must be considered by the control system.

  • Maximum and minimum actuator output.
  • Rate limits and response time.
  • Mechanical wear and maintenance condition.
  • Power availability and energy consumption.
  • Communication and command failures.

A controller may calculate a desired rudder angle, but the physical steering gear may not be able to reach that angle immediately. Safe control design must account for this delay and actuator limitation.

8. GNC Integration in Autonomous Ships

In an autonomous vessel, GNC functions are integrated with other systems, including perception, mission planning, collision-risk assessment, communications, and safety monitoring.

Typical Integrated Workflow

  1. The navigation system estimates the vessel's position, heading, and motion.
  2. The perception system detects and tracks relevant objects and environmental features.
  3. The mission planner provides the intended destination and route.
  4. The guidance function generates desired motion references.
  5. The control function calculates steering and propulsion commands.
  6. Actuators execute the commands within their physical limits.
  7. Feedback measurements are used to evaluate actual vessel movement.
  8. Safety functions monitor abnormal conditions and may request a safe response.

Coordination with Collision Avoidance

GNC does not independently decide every navigation action. A collision-avoidance or supervisory decision system may request a change in course or speed. Guidance and control then help execute the approved manoeuvre.

The system must ensure that a requested manoeuvre is physically achievable and remains compatible with vessel limitations, navigational constraints, and applicable collision regulations.

Safety principle: A technically successful steering command is not necessarily a safe navigational decision. GNC execution must remain coordinated with collision avoidance, COLREGs compliance, and the vessel's safety architecture.

9. Practical Engineering Scenario

Consider an autonomous cargo vessel travelling toward a waypoint along a planned route. Wind and current gradually push the vessel away from its intended track.

Step 1 — Navigation

GNSS, heading sensors, speed measurements, and inertial data are processed to estimate the vessel's position and movement.

Step 2 — Guidance

The guidance algorithm calculates the required heading reference to reduce cross-track error and continue toward the waypoint.

Step 3 — Control

The controller compares the desired heading with the estimated heading and calculates a steering command.

Step 4 — Actuation

The steering gear moves the rudder according to the command, subject to its response time and mechanical limits.

Step 5 — Feedback

New sensor measurements show whether the vessel is returning toward the planned track. The system updates its calculations and commands as necessary.

If the system detects an obstacle or a developing collision risk, a higher-level safety or collision-avoidance function may request a different manoeuvre rather than simply continuing to reduce cross-track error.

10. Engineering Challenges and Limitations

Environmental Uncertainty

Wind, waves, currents, and changing water conditions can make vessel behaviour differ from mathematical predictions.

Sensor Errors and Failures

Incorrect measurements, signal loss, calibration problems, or cyber interference can reduce the accuracy of navigation estimates.

Model Uncertainty

A mathematical model cannot perfectly represent every hydrodynamic effect, loading condition, or operating environment.

Control Delays

Sensors, communication networks, computers, and actuators all introduce delays that can affect system performance.

System Integration

Navigation, guidance, control, perception, propulsion, and safety systems must exchange information reliably and consistently.

Cybersecurity and Functional Safety

Unauthorised commands, software faults, compromised sensors, and communication failures can affect the vessel's ability to navigate and control its movement safely.

Testing and Validation

GNC algorithms must be tested under realistic conditions, including simulation, hardware-in-the-loop testing, controlled trials, and operational validation as appropriate.

11. Future Developments in Maritime GNC

  • AI-Assisted Control: Machine learning may help estimate complex system behaviour and support adaptive control.
  • Digital Twins: Virtual vessel models can support simulation, performance analysis, and predictive maintenance.
  • Advanced Sensor Fusion: Improved estimation methods may combine more diverse navigation and perception sources.
  • Energy-Aware Control: Control strategies may optimise propulsion and energy use while respecting safety limits.
  • Cooperative Autonomous Systems: Connected vessels may exchange information to support coordinated operations.
  • Resilient Navigation: Future systems will place greater emphasis on maintaining reliable navigation when satellite signals or other sources become unavailable.

AI can support GNC, but it does not automatically guarantee safe or reliable control. Any AI-assisted function must be evaluated for robustness, explainability where required, failure handling, and operational safety.

12. Key Takeaways

  • GNC stands for Guidance, Navigation and Control.
  • Navigation estimates the vessel's position and motion.
  • Guidance generates desired motion references.
  • Control calculates commands to achieve the desired movement.
  • Feedback helps the system correct deviations continuously.
  • Sensor fusion can improve state estimation but cannot eliminate all uncertainty.
  • Vessel dynamics describe how forces and control inputs affect motion.
  • Actuator limitations must be considered in real control systems.
  • GNC must coordinate with collision avoidance and safety functions.
  • Simulation, testing, and validation are essential before operational deployment.
