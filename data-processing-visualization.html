Data Processing & Visualization

Discover how raw maritime data is collected, validated, processed, analyzed, and transformed into meaningful information that supports monitoring, planning, and operational decision-making.

Maritime Data Data Quality Analytics Visualization
01 — INTRODUCTION

Understanding Maritime Data Processing

Modern ships, ports, and maritime facilities generate large amounts of information through sensors, navigation equipment, machinery monitoring systems, weather services, and digital communication networks.

What is Data Processing?

Data processing is the set of operations used to convert collected data into an organized, consistent, and useful form. It may involve validation, cleaning, transformation, filtering, integration, calculation, and storage.

What is Data Visualization?

Data visualization is the presentation of information through visual formats such as charts, maps, graphs, dashboards, and indicators. It helps users identify patterns, compare values, understand changes, and recognize situations that may require attention.

Important distinction: Processing changes or organizes data. Visualization communicates selected information visually. A clear chart cannot compensate for inaccurate or incomplete source data.
02 — DATA SOURCES

Sources of Maritime Data

Maritime data comes from multiple independent sources. Each source measures or reports a different aspect of vessel, port, or environmental conditions.

Navigation Systems

GNSS receivers, gyrocompasses, speed logs, and electronic chart systems provide information about position, heading, speed, and route.

Environmental Sensors

Instruments measure wind, temperature, pressure, water depth, sea conditions, and other environmental parameters.

Machinery Monitoring

Engine and equipment sensors provide readings such as temperature, pressure, vibration, fuel consumption, and operating status.

Communication Systems

Digital messages and external information services provide weather updates, operational reports, and information exchanged with shore facilities.

Port and Terminal Systems

Port Community Systems and terminal platforms may provide berth schedules, cargo status, vessel movements, and service information.

Human Data Entry

Crew members and operators may enter inspection records, maintenance reports, operational notes, and other manually recorded information.

03 — DATA TYPES

Types of Maritime Data

Data Type Examples Possible Use

05 — DATA QUALITY

Data Validation and Quality Control

Data quality is essential because incorrect measurements may produce misleading analysis and poor operational decisions.

Accuracy

How close a recorded value is to the actual or accepted reference value.

Completeness

Whether the required information is present without important missing records.

Consistency

Whether values and formats agree across related systems and records.

Timeliness

Whether data is available at a suitable time for the intended operational use.

Validity

Whether a value follows the expected format, range, and defined rules.

Uniqueness

Whether duplicate records have been detected and handled appropriately.

Example: Detecting an Invalid Sensor Reading

Imagine an engine temperature sensor normally reports values around 70–95°C under a particular operating condition. A sudden reading of 900°C should trigger a validation check.

  • Check whether the value is within the configured range.
  • Check the sensor status and timestamp.
  • Compare the reading with related measurements.
  • Flag the reading for investigation rather than silently accepting it.
Critical point: An unusual value is not automatically false. It may represent a genuine dangerous condition. A system should flag and investigate suspicious readings instead of automatically deleting them.
06 — DATA ANALYSIS

Processing and Analytical Techniques

1. Filtering

Filtering selects data that meets specific conditions. For example, a system may display only engine readings from a selected time period or equipment unit.

2. Aggregation

Aggregation combines multiple measurements into summary values such as averages, totals, minimums, or maximums.

3. Trend Analysis

Trend analysis examines how a value changes over time. It may reveal increasing vibration, rising fuel consumption, or gradual temperature changes.

4. Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection identifies observations that differ significantly from expected patterns. These observations may require investigation, but they are not necessarily evidence of a fault.

5. Data Integration

Data from different systems can be combined to provide a broader operational picture. This requires compatible formats, meaningful identifiers, timestamps, and suitable data definitions.

07 — VISUALIZATION METHODS

Maritime Data Visualization

Different visual formats answer different questions. Selecting the wrong chart can make accurate data difficult to interpret.

Line Charts

Useful for displaying changes over time, such as engine temperature, speed, or fuel consumption.

Bar Charts

Useful for comparing categories, such as monthly cargo volumes or equipment downtime.

Geographical Maps

Display spatial information such as vessel positions, port locations, and hydrographic features.

Dashboards

Combine indicators, charts, alerts, and operational summaries in one interface.

Tables

Present detailed records when exact values, timestamps, or identifiers are important.

Heatmaps

Use color intensity to show the distribution or concentration of values across a defined dataset or area.

08 — OPERATIONAL DASHBOARDS

Maritime Monitoring Dashboards

What is a Dashboard?

A dashboard is a visual interface that presents selected information from one or more data sources. It allows operators and managers to monitor important indicators without examining every raw record individually.

Navigation Dashboard

May present position, speed, heading, route information, and selected navigation data.

Engine Monitoring Dashboard

May show equipment temperatures, pressure, vibration, and operating trends.

Port Operations Dashboard

May display berth schedules, vessel arrivals, cargo status, and service coordination data.

Environmental Dashboard

May display weather observations, emissions indicators, and environmental measurements.

Design principle: A dashboard should prioritize relevant information, use understandable units, identify timestamps and data sources, and avoid overwhelming users with unnecessary indicators.
09 — DECISION SUPPORT

From Data to Operational Decisions

Processed information can support decisions, but the quality of a decision still depends on context, system limitations, and professional judgment.

Examples of Decision Support

  • Identifying unusual equipment behavior for maintenance investigation.
  • Comparing fuel consumption across different operating periods.
  • Reviewing vessel movement patterns and route information.
  • Monitoring port schedules and identifying possible delays.
  • Examining environmental measurements and operational trends.

Descriptive, Diagnostic, Predictive, and Prescriptive Analytics

  • Descriptive: What happened?
  • Diagnostic: Why might it have happened?
  • Predictive: What may happen next?
  • Prescriptive: What action could be considered?

Predictive and prescriptive methods depend on suitable data, validated models, and appropriate operational safeguards. They do not guarantee future outcomes.

11 — STANDARDS AND DATA GOVERNANCE

Standards, Interoperability, and Governance

IHO S-100 Universal Hydrographic Data Model

The International Hydrographic Organization's S-100 framework provides a foundation for developing standardized digital hydrographic products and services. It supports the management, processing, analysis, presentation, and exchange of hydrographic information.

ISO 19848:2024

This standard defines requirements for standardized data structures and identifiers for shipboard machinery and equipment. It supports more consistent access to, and exchange of, shipboard equipment data.

Data Governance

Data governance establishes responsibilities and rules for data ownership, access, quality, security, retention, and appropriate use. It helps ensure that information remains understandable and trustworthy throughout its lifecycle.

Remember: A data standard does not automatically guarantee accurate measurements. Data quality also depends on sensors, configuration, maintenance, transmission, and processing practices.
12 — SECURITY AND RELIABILITY

Protecting Maritime Data Systems

Data Integrity

Data integrity means protecting information against unauthorized or accidental alteration. Incorrect values can affect reports, monitoring, and analysis.

Access Control

Systems should restrict access according to user roles and operational responsibilities. Sensitive information should not be available to every user by default.

Secure Transmission and Storage

Appropriate security measures can protect information while it is transmitted between systems and while it is stored. These may include encryption, authentication, access monitoring, and secure backups.

Availability and Recovery

Data systems should be designed with suitable backup, recovery, and continuity arrangements. Critical operational decisions should not depend on a single unprotected data source.

13 — PRACTICAL APPLICATION

Practical Case: Monitoring Engine Temperature

Scenario

A ship's engine monitoring system receives temperature readings from several sensors at regular intervals. The readings are stored with timestamps and equipment identifiers.

The data processing platform checks the values, identifies missing readings, compares the measurements with configured limits, and calculates trends.

A dashboard displays the temperature history in a line chart. If a reading exceeds a defined warning threshold, the system can flag it for review.

The engineering team then examines the reading, sensor condition, equipment status, and related information before deciding what action is appropriate.

What Does This Example Demonstrate?

  • Sensors provide the original measurements.
  • Validation checks the readings.
  • Processing organizes and calculates information.
  • Visualization makes changes easier to understand.
  • Human expertise supports the final operational decision.
14 — CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

Challenges in Maritime Data Processing

Disconnected Systems

Different equipment may use different formats, identifiers, and communication methods.

Unreliable Connectivity

Ship-to-shore communication may be limited by coverage, bandwidth, cost, or interruptions.

Large Data Volumes

Continuous measurements can create significant storage, processing, and transmission demands.

Inconsistent Data Quality

Sensor faults, missing values, and inconsistent timestamps may affect analysis.

Cybersecurity Risks

Unauthorized access or manipulation may threaten the confidentiality and integrity of data.

Human Factors

Poor dashboard design or misunderstood indicators may lead to incorrect interpretation.

Future Developments

  • More standardized maritime data models.
  • Advanced analytics and machine learning applications.
  • Improved integration of hydrographic and operational data.
  • More capable digital twins and condition-monitoring systems.
  • Improved data governance and secure information exchange.
