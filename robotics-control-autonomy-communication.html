01 / FOUNDATIONS

Understanding Control, Autonomy & Communication

Marine robots require coordinated control and information systems to operate safely and effectively in complex underwater environments.

What Are Robotics Control, Autonomy & Communication?

Robotics control, autonomy, and communication are interconnected engineering disciplines that allow marine robotic platforms to perform movements, execute mission instructions, respond to sensor information, and exchange data.

Control systems regulate how a vehicle moves and responds to commands. Autonomous systems allow it to perform selected tasks with limited or no continuous human intervention. Communication systems transfer commands, status information, and collected data between vehicles, operators, and supporting infrastructure.

These technologies are essential in ROVs, AUVs, USVs, underwater gliders, and collaborative marine robotic systems.

Important distinction: A vehicle can have automatic control without being fully autonomous. For example, an ROV may automatically maintain its depth while the operator remains responsible for navigation and mission decisions.
02 / CONTROL ENGINEERING

Fundamentals of Marine Robot Control

Control systems translate desired vehicle behavior into commands that operate motors, thrusters, rudders, and other actuators.

What Is a Control System?

A control system is an arrangement of hardware and software that manages the behavior of a dynamic system.

In marine robotics, a controller receives information about the vehicle's current state, compares it with a desired state, and calculates the control action needed to reduce the difference.

The controller may send commands to thrusters, steering mechanisms, buoyancy systems, or other actuators depending on the vehicle design.

Open-Loop Control

Open-loop control applies a command without using feedback from the system output to correct the command.

For example, a controller may activate a motor for a specified duration without measuring whether the vehicle actually reaches the intended position.

  • Simple control structure.
  • Does not require output feedback.
  • Cannot automatically correct disturbances.
  • May be unsuitable for precise underwater positioning.

Closed-Loop Control

Closed-loop control uses feedback to compare the actual system output with a desired reference value.

The controller calculates the error and adjusts its output to reduce that error.

Error = Desired State − Measured State The controller uses this difference to determine an appropriate control action.

For example, an underwater vehicle commanded to maintain a depth of 20 meters can use pressure sensor measurements to estimate its current depth and adjust vertical thrust.

Feedback Control Architecture

A typical feedback control loop includes:

  1. Reference: The desired position, depth, heading, or speed.
  2. Sensor measurement: Information describing the current vehicle state.
  3. Error calculation: Comparison between desired and measured values.
  4. Controller: Calculates the required control response.
  5. Actuator: Executes the command through motors or thrusters.
  6. Feedback: New measurements update the control process.

PID Control

Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) control is a widely used feedback control method. It calculates a control output using three components of the control error.

  • Proportional (P): Responds to the current error.
  • Integral (I): Responds to accumulated error over time.
  • Derivative (D): Responds to the rate at which error changes.
u(t) = Kp e(t) + Ki ∫e(t)dt + Kd de(t)/dt Simplified continuous-time PID equation.

PID controllers can be used for depth, heading, speed, and other control tasks. Their performance depends on correct tuning, system dynamics, sensor quality, and operating conditions.

Advanced Control Methods

More demanding missions may require control methods beyond conventional PID.

  • Model Predictive Control (MPC): Uses a model to predict future behavior and optimize control actions over a prediction horizon.
  • Adaptive Control: Adjusts controller behavior when system characteristics change or are uncertain.
  • Robust Control: Seeks reliable performance despite disturbances and modeling uncertainty.
  • Nonlinear Control: Addresses systems whose dynamics cannot be accurately represented by simple linear relationships.
03 / SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

Marine Robotics Control Architecture

A control architecture organizes the components responsible for sensing, decision-making, movement, and supervision.

Major Architecture Components

  • Perception layer: Processes sensor information about the vehicle and its surroundings.
  • State estimation: Estimates position, velocity, orientation, and other vehicle states.
  • Guidance: Determines the desired direction, path, or trajectory.
  • Control: Calculates the commands required to follow the guidance instructions.
  • Actuation: Converts commands into physical movement.
  • Mission management: Coordinates high-level tasks, operational modes, and mission progress.

Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC)

GNC is a common engineering framework for organizing vehicle movement and navigation.

Although the three functions are closely connected, they have different responsibilities.

Guidance

Determines where the vehicle should go and how it should follow the planned path.

Navigation

Estimates where the vehicle is, how it is moving, and its orientation.

Control

Produces actuator commands to achieve the desired vehicle behavior.

Integration

Combines the three functions into a coordinated vehicle operation.

Sensor Fusion and State Estimation

Marine vehicles may combine information from several sensors to estimate their movement and orientation.

  • IMU: Measures angular velocity and acceleration.
  • Pressure sensor: Provides depth-related measurements.
  • Compass: Provides heading information.
  • DVL: Measures vehicle velocity relative to the bottom or surrounding water, depending on its operating mode.
  • Acoustic positioning: Can provide position information using external acoustic references.

Sensor fusion combines measurements to improve state estimation and reduce the weaknesses of individual sensors.

Techniques may include Kalman filtering, Extended Kalman Filters (EKF), and other estimation algorithms.

Underwater navigation cannot normally rely on GPS while submerged. Vehicles must use suitable combinations of inertial, acoustic, velocity, depth, and environmental measurements. Sensor fusion can reduce estimation errors, but it does not eliminate every source of uncertainty.
04 / AUTONOMOUS SYSTEMS

Understanding Autonomy in Marine Robotics

Autonomy describes the ability of a robotic system to perform tasks and make selected decisions without requiring continuous human control.

What Is Autonomous Operation?

An autonomous marine robot uses onboard sensors, software, control systems, and decision-making logic to execute a mission.

Before deployment, operators may define mission objectives, operating boundaries, waypoints, safety rules, and recovery conditions.

During operation, the vehicle uses its onboard systems to follow the mission and respond to selected events.

Levels of Operational Autonomy

There is no single universal autonomy scale used by every marine robotics manufacturer. The following levels provide a practical educational classification.

LEVEL 01

Direct Teleoperation

A human operator continuously sends movement commands and supervises vehicle behavior.

LEVEL 02

Assisted Control

The vehicle automatically maintains selected states, such as depth, heading, or altitude, while the operator remains in control.

LEVEL 03

Supervised Autonomy

The vehicle executes defined tasks independently while a human monitors mission progress and can intervene when communication allows.

LEVEL 04

Adaptive Autonomy

The vehicle can adjust selected mission actions according to environmental observations, constraints, or unexpected events.

Autonomy Does Not Mean No Human Involvement

Autonomous operation does not automatically mean that a vehicle can perform every mission without supervision.

Humans may still be responsible for mission planning, deployment, safety constraints, monitoring, recovery, data validation, and maintenance.

The appropriate level of autonomy depends on the mission's risk, environment, vehicle capability, and communication availability.

05 / AUTONOMOUS OPERATION

Mission Planning and Decision-Making

Mission planning defines what the vehicle should accomplish, where it should operate, and how it should respond to different situations.

Mission Planning Fundamentals

A mission plan is a structured set of objectives, navigation instructions, operational limits, and decision rules.

An underwater survey mission, for example, may specify an area to map, a target depth, a desired survey pattern, sensor settings, and a recovery location.

  • Define the mission objective.
  • Specify the operational area.
  • Set waypoints and planned trajectories.
  • Define depth, speed, and safety constraints.
  • Configure sensor and data collection schedules.
  • Estimate energy requirements.
  • Define recovery and contingency behavior.

Path Planning vs. Trajectory Tracking

Path planning and trajectory tracking are related but different engineering functions.

  • Path planning: Determines a suitable route from a starting location to a destination, considering obstacles and constraints.
  • Trajectory planning: Adds time and motion requirements to the planned route, such as speed, acceleration, and arrival timing.
  • Trajectory tracking: Uses navigation and control systems to follow the desired trajectory.

Decision-Making Systems

Decision-making logic allows a vehicle to select an action based on sensor information, mission objectives, and predefined constraints.

A vehicle may be programmed to change its behavior when it detects an obstacle, low battery reserves, unexpected depth, or a sensor fault.

Decision-making methods can include:

  • Rule-based decision systems.
  • Finite State Machines (FSM).
  • Behavior-based control.
  • Optimization-based decision methods.
  • Machine learning and selected AI methods.

Finite State Machines (FSM)

A Finite State Machine represents system behavior through a set of defined states and transitions between them.

An AUV mission may use states such as:

  1. Pre-deployment checks.
  2. Mission initialization.
  3. Underwater navigation.
  4. Survey or inspection.
  5. Obstacle response.
  6. Return or recovery.
  7. Mission completion.

A transition occurs when a specific condition is satisfied, such as reaching a waypoint or detecting a fault.

Obstacle Detection and Avoidance

Marine robots may encounter rocks, underwater structures, cables, marine organisms, and other obstacles.

Obstacle avoidance systems use sensors such as sonar, cameras, and other environmental instruments to detect potential hazards.

The autonomy system may evaluate the detected obstacle and select a safe response, such as changing heading, adjusting depth, slowing down, or stopping the mission.

06 / INTELLIGENT SYSTEMS

Artificial Intelligence in Marine Robotics

AI can support perception, classification, planning, and selected decision-making tasks in marine robotic platforms.

AI-Based Perception

AI-based perception uses algorithms to interpret information collected by sensors.

For example, computer vision models can process underwater images to identify objects, inspect structures, or classify marine organisms.

Sonar-processing algorithms can also help interpret acoustic observations and identify features in underwater environments.

Machine Learning

Machine learning algorithms identify patterns in data and use learned relationships to support prediction or classification.

Applications include environmental classification, sensor anomaly detection, and predictive maintenance.

Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning trains an agent to select actions through interactions with an environment or simulation.

Research applications include adaptive control, navigation, and decision-making.

AI-Based Navigation Support

Learning-based algorithms may help estimate environmental conditions, identify obstacles, and support route selection.

Their reliability must be validated under realistic underwater conditions.

Predictive Maintenance

AI can analyze motor measurements, battery data, vibration, and temperature to identify patterns associated with equipment degradation.

This can support earlier maintenance decisions when reliable data is available.

AI is not a substitute for safety engineering. A model that performs well in simulation may fail in real water because of sensor noise, poor visibility, unfamiliar environments, or conditions missing from its training data.
07 / COMMUNICATION SYSTEMS

Underwater Communication Technologies

Communication allows marine robots to exchange information with operators, support vessels, underwater instruments, and other robotic platforms.

Why Is Underwater Communication Difficult?

Communication underwater is more challenging than ordinary wireless communication in air. Radio-frequency signals are strongly attenuated in seawater, especially over practical underwater operating distances.

As a result, underwater systems commonly use acoustic communication, while optical and specialized electromagnetic methods are used in more limited conditions.

Each technology has different limitations involving range, data rate, latency, water conditions, and equipment requirements.

Acoustic Communication

Underwater acoustic communication uses sound waves to transmit digital information through water.

Acoustic modems convert digital data into acoustic signals and recover information from received signals.

This technology is useful for underwater telemetry, selected command messages, positioning support, and data exchange between underwater equipment.

  • Can support communication over relatively long underwater distances.
  • Does not require a physical tether.
  • Usually has lower data rates than many optical links.
  • Propagation delay can be significant.
  • Performance is affected by noise and multipath propagation.
  • Available bandwidth depends on range and operating conditions.

Optical Underwater Communication

Optical communication uses light to transmit information through water, often using LED or laser-based systems.

It can provide high data rates over relatively short distances when water conditions and alignment are suitable.

  • Potentially high data-transfer rates.
  • Low propagation latency.
  • Limited by water turbidity and optical attenuation.
  • Requires suitable transmitter-receiver alignment.
  • Usually more suitable for short-range links.

Electromagnetic Communication Underwater

Electromagnetic communication uses electromagnetic fields to transmit information.

Conventional radio communication is strongly limited underwater because seawater is conductive and attenuates radio-frequency signals.

Specialized low-frequency electromagnetic methods may be used in selected applications, particularly over limited distances or in shallow-water conditions.

Tether-Based Communication

A tether or umbilical can connect an underwater ROV to a surface control station.

Depending on the system design, the tether may carry electrical power and communication signals through copper conductors or fiber-optic links.

Tether-based systems can provide a high-capacity communication connection, but the physical cable introduces operational constraints involving movement, entanglement, drag, and cable management.

Technology Main Advantage Main Limitation Typical Use
Acoustic Longer underwater range Limited bandwidth and latency Underwater telemetry and commands
Optical High data rate Short range and water clarity dependence Short-range data transfer
Electromagnetic Low latency in suitable conditions Strong seawater attenuation Specialized short-range applications
Tether / Fiber Optic High-capacity wired connection Physical cable constraints ROV control and data links
Satellite / Radio Long-distance surface communication Requires suitable above-water operation Surfaced vehicle communication
Underwater vehicles generally cannot use ordinary GPS or satellite communication while submerged. A vehicle may surface periodically to obtain a satellite or GNSS position fix and exchange information through an above-water communication link.
08 / NETWORK ARCHITECTURE

Marine Robotic Networks and Data Exchange

Communication networks connect robotic vehicles with other systems and supporting infrastructure.

Underwater Acoustic Networks

An underwater acoustic network consists of underwater nodes that exchange information through acoustic communication links.

Network nodes may include underwater sensors, autonomous vehicles, fixed instruments, surface gateways, and acoustic modems.

These networks can support environmental monitoring, scientific data collection, selected vehicle coordination, and underwater observation systems.

Surface Gateways and Communication Relays

A surface gateway can connect underwater acoustic communication systems with conventional above-water networks.

For example, an underwater vehicle may send a short acoustic message to a surface buoy. The buoy can then forward information through radio, cellular, or satellite communication when available.

Communication Protocols

Communication protocols define how devices format, address, transmit, receive, and interpret information.

  • Physical layer: Defines the physical transmission method and signal characteristics.
  • Data link layer: Organizes data frames and manages access to the communication medium.
  • Network layer: Supports addressing and routing where required.
  • Application layer: Defines the meaning and structure of exchanged commands and data.

Not every underwater communication system implements the same protocol stack. Acoustic networks often require specialized protocols designed for their propagation and bandwidth constraints.

09 / HUMAN-ROBOT INTERACTION

Operator Interfaces and Human-Robot Interaction

Marine robotic systems often combine automated behavior with human supervision and operational decision-making.

Human-Robot Interaction (HRI)

Human-Robot Interaction describes how people communicate with, supervise, and collaborate with robotic systems.

In marine operations, an operator may define a mission, monitor vehicle status, issue commands, review sensor data, and respond to alerts.

Control Station

A control station provides the operator with interfaces for commanding and monitoring a vehicle.

  • Vehicle status display.
  • Navigation and mission maps.
  • Camera and sonar visualization.
  • Command and control interface.
  • Warnings and fault notifications.

Telemetry

Telemetry is the collection and transmission of measurements and operating information.

  • Battery status.
  • Vehicle depth and heading.
  • Motor and thruster information.
  • Navigation estimates.
  • System warnings and fault reports.

Command and Control

Operators may send commands to change a vehicle's direction, depth, speed, mission mode, or operating parameters.

The communication architecture must account for latency, loss of connection, authorization, command validation, and safe behavior when commands cannot be delivered.

Human Supervision and Control Authority

Some systems allow an operator to take control from an autonomous mode. Other systems may require the vehicle to complete a predefined safe behavior before a new control mode becomes active.

Clear control authority is essential: the operator and autonomous system must not issue conflicting commands without a defined arbitration mechanism.

10 / SAFETY & RELIABILITY

Fault Detection, Recovery and Safe Operation

Reliable marine robotics requires systems that can detect abnormal conditions and respond appropriately.

Fault Detection

Fault detection identifies conditions that may indicate a failure or abnormal behavior in a robotic platform.

Examples include:

  • Unexpected battery voltage changes.
  • Motor or thruster malfunction.
  • Navigation sensor disagreement.
  • Communication loss.
  • Unexpected depth or heading behavior.
  • Excessive temperature or current.

Fault-Tolerant Control

Fault-tolerant control aims to maintain acceptable system operation despite selected component failures or degradation.

Depending on the vehicle design, responses may include using remaining actuators, changing control parameters, reducing mission demands, or entering a safe operating mode.

Communication Loss and Safe Fallback

An underwater robot may lose its communication link because of acoustic interference, distance, environmental conditions, or equipment failure.

A safe fallback policy must be defined before deployment. Depending on the platform and mission, possible responses include:

  • Continue a previously authorized mission.
  • Hold position or enter a safe state.
  • Return toward a recovery location.
  • Surface when the vehicle design permits.
  • Attempt communication again at defined intervals.

These behaviors are platform-specific. Surfacing, for example, may be unsafe under ice or in certain operating conditions.

Cybersecurity and Command Integrity

Networked marine robots may face risks involving unauthorized access, altered commands, data interception, and communication disruption.

Security measures can include:

  • Authentication of authorized operators.
  • Encryption where supported and appropriate.
  • Command validation and access control.
  • Secure software updates.
  • Event logging and system monitoring.
  • Network segmentation and secure configuration.
11 / PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS

Applications in Marine Operations

Control, autonomy, and communication technologies support a wide range of scientific and industrial marine missions.

Autonomous Ocean Surveys

AUVs can follow planned survey routes, collect sensor measurements, and generate information about the seafloor and water column.

Offshore Infrastructure Inspection

ROVs and AUVs can inspect underwater pipelines, cables, platforms, and other subsea infrastructure.

Environmental Monitoring

Autonomous systems can collect environmental measurements and support long-duration ocean observation programs.

Marine Search Operations

Robotic platforms can support underwater search tasks using sonar, cameras, navigation, and mission-planning systems.

Multi-Robot Collaboration

Multiple marine robots can coordinate selected tasks, share information, and divide survey or monitoring areas.

Scientific Exploration

Autonomous and remotely operated vehicles enable researchers to explore environments that are difficult or hazardous for humans.

12 / ADVANCED SYSTEMS

Multi-Robot Coordination and Cooperative Missions

Multiple robotic platforms can work together when their navigation, communication, and task allocation systems are properly coordinated.

Cooperative Marine Robotics

A cooperative mission uses multiple robotic vehicles or sensor platforms to achieve a shared objective.

For example, a surface vehicle may support underwater vehicles by relaying communication, collecting data, or helping coordinate mission areas.

Task Allocation

Task allocation determines which vehicle should perform each part of a mission.

Decisions may depend on vehicle capabilities, remaining energy, location, sensor payload, and communication availability.

Formation Control

Formation control coordinates the relative positions and movements of multiple vehicles.

Maintaining a formation underwater is challenging because vehicles experience changing currents, navigation uncertainty, and communication delays.

Communication Constraints in Cooperative Missions

A multi-robot system cannot assume that every vehicle can exchange information continuously.

Acoustic links may have limited bandwidth, variable delays, and packet losses. Cooperative algorithms must be designed to work with intermittent information and incomplete knowledge of other vehicles.

13 / OPERATIONAL WORKFLOW

How an Autonomous Marine Mission Works

The following workflow illustrates how planning, sensing, control, communication, and recovery are integrated in an autonomous mission.

STEP 01

Mission Definition

Engineers and operators define objectives, operating boundaries, and mission requirements.

STEP 02

Mission Configuration

Waypoints, sensor settings, control parameters, and safety conditions are configured.

STEP 03

Deployment

The vehicle is deployed and its navigation, sensing, and control systems are activated.

STEP 04

State Estimation

Sensors provide information about the vehicle's position, orientation, and movement.

STEP 05

Guidance and Control

The system calculates commands to follow the planned path or perform the required task.

STEP 06

Autonomous Decisions

The vehicle evaluates selected events and responds according to its mission rules.

STEP 07

Communication

Available communication links exchange status messages, commands, and selected data.

STEP 08

Recovery and Analysis

The vehicle is recovered, collected data is reviewed, and mission performance is evaluated.

14 / TECHNICAL CHALLENGES

Engineering Challenges and Limitations

Reliable autonomy is difficult because underwater vehicles operate with incomplete information, uncertain environmental conditions, and communication constraints.

Navigation Uncertainty

Inertial navigation errors can accumulate over time, while acoustic positioning may be unavailable or intermittent.

Communication Delay

Acoustic signals travel much slower than electromagnetic signals in air, creating significant delays over distance.

Limited Bandwidth

Underwater acoustic links often cannot support the same data rates as conventional high-speed wireless networks.

Environmental Disturbances

Currents, waves, turbulence, changing water properties, and obstacles can affect vehicle motion and sensing.

Sensor Limitations

Sensor noise, poor visibility, calibration errors, and environmental interference can reduce perception quality.

Energy Constraints

Computing, sensing, propulsion, and communication all consume limited onboard energy.

Autonomy Validation

Algorithms must be tested in realistic conditions to ensure safe responses to unfamiliar situations and failures.

System Integration

Navigation, control, communication, and autonomy software must work together reliably across different hardware components.

15 / FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

The Future of Marine Robotics Intelligence

Research continues to improve the ability of marine robots to understand their environment, coordinate missions, and operate reliably with limited human intervention.

Adaptive Autonomy

Future systems may adjust mission behavior more effectively as environmental conditions and available resources change.

AI-Based Perception

Improved learning algorithms may support more reliable underwater object detection, classification, and environmental interpretation.

Cooperative Robotic Networks

Multiple underwater and surface vehicles may coordinate tasks through improved distributed planning and communication.

Hybrid Communication

Combining acoustic, optical, tethered, and above-water links may improve communication flexibility across mission phases.

Fault-Tolerant Intelligence

Improved monitoring and fault-management algorithms may help vehicles respond more safely to component failures.

Edge Computing

More onboard processing can allow vehicles to interpret sensor data and make selected decisions without transmitting every raw measurement.

The goal is not simply to remove humans from marine operations. It is to combine reliable automation, suitable autonomy, effective communication, and human supervision in a way that improves safety and mission performance.
