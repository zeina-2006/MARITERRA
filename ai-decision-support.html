01 / INTRODUCTION

Understanding Intelligent Decision Support Systems

A Decision Support System (DSS) is a computer-based system designed to help people analyze information, evaluate alternatives, and make better-informed decisions. In maritime operations, decision support can assist ship officers, marine engineers, fleet managers, and shore-based operators.

An Intelligent Decision Support System extends traditional DSS capabilities by incorporating methods such as machine learning, predictive analytics, optimization, knowledge-based reasoning, and natural language interfaces.

These systems do not simply display information. They can identify patterns, estimate possible future conditions, highlight risks, compare alternatives, and explain why a particular option may deserve attention.

📊

Data Analysis

Transforms operational data into structured information that supports maritime decisions.

🔮

Prediction

Estimates future conditions, possible equipment failures, and operational outcomes.

⚖️

Alternative Evaluation

Compares different options according to safety, cost, time, and operational constraints.

🧑‍✈️

Human Decision Support

Provides recommendations and explanations while keeping authorized personnel involved.

Core distinction: A decision support system helps a person make a decision. It is not necessarily an autonomous control system, and a recommendation should not automatically become an operational command.

02 / SYSTEM DESIGN

Intelligent DSS Architecture

A maritime decision support platform normally consists of several connected layers. Each layer has a specific responsibility, from collecting information to presenting recommendations.

01. Data Sources

Collects information from sensors, vessel systems, databases, weather services, and operational records.

02. Data Integration

Synchronizes, validates, cleans, and combines information from different sources.

03. Knowledge Base

Stores domain knowledge, procedures, constraints, rules, and relevant historical information.

04. Intelligence Engine

Uses analytics, machine learning, optimization, and reasoning to evaluate the current situation.

05. Recommendation Layer

Generates possible alternatives, predicted outcomes, risk indicators, and supporting explanations.

06. User Interface

Presents relevant information to authorized users and supports feedback and decision recording.

How the system operates

  1. Operational data is collected from relevant sources.
  2. The system checks data quality, timing, and consistency.
  3. Analytical models interpret the available information.
  4. Possible future states and operational risks are estimated.
  5. Alternative decisions are compared against defined criteria.
  6. Recommendations and their supporting evidence are presented.
  7. An authorized person reviews the information and takes responsibility for the decision.
  8. The outcome is recorded for monitoring and future improvement.

A sophisticated model cannot compensate for a poorly designed data pipeline. If the system receives inaccurate, delayed, or incomplete information, its recommendations may be unreliable.

03 / DATA FOUNDATION

Data Collection & Processing

Decision support quality depends heavily on the information available to the system. Maritime data comes from different equipment, databases, organizations, and external services.

📡

Navigation Sensors

Radar, AIS, GNSS, gyrocompass, speed logs, and other navigation-related measurements.

⚙️

Machinery Data

Engine temperature, pressure, vibration, fuel consumption, and equipment operating states.

🌦️

Environmental Data

Weather forecasts, wind, waves, visibility, ocean currents, and sea conditions.

📚

Historical Records

Maintenance history, incident reports, voyage records, and previous operational decisions.

🏗️

Port & Logistics Data

Berth availability, cargo information, port schedules, and operational restrictions.

🗂️

Technical Documentation

Equipment manuals, maintenance procedures, safety instructions, and engineering specifications.

Data preprocessing operations

  1. Validation: Identify missing, impossible, or inconsistent values.
  2. Cleaning: Handle duplicate records, noise, and data-quality problems.
  3. Normalization: Convert measurements into consistent units and representations.
  4. Time alignment: Account for differences in timestamps and sampling frequencies.
  5. Integration: Combine related records from multiple sources.
  6. Provenance: Record where information came from and when it was collected.

Data freshness matters. A correct measurement from several minutes ago may be unsuitable for a decision that depends on rapidly changing maritime conditions.

04 / OPERATIONAL INTELLIGENCE

Real-Time Maritime Data Analytics

Real-time analytics evaluates incoming information as it becomes available. This allows decision support systems to identify significant changes, detect unusual patterns, and update operational assessments.

Types of analytics

Descriptive Analytics

Explains what has happened using reports, dashboards, and historical summaries.

Diagnostic Analytics

Investigates why a particular event or operational change may have occurred.

Predictive Analytics

Estimates what may happen next using statistical and machine learning models.

Prescriptive Analytics

Compares possible actions and evaluates which options may best satisfy defined objectives and constraints.

Example: Machinery monitoring

Imagine that a vessel's engine monitoring system reports a gradual increase in vibration and temperature. A real-time analytics component can compare these measurements with historical operating patterns and identify whether the change deserves investigation.

A decision support system may then display the affected equipment, relevant measurements, historical trends, and possible explanations. It should not automatically declare a mechanical failure without sufficient evidence.

Real-time does not mean instantaneous or perfectly current. Processing delays, network latency, sensor update rates, and system workload all influence how quickly information reaches the decision-maker.

05 / FUTURE ESTIMATION

Predictive Models & Decision Forecasting

Predictive models estimate future conditions from historical and current information. Their outputs can help maritime professionals prepare for possible problems before they become operational incidents.

🔧

Failure Prediction

Estimates the likelihood of equipment degradation or failure using sensor patterns and maintenance records.

🌊

Weather Forecasting

Uses forecast information and analytical models to support preparation for changing environmental conditions.

⏱️

Arrival-Time Estimation

Estimates arrival times using voyage progress, operational constraints, and available environmental information.

📉

Demand Forecasting

Estimates fuel, energy, or resource requirements under different operating scenarios.

Common modeling approaches

  • Regression: Estimates continuous values such as expected fuel consumption.
  • Classification: Assigns observations to categories such as normal, warning, or critical.
  • Time-series models: Analyze measurements collected over time.
  • Anomaly detection: Identifies observations that differ from expected behavior.
  • Ensemble learning: Combines multiple models to improve predictive performance in suitable applications.

Prediction uncertainty

A prediction is not a guarantee. Models can be affected by limited training data, changing operating conditions, measurement errors, and unexpected events.

A useful decision support system should communicate uncertainty, identify important assumptions, and avoid presenting estimates as certain facts.

06 / OPERATIONAL RISK

Risk Assessment & Prioritization

Maritime professionals often face multiple alerts and competing operational demands. Risk assessment helps organize these issues according to their potential consequences and urgency.

Risk assessment dimensions

Likelihood

Estimates how likely a particular event or failure may be under the evaluated conditions.

Severity

Considers the potential consequences for people, vessels, assets, and the environment.

Exposure

Considers how long or how frequently an operation is exposed to a particular hazard.

Uncertainty

Evaluates the reliability and completeness of the available information.

Illustrative risk matrix

Likelihood Minor Impact Moderate Impact Severe Impact
Low Low Risk Low / Medium Medium Risk
Medium Low / Medium Medium Risk High Risk
High Medium Risk High Risk Critical Risk

This matrix is an educational illustration. Real maritime risk assessments require defined scales, context-specific assumptions, and appropriate professional methods.

Risk scores should not hide uncertainty. Two situations with the same numerical score may require different responses because their consequences, evidence, and time constraints are different.

07 / HUMAN-AI COLLABORATION

Human & AI Decision Collaboration

Intelligent decision support is most useful when it combines computational capabilities with human experience, professional judgment, and operational responsibility.

🤖

AI Responsibilities

Analyze large datasets, identify patterns, estimate outcomes, and present relevant alternatives.

👨‍✈️

Human Responsibilities

Interpret context, verify recommendations, apply professional judgment, and take authorized decisions.

🤝

Shared Workflow

Combines system analysis with human review, feedback, and operational oversight.

Human-in-the-loop approach

  1. The system receives operational information.
  2. AI analyzes the situation and identifies relevant patterns.
  3. The system presents recommendations and supporting evidence.
  4. An authorized person reviews the information.
  5. The person accepts, rejects, or modifies the recommendation as appropriate.
  6. The decision and its outcome are recorded for accountability and learning.

A critical human-factor risk is automation bias: a person may accept a system recommendation simply because it was generated by a computer. Good design must encourage verification rather than blind trust.

08 / TRUST & TRANSPARENCY

Explainable AI & Decision Transparency

Some AI models produce complex outputs that are difficult to interpret. In maritime environments, this creates an important challenge: professionals need to understand the evidence behind recommendations, especially when safety is involved.

What should an explanation communicate?

Relevant Inputs

Which measurements, events, or data sources influenced the output?

Key Factors

Which variables contributed most to the model's assessment?

Uncertainty

How reliable is the estimate, and what information is missing?

Model Limitations

Under which conditions may the model perform poorly or become unreliable?

Explainability techniques

  • Feature importance: Shows which input variables influence a model's output.
  • SHAP: Estimates feature contributions to individual predictions using a game-theoretic framework.
  • LIME: Builds local approximations to help explain individual model predictions.
  • Rule-based explanations: Presents explicit conditions and reasoning steps.
  • Confidence and uncertainty reporting: Communicates how much trust should be placed in a particular estimate.

An explanation is not automatically proof that a model is correct. Explainability must be combined with testing, validation, monitoring, and clear operational boundaries.

09 / NAVIGATION OPERATIONS

Bridge-Based Decision Support Systems

The ship bridge is a central environment for navigation and operational coordination. Decision support tools can help officers interpret information from multiple bridge systems and maintain awareness of relevant conditions.

🧭

Navigation Awareness

Organizes relevant position, heading, route, and navigational information.

🚢

Traffic Awareness

Helps officers review available target information and identify situations requiring attention.

🌦️

Weather Awareness

Combines weather information with voyage context to support operational preparation.

🔔

Alert Management

Organizes alerts by relevance and urgency to reduce unnecessary distraction.

Bridge decision support must complement established navigational equipment, watchkeeping practices, and applicable regulations. It must not create confusion about which information is authoritative or who is responsible for the vessel's safe navigation.

10 / MARINE ENGINEERING

Marine Engineering Decision Support

Marine engineering decision support focuses on equipment condition, machinery performance, maintenance planning, and operational efficiency.

🔩

Maintenance Planning

Helps prioritize inspections and maintenance activities using equipment condition and historical records.

🌡️

Condition Monitoring

Analyzes temperature, pressure, vibration, and other measurements to identify unusual behavior.

⚙️

Machinery Performance

Compares operating conditions with expected performance and relevant engineering limits.

📋

Technical Troubleshooting

Organizes fault information, technical documentation, and possible diagnostic paths for engineering teams.

Example: Main engine condition assessment

A decision support platform may combine engine temperature, lubrication pressure, vibration measurements, operating load, and maintenance history. If a pattern differs significantly from expected behavior, the system may flag the equipment for engineering review.

The recommendation should include the measurements and trends that triggered the alert. The responsible engineer must verify the condition using appropriate procedures and instruments.

11 / EMERGENCY MANAGEMENT

Emergency Response & Crisis Management

Emergencies require fast, coordinated, and reliable decisions. Intelligent systems may help organize information, identify relevant procedures, and support situational awareness.

Incident Detection

Identifies unusual sensor patterns or events that may require investigation.

Situation Assessment

Combines relevant operational information to help establish an overall picture of the event.

Procedure Retrieval

Helps personnel locate relevant emergency instructions and technical documentation.

Resource Coordination

Supports organizing communication, available resources, and incident information.

Example emergency support workflow

  1. Receive an alarm or incident report.
  2. Collect relevant equipment and environmental information.
  3. Identify the affected systems and potential consequences.
  4. Present applicable procedures and supporting information.
  5. Support communication and coordination among authorized personnel.
  6. Record the incident and subsequent actions.

Emergency response systems must not delay established emergency procedures. AI-generated recommendations are supplementary and must not replace approved instructions, professional judgment, or the chain of command.

12 / ENERGY & EFFICIENCY

Fuel & Energy Decision Support

Energy-related decision support evaluates operational information to help maritime professionals understand fuel consumption, machinery efficiency, and energy demand.

Fuel Performance

Analyzes consumption patterns in relation to operating conditions and vessel performance.

Energy Forecasting

Estimates energy requirements using operational data and relevant environmental conditions.

Efficiency Comparison

Compares historical performance and alternative operating scenarios.

Emissions Monitoring

Supports tracking and analysis of relevant emissions indicators and reporting data.

A useful system must consider the trade-offs between energy efficiency, voyage requirements, equipment limits, safety, weather, and contractual obligations. The lowest predicted fuel consumption is not necessarily the best operational choice.

13 / SYSTEM ASSURANCE

Performance Monitoring & Model Evaluation

Decision support systems require continuous evaluation. A model that performs well during development may become less reliable when equipment, operating conditions, or data patterns change.

Important evaluation dimensions

Accuracy

Measures how often predictions or classifications are correct within the evaluation dataset.

Precision & Recall

Evaluate the quality of positive predictions and the ability to identify relevant cases.

Calibration

Checks whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed event frequencies.

Latency

Measures how long the system takes to process information and produce results.

Availability

Measures whether the system is accessible and operational when required.

Human Feedback

Collects feedback about recommendation usefulness, errors, and operational suitability.

Model drift

Model drift occurs when the statistical characteristics of incoming data or the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes over time. For example, a model trained on one vessel type or operating environment may not perform equally well on another.

Monitoring should include data quality, model behavior, operational outcomes, and documented procedures for review, retraining, or rollback when necessary.

14 / LIMITATIONS & RELIABILITY

Challenges of Intelligent Maritime DSS

Data Quality

Missing, inconsistent, delayed, or incorrect information can weaken recommendations.

Model Uncertainty

Predictions may be unreliable when conditions differ from the model's training data.

Cybersecurity

Unauthorized access, data manipulation, and system compromise can affect decision integrity.

Explainability

Complex algorithms may produce recommendations that are difficult to interpret.

Alert Fatigue

Too many low-value notifications can distract users and reduce attention to important warnings.

Integration

Different equipment standards, interfaces, and data formats can complicate system integration.

Human Factors

Overreliance, workload, training, and unclear responsibilities can undermine effective use.

Legal & Regulatory Issues

Data governance, responsibility, compliance, and operational approval require careful consideration.

A decision support platform should be evaluated by the quality of the decisions it helps people make—not merely by the complexity of its AI model or the number of features displayed on its dashboard.

15 / NEXT-GENERATION SYSTEMS

The Future of Intelligent Maritime DSS

Future maritime decision support systems may become more integrated, context-aware, and capable of interpreting complex operational information.

🧠

Advanced AI Assistants

Natural language interfaces may help professionals search technical information and summarize operational data.

🧬

Digital Twins

Virtual vessel models can support scenario analysis, engineering assessment, and predictive maintenance.

🌐

Connected Maritime Data

Improved integration may connect vessel, port, fleet, and environmental information.

⚡

Edge AI

Onboard processing can support low-latency analysis when cloud connectivity is limited.

🔐

Trustworthy AI

Greater attention to robustness, fairness, transparency, security, and accountability.

📈

Adaptive Decision Support

Systems may improve their assessments through monitored feedback and carefully governed model updates.

The future of maritime decision support will depend on trustworthy data, engineering validation, human-centered design, cybersecurity, and clear operational responsibility.
