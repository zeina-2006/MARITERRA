Sensors and Environmental Perception

Discover how autonomous ships observe the maritime environment, collect sensor data, detect surrounding objects, and transform raw measurements into reliable situational awareness.

MARITIME SENSING SENSOR FUSION ENVIRONMENTAL PERCEPTION
01 / Introduction

What Is Environmental Perception?

Environmental perception is the process through which an autonomous ship gathers and interprets information about its surroundings. It allows the vessel to understand what is happening around it rather than simply knowing its own position.

An autonomous ship must detect nearby vessels, floating objects, navigation buoys, coastlines, port structures, weather conditions, and other potential hazards. It must also estimate where these objects are, how they are moving, and whether they may create a risk.

Unlike a human lookout, an electronic perception system does not rely on a single sense. It combines several technologies, including radar, cameras, LiDAR, GNSS, inertial sensors, and supporting information systems.

The Main Objective

The objective is to create a consistent and continuously updated representation of the vessel's surrounding environment. This representation supports navigation, collision-risk assessment, route planning, and safe autonomous operation.

02 / Core Process

How Does Environmental Perception Work?

Environmental perception is not simply the act of collecting sensor measurements. The system must process those measurements, recognize meaningful objects, estimate their positions, and maintain updated information about them.

STEP 02 Data Processing
STEP 03 Object Detection
STEP 04 Tracking and Awareness

1. Data Acquisition

Sensors collect measurements from the physical environment. A radar may return information about the distance and direction of a target, while a camera captures visual information.

2. Data Processing

Raw measurements may contain noise, interference, missing values, or irrelevant information. Processing techniques help filter, organize, synchronize, and prepare the data for further analysis.

3. Object Detection and Recognition

Detection identifies the presence of an object. Recognition or classification attempts to determine what kind of object it is, such as a vessel, buoy, floating debris, or navigation structure.

4. Tracking and Situational Awareness

Tracking estimates how an object moves over time. The system can combine successive observations to estimate its position, speed, and direction of movement.

Important distinction: Detection answers "Is something there?", classification asks "What might it be?", and tracking asks "Where is it moving over time?" These are related but different engineering tasks.

03 / Sensor Technologies

Main Sensors Used in Autonomous Ships

Different sensors observe different aspects of the maritime environment. Their strengths and weaknesses are complementary, which is why autonomous vessels generally require multiple sensing technologies.

SENSOR 01

Marine Radar

Marine radar transmits electromagnetic waves and analyzes the signals reflected by objects. It can provide information about target range, bearing, and movement.

Main applications:

  • Detecting surrounding vessels.
  • Monitoring navigation hazards.
  • Supporting collision-risk assessment.
  • Operating in darkness and many low-visibility conditions.

Limitations: Sea clutter, rain clutter, interference, blind sectors, and difficulties identifying the exact type of some targets.

SENSOR 02

LiDAR

Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) uses laser pulses to measure distances. It produces spatial measurements that can be organized into a three-dimensional point cloud.

Main applications:

  • Measuring distances to nearby objects.
  • Mapping port structures and coastlines.
  • Detecting obstacles in close-range navigation.
  • Supporting three-dimensional environmental mapping.

Limitations: Performance can be reduced by fog, heavy rain, spray, water droplets, and other atmospheric or environmental conditions.

SENSOR 03

Visual Cameras

Cameras capture images and video of the surrounding environment. Computer vision algorithms can analyze these images to identify objects and visual patterns.

Main applications:

  • Recognizing vessels and navigation buoys.
  • Identifying visual characteristics of objects.
  • Detecting floating debris and obstacles.
  • Reading visual navigation information where applicable.

Limitations: Lighting conditions, darkness, glare, fog, rain, image quality, and camera field of view.

SENSOR 04

Thermal and Infrared Cameras

Thermal cameras detect infrared radiation and represent differences in thermal energy as an image. They can help identify objects based on their thermal contrast.

Main applications:

  • Supporting night-time observation.
  • Detecting some objects with low visible-light contrast.
  • Providing an additional observation channel.
  • Supporting search and monitoring operations.

Limitations: Thermal contrast varies with weather, object temperature, distance, and environmental conditions. Thermal imaging does not replace radar or visible-light cameras.

SENSOR 05

GNSS Receivers

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) provide position and timing information using signals from satellite constellations such as GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, and BeiDou.

Main applications:

  • Estimating the ship's geographic position.
  • Supporting route navigation.
  • Providing timing information for system synchronization.
  • Supporting the position estimation of other sensors.

Limitations: Signal blockage, multipath, interference, jamming, and spoofing can affect GNSS reliability.

SENSOR 06

Inertial Measurement Units (IMU)

An IMU typically contains accelerometers and gyroscopes. It measures linear acceleration and angular velocity, helping estimate how the vessel moves and rotates.

Main applications:

  • Measuring roll, pitch, and yaw motion.
  • Supporting vessel attitude estimation.
  • Providing motion information during GNSS interruptions.
  • Helping compensate for sensor movement.

Limitations: Measurement errors accumulate over time, and inertial estimates can drift without correction from other information sources.

SENSOR 07

Automatic Identification System (AIS)

AIS is a cooperative maritime communication system. Participating vessels transmit information such as identity, position, course, and speed through radio communication.

Main applications:

  • Receiving identification information from equipped vessels.
  • Supporting vessel traffic monitoring.
  • Comparing reported vessel movement with other observations.
  • Supporting maritime situational awareness.

Limitations: AIS depends on transmitted information. Some targets may not transmit AIS, and received information may be missing, delayed, incorrect, or inconsistent.

SENSOR 08

Echo Sounders and Sonar

Echo sounders use acoustic signals to measure water depth beneath the vessel. Sonar systems can use sound waves to detect or investigate underwater objects and features.

Main applications:

  • Measuring water depth.
  • Supporting under-keel clearance awareness.
  • Detecting underwater obstacles with suitable sonar systems.
  • Supporting seabed and underwater mapping.

Limitations: Acoustic performance depends on water conditions, equipment configuration, noise, seabed characteristics, and the sonar system's operating range.

SENSOR 09

Weather and Environmental Sensors

Environmental instruments measure conditions that may affect ship operation and sensor performance.

Examples include:

  • Wind speed and direction sensors.
  • Air temperature and pressure sensors.
  • Humidity sensors.
  • Wave and sea-state monitoring instruments.

Limitations: Measurements are local and may not fully represent conditions across the entire surrounding area.

SENSOR 10

Machinery and Vessel-State Sensors

These sensors monitor the ship itself rather than external obstacles. They provide information about the vessel's operational condition.

Examples include:

  • Engine temperature and pressure sensors.
  • Propulsion and steering feedback sensors.
  • Fuel and power monitoring instruments.
  • Vibration and equipment condition sensors.

Importance: Environmental awareness alone is insufficient. An autonomous vessel must also understand whether its own systems are operating correctly.

04 / Technical Comparison

Comparing Maritime Sensors

No sensor is universally superior. The appropriate technology depends on the task, operating environment, required accuracy, detection range, cost, and integration requirements.

05 / Data Integration

Multi-Sensor Fusion

Sensor fusion is the process of combining information from different sensors to produce a more complete and reliable estimate of the environment or vessel state.

Each sensor provides only part of the overall picture. Radar may detect a target's range and bearing, a camera may provide visual characteristics, and AIS may provide transmitted identification and movement information.

A fusion system attempts to associate relevant observations, account for their uncertainty, and estimate which observations refer to the same real-world object.

Why Is Sensor Fusion Necessary?

  • Different sensors have different strengths and weaknesses.
  • One sensor may detect an object that another sensor cannot clearly observe.
  • Combining independent measurements can improve estimation.
  • Cross-checking can help identify inconsistent or faulty data.
  • Multiple observations can support more stable object tracking.

Example of Sensor Fusion

Imagine an autonomous vessel approaching a busy port. Its radar detects a target ahead. A camera captures an image of the target, while AIS receives information from a nearby transmitting vessel. The system can compare these observations to determine whether they may represent the same object.

Important Fusion Techniques

1. Data-Level Fusion: Combines measurements at an early stage. It may preserve detailed information but requires careful calibration, synchronization, and compatible measurement representations.

2. Feature-Level Fusion: Combines extracted features from different sensors. For example, image features can be combined with spatial measurements from LiDAR.

3. Decision-Level Fusion: Combines the outputs of separate detection or classification systems. It can be useful when sensors or algorithms operate independently.

Important: Sensor fusion does not automatically make a system correct. Poor calibration, incorrect object association, timing errors, and unreliable input data can produce misleading fused results.

06 / Intelligent Perception

Artificial Intelligence in Environmental Perception

Artificial Intelligence can help autonomous ships interpret complex sensor data. Machine learning and computer vision algorithms can analyze images, point clouds, radar information, and other measurements.

Object Detection

Object detection algorithms identify potential objects within sensor data and estimate their locations. A camera-based model, for example, may identify a vessel or buoy in an image.

Object Classification

Classification assigns an object to a category based on its observed characteristics. Possible categories include vessels, buoys, land structures, floating debris, and other obstacles.

Object Tracking

Tracking algorithms associate observations across multiple time steps. They estimate how an object moves and help maintain a continuous representation even when measurements temporarily become uncertain.

Semantic Segmentation

Semantic segmentation classifies individual pixels in an image into categories. It may distinguish water, sky, vessels, coastlines, and port structures.

Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection identifies patterns that differ from expected behavior. For example, the system may flag unusual movement, inconsistent sensor readings, or unexpected changes in a monitored area.

Critical limitation: AI predictions are estimates, not guaranteed facts. Models can fail when they encounter unfamiliar objects, unusual weather, poor-quality data, or conditions that differ from their training data. Safety-critical systems need validation, monitoring, uncertainty handling, and appropriate fallback procedures.

07 / Engineering Challenges

Challenges in Maritime Environmental Perception

1. Harsh Weather Conditions

Fog, heavy rain, sea spray, waves, and strong sunlight can reduce the quality of sensor measurements. Different sensor types respond differently to these conditions.

2. Sea Clutter and Reflections

Waves and reflections may produce unwanted radar returns or confusing visual patterns. These effects can make it harder to distinguish real targets from environmental interference.

3. Sensor Calibration

Sensors must be accurately calibrated and aligned. Incorrect calibration can cause measurements from different sensors to appear inconsistent or refer to different locations.

4. Time Synchronization

Sensors may operate at different update rates. Their data must be time-aligned so the system does not combine observations that describe different moments as if they were simultaneous.

5. Sensor Failure and Degradation

Sensors may become damaged, blocked, disconnected, or gradually less accurate. Autonomous systems need ways to monitor sensor health and recognize when measurements should not be trusted.

6. GNSS Interference

Jamming, spoofing, and signal blockage can affect satellite-based positioning. A resilient navigation solution should use additional information sources and detect suspicious inconsistencies.

7. Processing and Communication Requirements

High-resolution cameras and LiDAR can generate large data volumes. Processing must be fast enough for the operational task, while communication links must support the information that needs to be shared with remote operators or other systems.

8. Uncertainty and False Detection

A system may mistake waves, birds, floating debris, or reflections for relevant targets. It may also fail to detect a real obstacle. Perception systems therefore need to estimate confidence and handle uncertain observations carefully.

08 / Practical Application

Practical Scenario: Detecting a Vessel Near a Port

Consider an autonomous ship approaching a port where other vessels, buoys, small craft, and port structures are present. Its perception system must maintain an updated picture of the surrounding area.

How the System Responds

  1. Marine radar detects a possible vessel and estimates its range and bearing.
  2. A camera captures visual information that may help classify the detected object.
  3. AIS information is checked for a matching transmitting vessel, if available.
  4. GNSS and IMU measurements help estimate the autonomous ship's own position and movement.
  5. Sensor fusion attempts to associate the observations and estimate the target's position and movement.
  6. The tracking system updates the target's estimated motion as new measurements arrive.
  7. The perception output is passed to navigation and decision-making systems for further assessment.

The perception system does not independently decide every navigation action. It supplies information to other systems, which assess the situation and determine suitable responses under the vessel's safety and operational requirements.

What if AIS is unavailable? The system should not automatically conclude that no vessel exists. Radar, cameras, and other suitable sensors must continue supporting target detection and tracking.

09 / Future Developments

The Future of Maritime Perception

Advanced Multi-Modal Perception

Research continues to combine radar, LiDAR, visible-light cameras, thermal cameras, GNSS, and inertial measurements to improve environmental awareness.

Edge AI Processing

Edge computing allows some perception algorithms to operate directly on onboard computers. This can reduce dependence on continuous communication with remote systems.

Cooperative Perception

Ships may exchange selected information with other vessels, shore infrastructure, or maritime traffic services. Such cooperation can extend awareness beyond an individual vessel's direct sensor coverage.

Resilient and Self-Monitoring Sensors

Future systems are expected to improve their ability to detect sensor degradation, identify inconsistent measurements, and adapt when individual sensing channels become unavailable.

Improved Maritime Datasets

More diverse and carefully validated datasets can help develop and evaluate perception algorithms under different weather, lighting, traffic, and geographic conditions.

10 / Key Takeaways

What You Should Remember

Environmental perception helps autonomous ships understand their surroundings.
Radar, LiDAR, cameras, GNSS, and IMUs provide different types of information.
AIS is a cooperative information system, not a universal object-detection sensor.
Detection, classification, and tracking are separate technical tasks.
Sensor fusion combines complementary measurements to improve the overall estimate.
AI can support perception, but its outputs must be validated and monitored.
Calibration, synchronization, and sensor health are essential engineering requirements.
Perception provides information to navigation and decision-making systems; it is not the entire autonomous control system.
