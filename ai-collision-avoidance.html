01. Overview of Collision Avoidance & Risk Prediction

Maritime collision avoidance is the process of identifying dangerous vessel encounters and taking appropriate action to prevent collisions. It is a fundamental part of safe navigation, especially in busy shipping lanes, port approaches, narrow channels and areas with complex traffic.

Traditional collision assessment relies on navigational observations, radar information, Automatic Identification System (AIS) data, electronic charts and the professional judgment of the bridge team. Artificial intelligence can strengthen this process by analyzing multiple data sources, recognizing traffic patterns and estimating how encounter situations may develop.

AI-based risk prediction does not simply ask whether two vessels are currently close. It evaluates their relative movement, projected trajectories, environmental conditions and other contextual factors to help identify situations that may require attention.

Core principle: Collision avoidance is not just a mathematical distance problem. It involves relative motion, vessel maneuverability, navigational rules, environmental conditions and human judgment.

02. Fundamentals of Maritime Collision Situations

A collision risk situation can develop when two or more vessels move along paths that may result in an unsafe proximity. The assessment requires understanding both the current positions and the relative movement of the vessels.

Relative Motion

Relative motion describes how one vessel appears to move when observed from another vessel. It is useful for estimating whether another vessel is approaching, moving away or maintaining a potentially dangerous encounter geometry.

Relative Velocity V(relative) = V(target) − V(own ship)

Here, the velocities must be expressed in the same coordinate reference system. The resulting vector describes the target vessel's movement relative to the own ship.

Important Encounter Variables

Relative Bearing

The direction of the target vessel measured relative to the observing vessel's heading.

Range

The distance separating two vessels at a particular observation time.

Relative Speed

The rate at which the separation between vessels changes according to their relative movement.

Heading

The direction in which a vessel's bow is pointing, which may differ from its course over ground.

Why Distance Alone Is Not Enough

Two vessels may be physically close but moving apart. Another pair may be farther away while approaching on intersecting courses. Therefore, collision assessment requires time-dependent analysis rather than relying only on current range.

03. Types of Vessel Encounter Situations

Vessel encounters are commonly analyzed according to the relative positions and directions of the vessels. Understanding these situations helps navigators interpret developing traffic interactions and apply the relevant collision-prevention rules.

↗️

Crossing Situation

Two power-driven vessels approach on courses that cross. Their relative movement and applicable rules must be assessed carefully.

↔️

Head-On Situation

Two power-driven vessels approach on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses, potentially creating a risk of collision.

↘️

Overtaking Situation

One vessel approaches another from a direction that meets the applicable overtaking definition under COLREGs.

🔄

Multiple-Vessel Encounter

Several vessels interact simultaneously, making traffic assessment and maneuver planning more complex.

Complex Traffic Environments

Busy waterways introduce additional challenges. A vessel may encounter crossing traffic, fishing vessels, ferries, restricted maneuvering areas and vessels entering or leaving ports.

An AI system should consider the wider traffic situation instead of evaluating every target independently. A maneuver that reduces risk with one vessel could create a new problem with another.

Important: Encounter classification supports understanding, but the actual navigational situation and applicable COLREG requirements must be determined by qualified personnel.

04. Closest Point of Approach (CPA) & Time to CPA (TCPA)

CPA and TCPA are important concepts used in radar plotting and collision risk assessment. They help estimate the closest predicted separation between two vessels and when that separation is expected to occur, assuming the current relative motion continues.

Closest Point of Approach

The Closest Point of Approach (CPA) is the predicted minimum distance between two moving objects under a constant relative-velocity model. It helps identify whether their projected paths may bring them too close.

Conceptual CPA Calculation TCPA = − (r · v) / |v|²

CPA = |r + v × TCPA|

Where r is the relative position vector and v is the relative velocity vector. The equations assume constant relative velocity and a consistent coordinate system.

Time to Closest Point of Approach

TCPA estimates the time remaining until the predicted closest approach. A positive TCPA indicates that the closest approach lies in the future under the model. A negative value indicates that the predicted closest point has already passed.

Small CPA

A small predicted separation may indicate a close encounter requiring careful assessment.

Positive TCPA

The predicted closest approach is still ahead if the relative motion remains unchanged.

Negative TCPA

The calculated closest approach has already occurred under the assumed motion model.

Changing Motion

Alterations in heading or speed can change CPA and TCPA estimates significantly.

CPA and TCPA Limitations

CPA and TCPA are predictions, not guarantees. Their accuracy depends on the quality and freshness of position and velocity information. They can become less reliable when vessels maneuver, data is delayed or the motion assumptions are no longer valid.

Critical distinction: CPA is not a universal legal safety threshold. An acceptable passing distance depends on the circumstances, vessel characteristics, environment, traffic and professional assessment.

05. Sensor Systems & Maritime Data Integration

AI-based collision avoidance depends on accurate information about own-ship movement and surrounding vessels. Different sensors and information systems provide complementary observations.

📡

Marine Radar

Detects surrounding objects and supports the measurement of target range and bearing.

🛰️

AIS

Provides transmitted vessel information such as identity, position, course and speed when available and valid.

🧭

GNSS

Supports positioning and navigation through satellite-based location information.

⚓

Gyrocompass & Heading Sensors

Provide heading information used to understand vessel orientation and movement.

📷

Optical Cameras

Can support visual recognition of vessels, lights, navigation marks and surrounding objects.

🌊

Environmental Sensors

Provide contextual observations such as wind and other environmental conditions.

Sensor Fusion

Sensor fusion combines information from multiple sources to build a more complete representation of the surrounding environment. For example, radar may detect a target while AIS provides transmitted identity and movement information.

The system must associate observations that refer to the same physical target. This is known as data association or target association. Incorrect association can produce misleading tracking results.

Data Quality Problems

  • Latency: Information may arrive later than the actual observation time.
  • Noise: Sensor measurements may contain errors or fluctuations.
  • Missing observations: Targets may temporarily disappear from sensor coverage.
  • AIS limitations: Transmitted information may be unavailable, delayed or incorrect.
  • Coordinate alignment: Different sources may use different reference systems.
  • Target association: Multiple detections may be incorrectly matched to one vessel.
Engineering lesson: Combining more data does not automatically produce better decisions. Sensor fusion must account for uncertainty, reliability, synchronization and the possibility of misleading observations.

06. AI-Based Collision Risk Prediction

Collision risk prediction estimates the possibility of an unsafe encounter developing over time. AI systems may combine vessel motion, CPA/TCPA, traffic density, environmental conditions and historical encounter patterns to produce risk indicators.

Risk Factors

Relative Motion

Relative speed, direction and projected trajectories between vessels.

CPA and TCPA

Predicted passing distance and time to the closest point of approach.

Traffic Density

The number and movement complexity of nearby vessels.

Environmental Conditions

Visibility, weather, sea state and other conditions affecting navigation.

Vessel Maneuverability

Turning behavior, stopping characteristics and vessel-specific limitations.

Data Confidence

The reliability and freshness of the information used to predict an encounter.

Risk Assessment Pipeline

ObserveCollect vessel and sensor data
TrackEstimate target movement
PredictProject future encounter paths
AssessEstimate encounter risk
AlertPresent relevant warnings
ReviewHuman navigational assessment

Risk Scores and Their Interpretation

A predictive model may generate a numerical risk score to help prioritize attention. Such scores are model outputs and should not be confused with a universal probability of collision unless the model has been specifically calibrated and validated for that purpose.

Illustrative Risk Model Risk Score = f(CPA, TCPA, relative motion, traffic, environment, data confidence)

A useful system should explain the main factors contributing to a high-risk assessment. It should also communicate uncertainty and avoid presenting false precision.

07. AI Techniques for Collision Prediction

Different machine learning techniques can support different parts of a collision avoidance system. Some methods identify patterns in historical encounters, while others predict future vessel movement or classify risk levels.

1. Classification Models

Classification models assign observations to predefined categories. A system may classify an encounter as low, moderate or high priority based on validated operational criteria.

2. Regression Models

Regression models estimate continuous values such as predicted separation, vessel speed or trajectory-related quantities. Their performance depends on the quality and representativeness of the training data.

3. Recurrent Neural Networks

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs) can process sequential observations. They may be useful when the evolution of vessel positions and movement over time is important.

4. Transformer-Based Models

Transformer architectures use attention mechanisms to learn relationships within sequences and structured data. They may support trajectory prediction and the analysis of interactions between multiple moving vessels.

5. Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection identifies unusual patterns that differ from expected behavior. For example, an unexpected change in speed or heading may deserve additional attention.

Model selection: A more complex neural network is not automatically safer or more accurate. Model choice must be supported by representative data, appropriate evaluation and operational testing.

08. Intelligent Collision Avoidance Strategies

Collision avoidance systems may evaluate possible actions and estimate how they affect future separation and traffic interactions. Their purpose is to support early assessment and help identify potential conflicts before they become urgent.

Possible Maneuver Variables

Course Alteration

Evaluate how changing heading may affect the predicted encounter geometry.

Speed Adjustment

Assess how changes in speed influence relative motion and available decision time.

Alternative Trajectories

Compare candidate movement paths against traffic and navigational constraints.

Early Warning

Provide alerts when observations suggest that an encounter requires attention.

Predictive Maneuver Evaluation

An intelligent system can simulate several possible actions and compare their predicted consequences. It may evaluate whether an action reduces the likelihood of a close encounter, creates a new conflict or conflicts with navigational constraints.

This evaluation must include other vessels, not just the primary target. In dense traffic, a maneuver that appears effective against one vessel may create an unsafe situation with another.

Safety requirement: AI-generated maneuver suggestions must not override COLREGs, the vessel's safety procedures or the master's professional judgment.

09. COLREGs & Regulatory Safety Framework

The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea, commonly known as COLREGs, establish rules for preventing collisions between vessels. These rules address lookout, safe speed, risk of collision, actions to avoid collision and responsibilities in different encounter situations.

Important COLREG Concepts

  • Rule 5 — Look-out: Every vessel must maintain a proper lookout using all available appropriate means.
  • Rule 6 — Safe Speed: Vessels must proceed at a safe speed appropriate to the prevailing circumstances and conditions.
  • Rule 7 — Risk of Collision: Appropriate means must be used to determine whether risk of collision exists.
  • Rule 8 — Action to Avoid Collision: Action taken to avoid collision must follow the applicable requirements.
  • Rules 13–15: Address overtaking, head-on and crossing situations.
  • Rule 17: Addresses action by the stand-on vessel.

How AI Supports Regulatory Compliance

AI may help organize observations, identify possible encounter types, calculate motion estimates and provide decision-support information. However, an AI model does not replace the interpretation or application of COLREGs by qualified navigators.

A system should be designed so that its recommendations remain consistent with applicable regulations and vessel procedures. Its outputs must not encourage a maneuver that creates a new danger or conflicts with a required navigational action.

Essential distinction: AI can assist in recognizing and analyzing a collision risk. It cannot independently assume the legal and operational responsibilities assigned to the vessel and its master.

10. Human-AI Cooperation on the Bridge

A collision avoidance system should be designed around the bridge team's operational needs. It must present relevant information clearly, avoid unnecessary alarms and support rather than replace human judgment.

Effective Human-Machine Interaction

Clear Alerts

Warnings should communicate the nature and urgency of the potential encounter.

Explainable Results

Operators should understand why the system has assigned a particular risk level.

Uncertainty Display

Information quality and prediction confidence should be visible to the user.

Human Control

Authorized personnel should retain control over operational navigational decisions.

The Problem of Alert Fatigue

If a system generates too many warnings, bridge personnel may become less responsive to important alerts. This is known as alert fatigue. Excessive false positives can reduce trust in the system and make genuine hazards harder to identify.

Effective alert design should consider relevance, timing, severity, clarity and the operational context. Risk thresholds should be evaluated using real-world requirements rather than selected solely for convenience.

11. Collision Avoidance in Autonomous Ships

Autonomous and remotely operated vessels create additional requirements for collision avoidance. Their systems may need to perceive nearby traffic, predict future movement, evaluate actions and communicate their decisions through integrated control architectures.

Autonomous Collision Avoidance Pipeline

PerceptionDetect nearby objects
TrackingEstimate target motion
PredictionEstimate future trajectories
PlanningEvaluate candidate actions
ControlExecute approved control commands
MonitoringVerify actual vessel behavior

Major Technical Requirements

  • Reliable object detection and target tracking.
  • Robust behavior when sensors disagree or become unavailable.
  • Trajectory prediction under uncertain vessel behavior.
  • Safe planning within operational and regulatory constraints.
  • Clear communication with remote operators when applicable.
  • Fail-safe behavior when critical system components malfunction.

Autonomous collision avoidance is especially challenging in situations involving unusual vessel behavior, poor visibility, dense traffic and incomplete information. These conditions require extensive simulation, testing and safety validation.

12. Engineering Workflow: Building a Collision Risk System

Developing a dependable collision risk prediction system requires a complete engineering process. It is not enough to train a model on historical AIS records and display a risk score.

01. DefineSpecify risk and safety requirements
02. CollectGather synchronized maritime observations
03. ProcessClean data and associate targets
04. PredictEstimate motion and encounter outcomes
05. EvaluateTest performance and failure modes
06. DeployIntegrate with human decision support

Important Evaluation Metrics

Precision

Measures the proportion of predicted positive alerts that are actually positive according to the chosen reference labels.

Recall

Measures how many relevant positive cases the model successfully identifies.

False Positive Rate

Measures how frequently the system incorrectly raises an alert for a negative case.

Prediction Error

Measures the difference between predicted and observed target positions or trajectories.

Calibration

Checks whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed event frequencies.

Response Time

Measures how quickly the system processes new observations and produces useful outputs.

Important evaluation warning: Collision events are relatively rare compared with ordinary vessel encounters. A model can achieve high overall accuracy simply by predicting that almost every situation is safe. Precision, recall, calibration and scenario-based testing are therefore essential.

13. Challenges & Limitations

Incomplete AIS Data

Some vessels may not transmit usable AIS information, and transmitted data may be inaccurate or delayed.

Sensor Errors

Radar clutter, positioning errors and environmental interference may affect target detection.

Unpredictable Behavior

Another vessel may change course or speed unexpectedly, invalidating previous predictions.

Rare Collision Events

Limited examples of serious incidents make model training and validation difficult.

False Alarms

Excessive alerts can reduce operator trust and contribute to alert fatigue.

Model Generalization

A model trained in one traffic environment may not perform equally well in different regions or vessel populations.

Another major challenge is explainability. Maritime professionals need to understand the reasons behind an alert and whether the recommendation is based on reliable observations.

AI systems should also be tested against rare and difficult scenarios, including sensor failures, unusual maneuvers, poor visibility and complex multi-vessel encounters.
