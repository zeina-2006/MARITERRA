Underwater Gliders

Explore the engineering principles behind buoyancy-driven underwater vehicles, hydrodynamic lift, energy-efficient movement, autonomous navigation, and long-duration ocean observation.

01 / Foundations

Understanding Underwater Gliders

An underwater glider is an autonomous underwater vehicle that changes its buoyancy to move vertically and uses hydrodynamic wings to convert that motion into forward travel.

What Is an Underwater Glider?

An underwater glider is a specialized uncrewed underwater platform designed for long-duration oceanographic observation. Unlike conventional propeller-driven vehicles, many gliders move through the water by repeatedly changing their buoyancy.

When the vehicle becomes slightly negatively buoyant, it descends. When it becomes positively buoyant, it ascends. Its wings generate hydrodynamic lift during these vertical movements, producing a horizontal component of motion.

This process allows the vehicle to travel through the water column using relatively little energy. Gliders are therefore particularly useful for collecting repeated measurements over extended periods.

CORE PRINCIPLE 01

Buoyancy Control

The vehicle changes its effective volume and buoyancy using an internal buoyancy engine. This creates controlled sinking and rising movements.

CORE PRINCIPLE 02

Hydrodynamic Lift

Wings interact with the surrounding water. As the glider moves vertically, these surfaces generate lift and help convert vertical movement into forward displacement.

CORE PRINCIPLE 03

Low-Energy Operation

Many underwater gliders do not use a conventional propulsion motor during normal travel. Their buoyancy engine and control systems consume energy while the wings provide hydrodynamic lift.

CORE PRINCIPLE 04

Long-Duration Observation

Their energy-efficient movement allows some platforms to operate for weeks or months, depending on the vehicle, battery configuration, sensors, communication schedule, and mission.

Important engineering distinction: Underwater gliders are generally considered a specialized type of AUV. The difference is mainly their propulsion approach and mission profile, not the absence of autonomy.

03 / Engineering Architecture

How an Underwater Glider Is Built

A glider combines mechanical structures, buoyancy systems, control electronics, energy storage, navigation equipment, and scientific payloads.

1. Pressure-Resistant Hull

The hull protects internal electronics, batteries, sensors, and mechanical components from the external hydrostatic pressure of the ocean.

Engineers select hull materials and structural geometry according to the intended operating depth, pressure rating, weight limitations, corrosion resistance, and manufacturing requirements.

  • Provides structural protection for internal components.
  • Maintains watertight integrity.
  • Supports external wings and sensor installations.
  • Must withstand pressure at the vehicle's rated depth.

2. Buoyancy Engine

The buoyancy engine is one of the most important systems in an underwater glider. It changes the vehicle's displaced volume and therefore its buoyancy relative to the surrounding seawater.

Many designs use a hydraulic or electromechanical pumping arrangement to move oil between an internal reservoir and an external flexible bladder.

When the external bladder expands, the vehicle's displaced volume increases. Its mass may remain approximately constant, but its average density decreases, making it more positively buoyant.

When the bladder contracts, displaced volume decreases and the vehicle becomes less buoyant. The exact arrangement varies by glider design.

  • Hydraulic pump or syringe mechanism.
  • Internal oil reservoir.
  • External flexible bladder.
  • Valves and fluid pathways.
  • Position or pressure feedback for system control.

3. Wings and Hydrodynamic Surfaces

Glider wings are designed to generate lift as the vehicle moves through water. They transform part of the vertical movement into forward movement.

The wing geometry, angle of attack, vehicle speed, and surrounding water conditions influence the aerodynamic-style hydrodynamic forces acting on the vehicle.

Unlike a powered aircraft, the glider does not obtain energy from an engine to maintain forward motion. Its buoyancy-driven vertical movement supplies the motion through which the wings generate lift.

4. Internal Mass-Shift Mechanism

Some gliders move an internal mass, battery pack, or dedicated ballast mechanism to change the vehicle's pitch and roll.

This helps control the direction of travel and attitude during descent and ascent. Some platforms also use control surfaces or rudders, depending on their design.

5. Battery and Power System

Batteries supply electrical energy to the buoyancy engine, onboard computer, sensors, navigation equipment, and communication systems.

Battery chemistry and configuration depend on mission duration, operating depth, power demand, temperature, safety requirements, and maintenance strategy.

  • Primary batteries may be selected for specific long-duration missions.
  • Rechargeable batteries can support repeated deployments.
  • Power management schedules high-energy operations.
  • Battery monitoring helps estimate remaining mission endurance.

6. Onboard Computing and Control Electronics

The onboard computer executes mission instructions, processes sensor measurements, monitors vehicle health, and controls the buoyancy and attitude mechanisms.

It can also detect abnormal conditions and execute pre-programmed safety responses, such as changing the mission profile or attempting to return to the surface.

7. Scientific Payload Integration

The payload is selected according to the scientific question or operational objective. A glider may carry several instruments simultaneously, subject to available payload space, power, weight, and hydrodynamic constraints.

  • CTD instruments for conductivity, temperature, and pressure.
  • Dissolved oxygen sensors.
  • Chlorophyll fluorescence sensors.
  • Optical backscatter and turbidity sensors.
  • Acoustic Doppler Current Profilers (ADCPs), when fitted.
  • Hydrophones for passive acoustic monitoring.
  • Nutrient and other specialized chemical sensors.
04 / Movement Mechanics

The Sawtooth Gliding Pattern

Repeated buoyancy adjustments create alternating descents and ascents through the water column.

PHASE A

Descending Phase

The buoyancy system reduces the vehicle's effective displaced volume. The glider becomes negatively buoyant and begins descending.

Its wings generate lift during this movement, allowing it to travel forward along a sloping path rather than simply falling vertically.

PHASE B

Ascending Phase

The buoyancy system increases the vehicle's displaced volume. The glider becomes positively buoyant and rises toward the surface.

Its wings again generate lift, allowing forward movement during the ascent.

Why Is the Pattern Called Sawtooth?

When depth is plotted against horizontal distance, the repeated descending and ascending paths create a zigzag pattern that resembles the teeth of a saw.

The glider can repeat this cycle many times while collecting measurements at different depths. Mission parameters determine the depth range, pitch, horizontal route, and sampling frequency.

Engineering insight: The glider's forward movement is not produced by buoyancy alone. Buoyancy creates vertical motion; hydrodynamic lift from the wings creates the horizontal component of travel.
05 / Navigation and Communication

Navigation, Positioning and Data Exchange

Operating underwater creates navigation and communication constraints that must be considered when designing a glider mission.

1. GPS and Surface Positioning

GPS signals do not normally penetrate seawater sufficiently for a submerged glider to receive a satellite position fix.

When the vehicle reaches the surface, its GPS receiver can obtain a position estimate. This surface fix helps the operator assess the vehicle's location and correct its navigation plan.

2. Dead Reckoning and Navigation Estimation

Between surface position fixes, the vehicle estimates its movement using its previous position, heading information, estimated speed, and elapsed time.

This approach is known as dead reckoning. Errors can accumulate over time because of sensor uncertainty, water currents, and imperfect estimates of vehicle motion.

Some gliders use additional navigation sensors and estimation methods to improve their position and orientation estimates.

3. Inertial Measurement Units

An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) typically contains accelerometers and gyroscopes. It measures vehicle acceleration and angular motion, helping the control system estimate attitude and movement.

Inertial measurements are useful underwater, but small sensor errors can accumulate when integrated over time. They should not be treated as a perfect position source.

4. Pressure and Depth Sensors

Pressure sensors measure surrounding water pressure. With appropriate calibration and seawater assumptions, pressure measurements can be used to estimate depth.

Depth information helps the vehicle follow mission limits, control its vertical trajectory, and avoid exceeding its rated operating depth.

5. Satellite Communication at the Surface

Many gliders periodically surface to transmit selected information and receive updated instructions using satellite communication.

Depending on the communication system and mission configuration, transmitted information may include position, engineering status, selected scientific measurements, and mission updates.

6. Underwater Communication Limitations

Radio-frequency satellite communication is generally unavailable while the vehicle is submerged. Underwater acoustic communication may be possible when suitable equipment is installed, but it has limited bandwidth, latency, and environmental constraints.

Consequently, many glider missions rely on onboard mission logic and stored data between surface communication sessions.

Key distinction: Surfacing is not merely a recovery step. It can be an important part of the glider's navigation, communication, and mission-management strategy.
06 / Scientific Instrumentation

Oceanographic Sensors and Measurements

Gliders collect measurements that help researchers understand physical, chemical, and biological processes in the ocean.

CTD Sensors

Measure conductivity, temperature, and pressure. These measurements are used to estimate salinity and characterize water masses.

Dissolved Oxygen

Measures oxygen concentration in seawater. This helps investigate water quality, biological activity, and oxygen-depleted regions.

Chlorophyll Fluorometer

Detects fluorescence associated with chlorophyll, helping researchers study phytoplankton distribution.

Optical Backscatter

Measures light scattered by particles in the water and can support estimates of suspended particle concentration.

Acoustic Instruments

Hydrophones and other acoustic instruments can be used to monitor underwater sounds and marine life activity.

Current Profilers

Some platforms carry current-measuring instruments, including ADCP systems, to investigate water movement.

From Raw Measurements to Ocean Knowledge

Sensor measurements are not automatically scientific conclusions. Data processing may require calibration, quality control, time synchronization, depth correction, sensor validation, and comparison with independent observations.

For example, conductivity and temperature measurements combined with pressure help scientists estimate salinity and identify different water masses.

07 / Mission Operations

How a Glider Mission Works

A successful mission requires preparation, deployment, remote supervision, data management, and post-mission maintenance.

01

Mission Planning

Define route, depth limits, sensors, and objectives.

02

Preparation

Check batteries, buoyancy, calibration, and safety.

03

Deployment

Launch the vehicle and verify initial operation.

04

Monitoring

Review position, health, and transmitted data.

05

Recovery

Retrieve the vehicle, download data, and inspect systems.

Mission Planning Considerations

  • Scientific objectives and required measurements.
  • Operating depth and pressure rating.
  • Ocean currents and expected drift.
  • Bathymetry, seabed clearance, and coastal hazards.
  • Battery capacity and expected energy consumption.
  • Communication schedule and satellite coverage.
  • Weather, sea state, shipping traffic, and recovery arrangements.
  • Emergency behavior and loss-of-communication procedures.

Remote Piloting and Human Supervision

Although gliders are autonomous vehicles, their missions are often supervised by human operators or scientific teams.

Operators review vehicle health, transmitted measurements, navigation estimates, and environmental conditions. They may update waypoints, depth limits, or mission parameters when necessary.

Autonomy reduces the need for continuous direct control, but it does not remove the need for mission planning, risk management, and operational oversight.

08 / Practical Applications

Where Underwater Gliders Are Used

Their long endurance and ability to repeatedly sample different depths make gliders valuable for ocean observation and environmental research.

Oceanographic Research

Gliders collect temperature, salinity, pressure, and current-related measurements to study ocean circulation and water-mass structure.

Climate Research

Repeated observations help researchers investigate ocean heat content, changing water properties, and processes relevant to climate variability.

Hurricane Monitoring

Gliders can collect subsurface temperature and salinity information in regions affected by tropical storms, supporting research into ocean conditions associated with storm intensity.

Marine Ecosystem Monitoring

Optical, chemical, and acoustic sensors can help scientists investigate plankton, oxygen conditions, suspended particles, and marine animal activity.

Coastal Observation

Gliders can monitor coastal water properties and help researchers study interactions between currents, temperature, salinity, and environmental conditions.

Operational Ocean Forecasting

Quality-controlled glider observations may be incorporated into ocean models and forecasting systems to improve understanding of subsurface conditions.

09 / Real-World Systems

Examples of Operational Glider Platforms

Existing scientific programs demonstrate how different glider designs support long-duration ocean observation.

CASE STUDY 01

Slocum Gliders — NOAA and Teledyne Webb Research

Slocum gliders are buoyancy-driven underwater platforms used for oceanographic measurements. They can be configured with different scientific instruments and depth capabilities according to the mission.

NOAA's Uncrewed Systems Operations Center announced the acquisition of a Slocum glider named Kelpie in 2024. NOAA described it as capable of scientific missions to 1,000 meters.

This example illustrates how gliders can contribute to sustained environmental observations without requiring a research vessel to remain continuously alongside them.

CASE STUDY 02

Spray Gliders — Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution

Spray gliders are autonomous underwater vehicles used to measure ocean temperature, current velocity, and other properties at different depths.

They follow programmed routes and periodically surface to transmit collected information and receive updated instructions.

Their long deployment periods make them suitable for observing ocean circulation and changes that occur over weeks or months.

CASE STUDY 03

Ocean Observatories Initiative Gliders

The Ocean Observatories Initiative uses gliders as mobile observing platforms. They supplement measurements collected by fixed ocean observing infrastructure.

Their missions demonstrate how autonomous mobile platforms can be integrated into broader ocean monitoring networks.

10 / Technical Challenges

Engineering and Operational Challenges

Energy efficiency does not make underwater gliders immune to mechanical, environmental, navigation, or operational risks.

1. Hydrostatic Pressure

Pressure increases with depth. Hulls, seals, connectors, sensors, and external components must remain within their design pressure limits.

2. Buoyancy System Reliability

Leakage, pump failure, blocked fluid pathways, or incorrect buoyancy calibration can affect the vehicle's ability to control its vertical movement.

3. Navigation Drift

Position estimates may become less accurate between surface GPS fixes. Currents, sensor errors, and dead-reckoning uncertainty can cause the vehicle to deviate from its intended route.

4. Energy Limitations

Although gliders are energy efficient, batteries still supply all electrical systems. Excessive communication, sensor operation, or repeated buoyancy adjustments can reduce mission endurance.

5. Ocean Currents and Environmental Conditions

Strong currents can move a glider away from its intended route. Waves, turbulence, temperature changes, and coastal conditions may complicate navigation and recovery.

6. Communication Interruptions

Satellite communication is generally available only when the glider is at the surface. A failed surfacing event or communication malfunction may delay data transfer and remote intervention.

7. Sensor Accuracy and Calibration

Sensor drift, biofouling, contamination, calibration errors, and data synchronization problems can reduce scientific data quality.

8. Recovery and Physical Damage

Recovery may be affected by poor weather, vessel availability, shipping traffic, inaccurate position estimates, or vehicle damage.

11 / Future Developments

The Future of Underwater Glider Technology

Research is improving the endurance, sensing, navigation, autonomy, and scientific value of underwater glider platforms.

Advanced Autonomous Navigation

Improved navigation estimation and mission-planning algorithms can help gliders respond to changing currents, environmental conditions, and mission constraints.

AI-Assisted Data Analysis

Machine learning may help identify unusual measurements, classify acoustic signals, detect sensor anomalies, and prioritize scientific observations.

Improved Energy Systems

Better battery technologies and more efficient electrical components may extend mission duration and improve operational reliability.

Advanced Scientific Payloads

Smaller and more energy-efficient sensors can expand the range of physical, chemical, and biological measurements collected by gliders.

Coordinated Marine Networks

Gliders may work alongside fixed observatories, research vessels, surface vehicles, satellites, and other underwater platforms.

Adaptive Mission Planning

Future systems may adjust sampling locations or mission priorities in response to detected ocean features, while remaining subject to safety constraints and operator supervision.
