INERTIAL NAVIGATION & POSITIONING

Discover how inertial measurement units, gyroscopes, accelerometers, and navigation algorithms estimate vessel movement, orientation, velocity, and position when satellite positioning is limited or unavailable.

Understanding Inertial Navigation

Inertial Navigation is a positioning approach that uses measurements of acceleration and angular motion to estimate how a vessel moves through space. Unlike satellite navigation, an inertial system does not need to continuously receive external positioning signals. It can continue estimating movement during temporary GNSS outages, making it valuable for ships, submarines, underwater vehicles, and offshore operations. However, its errors accumulate over time, so practical systems often combine inertial measurements with external sensors and positioning references.

01 — FOUNDATIONS

What Is Inertial Navigation?

The principles behind estimating motion without continuously depending on an external position signal.

1.1 The Basic Concept

An Inertial Navigation System (INS) estimates the position, velocity, and orientation of a moving platform by processing measurements from inertial sensors. These measurements describe how the platform accelerates and rotates relative to a reference frame.

The system starts from an initial position, velocity, and orientation. It then updates its motion estimate by continuously processing new sensor measurements. This process is commonly called dead reckoning when position is advanced using estimated movement.

Core Engineering Principle

An inertial system does not directly measure geographic position. It measures motion and calculates position by integrating those measurements over time.

Why Is It Important in Maritime Operations?

  • Maintaining short-term navigation estimates during GNSS signal loss.
  • Supporting underwater vehicles that cannot receive ordinary satellite signals while submerged.
  • Providing vessel attitude information such as roll, pitch, and heading-related motion.
  • Supporting offshore survey, subsea inspection, and dynamic positioning applications.
  • Providing motion data to navigation, stabilization, and control systems.

1.2 Position, Velocity, and Attitude

An INS commonly estimates several related navigation quantities. These values are connected, but they describe different aspects of the vessel's movement.

Position

Describes where the vessel is located. It may be represented using latitude, longitude, and height or depth-related reference information.

Velocity

Describes the rate and direction of movement. It can be expressed as north, east, and down velocity components.

Attitude

Describes the orientation of the vessel, commonly represented by roll, pitch, and yaw or heading.

Angular Rate

Describes how quickly the vessel rotates around its body axes. It is measured using gyroscopes.

Accurate navigation requires these quantities to be interpreted together. For example, acceleration measured along the vessel's forward axis must be transformed into an appropriate navigation reference frame before it can be used to update geographic velocity and position.

02 — SENSOR TECHNOLOGY

IMU Components and Measurements

Understanding the sensors that provide the raw measurements used by inertial navigation algorithms.

2.1 Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)

An Inertial Measurement Unit is a sensor assembly that measures the movement and rotation of a platform. A typical IMU contains three-axis accelerometers and three-axis gyroscopes. Some units also include magnetometers or additional environmental sensors.

Accelerometers

Accelerometers measure specific force along their sensitive axes. They detect changes in motion and the effect of gravity as experienced by the sensor. Their measurements are essential for estimating velocity and position.

Gyroscopes

Gyroscopes measure angular rate: how quickly the platform rotates around its axes. The navigation algorithm uses these measurements to update the estimated orientation of the vessel.

Magnetometers

Magnetometers measure the surrounding magnetic field. They may provide heading-related information in suitable conditions, but magnetic disturbances from steel structures, electrical equipment, and nearby magnetic sources can affect their readings.

2.2 IMU vs INS

These terms are related but are not interchangeable. An IMU is primarily a measurement unit, while an INS includes the processing and navigation algorithms required to calculate navigation states.

Aspect IMU INS
Main role Measures acceleration and angular rate. Estimates position, velocity, and orientation.
Typical components Accelerometers and gyroscopes. IMU, processor, navigation software, and initialization data.
Output Raw or calibrated sensor measurements. Navigation estimates and system status.
External aiding Not necessarily included. May integrate GNSS, DVL, odometry, or other references.
Important Distinction

An IMU alone does not automatically provide a reliable geographic position. Navigation software must process its measurements, apply calibration and alignment information, and account for errors.

2.3 Types of Inertial Sensors

Inertial sensors are available in different technologies and performance classes. Their accuracy, size, cost, power consumption, and long-term stability vary significantly.

  • MEMS sensors: Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems are compact, relatively low-cost sensors commonly used in consumer, industrial, and navigation applications.
  • Fiber Optic Gyroscopes (FOG): Use optical interference principles to measure rotation and are used in many higher-performance navigation systems.
  • Ring Laser Gyroscopes (RLG): Use counter-propagating laser beams to measure angular rotation and are used in demanding navigation applications.
  • Mechanical gyroscopes: Use a rotating mechanical element and are associated with traditional inertial navigation designs.

No sensor technology is automatically best for every vessel. Selection depends on the required accuracy, operating duration, environmental conditions, installation constraints, and available budget.

03 — NAVIGATION PROCESSING

How an INS Calculates Movement

From raw measurements to an estimated navigation solution.

3.1 The Navigation Calculation Process

An inertial navigation algorithm processes measurements in a sequence. The exact implementation depends on the system architecture, sensor grade, reference frame, and navigation application.

  1. Initialization: The system receives initial position, velocity, and attitude information. The quality of these initial values affects the navigation solution.
  2. Sensor measurement: Accelerometers and gyroscopes provide new measurements at regular intervals.
  3. Calibration and correction: The system compensates for known sensor biases, scale-factor errors, misalignment, and other modeled effects.
  4. Attitude update: Angular-rate measurements are processed to update the estimated orientation of the vessel.
  5. Acceleration transformation: Measured specific force is transformed from the sensor or body frame into a navigation frame.
  6. Velocity update: Corrected acceleration is integrated over time to estimate changes in velocity.
  7. Position update: Estimated velocity is integrated to update the vessel's position.
  8. Quality monitoring: The system evaluates uncertainty and compares available measurements from external references.

3.2 Mathematical Principles

In simplified form, acceleration can be integrated to estimate velocity, and velocity can be integrated to estimate displacement.

Velocity = Initial Velocity + Integrated Acceleration
Displacement = Initial Position + Integrated Velocity

These expressions describe the basic idea, not the complete equations used in a real navigation system. Practical INS algorithms must account for orientation, gravity, Earth's rotation, coordinate transformations, and the movement of the Earth-fixed reference frame.

Reference Frames

  • Body frame: Axes fixed to the vessel or sensor unit.
  • Navigation frame: A local reference frame, often North-East-Down or East-North-Up.
  • Earth-centered reference frame: A global coordinate frame used in some navigation calculations.

Correct transformation between these frames is essential. A small orientation error can cause acceleration to be assigned to the wrong direction, which may produce significant position errors over time.

04 — ERROR AND ACCURACY

Inertial Drift and Navigation Errors

Why an inertial system can continue operating while its estimated position gradually becomes less accurate.

4.1 Understanding Inertial Drift

Inertial navigation relies on repeated integration of sensor measurements. Even small measurement errors can accumulate as the system continues calculating velocity and position.

For example, a small accelerometer bias may cause an increasing velocity error. As that velocity error is integrated again, the estimated position may diverge from the vessel's actual position.

Common Sources of Error

  • Bias: A persistent offset in sensor measurements.
  • Noise: Random variations in sensor readings.
  • Scale-factor error: A difference between the true input and the measured response.
  • Misalignment: Incorrect orientation between sensor axes and the intended reference frame.
  • Temperature effects: Changes in sensor behavior as operating temperature changes.
  • Vibration: Mechanical disturbance that can affect measurements and installation stability.
  • Initial alignment error: Incorrect initial orientation or motion conditions.

4.2 Error Growth and Operating Time

The rate at which an INS loses accuracy depends on sensor quality, calibration, navigation algorithm, initial conditions, and the availability of external aiding sources.

A higher-grade inertial system may maintain useful accuracy for longer periods without external updates. A lower-cost system may experience faster drift and require more frequent corrections.

Engineering Insight

"GNSS-denied operation" does not mean that position remains perfectly accurate. It means the system can continue estimating movement without receiving a new satellite position fix. Its uncertainty normally increases with time.

Ways to Reduce Navigation Errors

  • Use appropriate sensor calibration and alignment.
  • Select inertial sensors suitable for the required mission.
  • Integrate independent external measurements.
  • Monitor estimated uncertainty and sensor health.
  • Use redundant references where operationally appropriate.
  • Inspect installation, mounting, vibration, and temperature conditions.
05 — SENSOR FUSION

GNSS/INS Integration and Sensor Fusion

Combining complementary navigation sources to improve continuity and reliability.

5.1 Why Combine GNSS and INS?

GNSS provides externally referenced position and velocity information, but its signals can be interrupted, obstructed, jammed, or spoofed. An INS can continue estimating short-term movement during those interruptions, but its errors accumulate.

Combining the two systems allows each source to compensate for some of the other's weaknesses. GNSS can help correct inertial drift, while inertial measurements can provide continuous short-term motion estimates between GNSS updates.

GNSS Contribution

Provides an externally referenced position and velocity estimate when satellite measurements are available and trustworthy.

INS Contribution

Provides high-rate motion and orientation estimates and supports continuity during temporary GNSS interruptions.

5.2 Sensor Fusion Algorithms

Sensor fusion combines information from multiple sources to produce a navigation estimate that considers the strengths and uncertainties of the available measurements.

Kalman Filtering

A Kalman filter is a mathematical estimation method used in many navigation systems. It predicts the next navigation state using a system model, then updates that prediction when new measurements become available.

The filter also estimates uncertainty. This allows the system to consider how much confidence should be placed in the predicted state and in incoming measurements.

Common Integration Architectures

  • Loosely coupled integration: GNSS position or velocity solutions are supplied to the navigation filter as measurements.
  • Tightly coupled integration: The navigation filter processes GNSS observations at a lower measurement level, such as satellite pseudorange and Doppler measurements, alongside inertial data.
  • Deep integration: GNSS receiver tracking and inertial information are more closely integrated to support signal tracking and navigation under challenging conditions.

The appropriate architecture depends on receiver design, mission requirements, processing capability, and the expected operating environment.

06 — MARITIME APPLICATIONS

Inertial Navigation in the Maritime Environment

How inertial systems support surface vessels, underwater vehicles, and offshore operations.

6.1 Surface Vessel Navigation

On surface vessels, inertial systems can support navigation continuity, vessel motion measurement, and the integration of navigation sensors.

  • Supporting position estimation during temporary GNSS outages.
  • Providing roll, pitch, and heading-related information.
  • Supporting stabilization and motion compensation systems.
  • Assisting navigation systems that combine multiple sensor sources.
  • Providing motion information for offshore survey equipment.

The INS output must still be checked against other available navigation references. It should not be treated as an unquestionable source of truth.

6.2 Underwater Vehicles and Subsea Operations

Submerged vehicles cannot normally receive ordinary GNSS signals directly through seawater. They therefore use alternative methods to estimate their position and movement.

Doppler Velocity Log (DVL)

A DVL measures the velocity of a vehicle relative to the seabed or surrounding water using acoustic Doppler measurements. When bottom lock is available, it can provide useful velocity information relative to the seabed.

Water-track measurements estimate velocity relative to the surrounding water instead. This is not necessarily the same as velocity over the seabed, particularly when currents are present.

Acoustic Positioning

Underwater acoustic positioning systems use sound signals exchanged between transponders, seabed references, or support vessels to estimate the position of submerged platforms.

Other Supporting Technologies

  • Pressure sensors and depth measurement systems.
  • Acoustic navigation and ranging systems.
  • USBL and LBL positioning systems.
  • Terrain-relative navigation in suitable environments.
  • GNSS updates when the vehicle surfaces or uses an appropriate external antenna arrangement.

6.3 INS and DVL Integration

An INS and DVL can complement each other in underwater navigation. The INS estimates motion using inertial measurements, while the DVL supplies velocity observations that can help limit inertial drift.

When DVL bottom lock is lost because of altitude, seabed characteristics, water conditions, or operational geometry, the navigation system may continue using inertial estimates. However, position uncertainty can increase until useful external measurements become available again.

Do Not Confuse the Measurements

DVL velocity relative to the seabed and DVL velocity relative to the water are different measurements. Current velocity can make the distinction operationally important.

07 — ENGINEERING CHALLENGES

Technical Challenges and System Reliability

The practical limitations that engineers must consider when designing and operating inertial navigation systems.

7.1 Environmental and Installation Challenges

  • Vessel vibration: Engines, propellers, and machinery can introduce vibration that affects sensor measurements.
  • Temperature variation: Changes in temperature may affect sensor bias and scale-factor stability.
  • Mechanical alignment: Incorrect mounting orientation can create systematic navigation errors.
  • Magnetic interference: Nearby electrical and ferromagnetic equipment can affect magnetometer-based measurements.
  • Acoustic conditions: DVL and acoustic positioning performance can be affected by seabed properties, water conditions, and geometry.

7.2 GNSS-Denied and Degraded Environments

GNSS-denied operation can occur when satellite signals are obstructed, unavailable, or deliberately disrupted. Inertial navigation can support continuity, but the system must monitor how uncertainty grows.

Engineers may combine inertial measurements with radar observations, DVL, acoustic references, visual navigation, or other independent sources where suitable.

The choice of aiding source depends on the vessel, environment, available equipment, and the required navigation accuracy.

7.3 Data Integrity and Cybersecurity

An integrated navigation solution depends on the quality and trustworthiness of its input data. A sensor can produce plausible-looking measurements even when it is malfunctioning or compromised.

  • Monitor sensor status, validity, and measurement quality.
  • Compare independent data sources when available.
  • Detect unexpected changes and inconsistent measurements.
  • Protect navigation interfaces and configuration access.
  • Maintain secure software and equipment update procedures.
  • Provide clear alarms when integrity cannot be verified.
  • Ensure operators understand fallback modes and system limitations.

Redundant sensors do not guarantee reliability if they share the same failure mode, power source, data network, or incorrect reference information. Independence and integrity monitoring matter.

08 — PRACTICAL ENGINEERING

Operational Scenarios and Applications

Applying inertial navigation principles to real maritime situations.

8.1 Scenario: Temporary GNSS Signal Loss

A vessel is operating near a location where satellite signals become temporarily unavailable.

  1. The GNSS receiver reports loss or degradation of its position solution.
  2. The INS continues processing accelerometer and gyroscope measurements.
  3. The navigation system estimates vessel movement using its current state.
  4. The system monitors uncertainty and indicates that external positioning is unavailable.
  5. When trustworthy GNSS measurements return, the integrated system can use them to correct its estimate.

The operator should verify the navigation solution using other available references and follow the vessel's approved procedures.

8.2 Scenario: Underwater Survey Vehicle

An autonomous underwater vehicle is conducting a subsea inspection mission.

  1. The IMU measures the vehicle's acceleration and angular motion.
  2. The INS calculates orientation, velocity, and position estimates.
  3. The DVL provides velocity measurements when suitable acoustic returns are available.
  4. Depth sensors provide additional vertical reference information.
  5. Acoustic positioning updates may correct the estimated position when available.
  6. The navigation system monitors data quality and uncertainty throughout the mission.

This architecture provides a more robust navigation solution than relying on inertial measurements alone, although its actual accuracy depends on the quality and availability of each sensor.

09 — FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

The Future of Inertial Maritime Navigation

New sensor technologies and algorithms are improving navigation resilience and system integration.

9.1 Emerging Technologies

  • Advanced MEMS: Improvements in sensor design, calibration, and processing may support smaller and more capable inertial systems.
  • Fiber optic and advanced optical sensors: Continued development supports high-performance navigation applications.
  • Multi-sensor fusion: Combining inertial, acoustic, satellite, radar, and velocity measurements can improve navigation continuity.
  • Machine learning: Data-driven methods may assist anomaly detection, sensor monitoring, and environmental estimation.
  • Resilient PNT: Navigation architectures increasingly consider multiple positioning, navigation, and timing sources rather than relying on GNSS alone.

9.2 Intelligent Navigation and Autonomy

Future autonomous and remotely operated vessels will depend on reliable estimates of position, velocity, and orientation. Inertial systems can contribute to this capability by providing continuous motion information.

However, autonomy requires more than an accurate position estimate. A complete navigation architecture must also consider obstacle detection, route planning, environmental awareness, collision avoidance, communications, system integrity, and human oversight.

Final Engineering Perspective

Inertial navigation is not a replacement for every other navigation technology. Its greatest value comes from providing continuous motion estimates as part of a carefully designed, monitored, and integrated navigation architecture.
